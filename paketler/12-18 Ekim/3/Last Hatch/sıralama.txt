1. At nine forty on a September night, the sky turns red.
2. Not a sunset red. A slow, pulsing red that ripples across the stars like a curtain.
3. Scout presses against your leg and will not stop whining.
4. Then the porch light buzzes, flickers, and goes out.
5. Down the road, a transformer on a pole cracks like thunder and goes dark.
6. Your phone still has a full battery. But there is no signal. And it never comes back.
7. This is a solar storm. And you are about to live one full year without power.
8. So tell me. How long would you last?
9. Day one, you wake at dawn to total silence. No fridge hum, no well pump, no traffic on the far highway.
10. You live alone on a small farm in rural Missouri, twelve miles from the nearest town. Just you and Scout.
11. You crank the handle of an old emergency radio from the kitchen drawer. Mostly static. Then a voice.
12. A volunteer station says the storm hit the whole planet overnight. The grid is down across much of the country.
13. You think it must be a freak event. But storms like this have happened before.
14. In eighteen fifty-nine, the Carrington storm made telegraph lines throw sparks and shocked operators at their desks.
15. That night, the northern lights were seen as far south as Cuba and Hawaii.
16. In nineteen eighty-nine, a much smaller storm blacked out six million people in Quebec in about ninety seconds.
17. And in May twenty twenty-four, the strongest storm in about twenty years painted auroras as far south as Alabama.
18. Back in eighteen fifty-nine, the world barely ran on wires. Now, almost everything does.
19. Here is the good news. Radiation from a solar flare cannot pass through the atmosphere to harm people on the ground.
20. What it hurts is the machines. The power lines, the satellites, and the signals that guide every phone and car.
21. You open the fridge for just a second. Closed, it keeps food cold for about four hours.
22. A full freezer holds for about two days. So the first meals are easy. You eat the meat first, and so does Scout.
23. Then you think about water, and your stomach drops.
24. Your water comes from a well, and the well pump runs on electricity.
25. In the basement you count blue jugs. Thirty gallons of stored water, an old habit from your grandfather.
26. Emergency planners say one gallon per person, per day, for drinking and basic hygiene.
27. Scout needs his own water too, on top of yours. Pets belong in the plan, not as an afterthought.
28. Thirty gallons sounds like a lot. Split between the two of you, it will not even last a month.
29. Week one, the cars on the road thin out, then stop. Gas stations cannot pump fuel without power.
30. The radio voice comes back each evening at seven. It is the only schedule you have left.
31. On the fifth night, it says something that makes you sit down on the kitchen floor.
32. The storm burned out huge transformers across the grid. Not the small ones. The giant ones that move power between states.
33. Each one is built to order. Even in normal times, the wait for a new one can stretch past twenty months.
34. You look at Scout and say it out loud. This is not a week, buddy. This is a year.
35. You grab a black marker and draw a calendar on the cellar wall. Day one gets a thick black cross.
36. Day eleven, you drag the small gas generator out of the shed. Eight gallons of fuel. That is all.
37. It is pouring rain, and the garage would be so much easier. You stop in the doorway.
38. Then you remember. Generators go outdoors, at least twenty feet from windows and doors. Never in a garage, even with the door open.
39. Carbon monoxide has no color and no smell. It gives no warning at all.
40. So you run it in the rain, far from the house, under a little roof of old tin.
41. Two hours a day. Just enough for the radio battery, a lamp, and a few minutes of the well pump.
42. You fill every jug, every pot, and the bathtub. Water is time.
43. Day twenty-six, the generator coughs twice and dies. The last of the fuel is gone.
44. You stand in the yard while the silence settles back over the farm.
45. There is a creek half a mile through the woods. The water looks clean. That means nothing.
46. You carry it home in buckets, and you boil every single drop.
47. A rolling boil for one full minute kills the germs that make you sick. Above six thousand five hundred feet, boil it for three.
48. But boiling cannot remove chemicals. So you only take water upstream, far above the old farm road.
49. Every trip, Scout runs ahead and checks the path. Every trip, he brings home a new stick.
50. Day forty, you pull every can off the shelves and sort them on the kitchen table.
51. Tomatoes, fruit, and pickles go to the front. High acid foods keep their best quality for about twelve to eighteen months.
52. Beans, soups, and canned meat go to the back. Low acid foods can keep for two to five years.
53. You count three hundred and ten cans. One a day, plus the garden, plus whatever you can find.
54. It is enough. Barely. If nothing goes wrong.
55. Month two, the garden becomes your new job. Squash, potatoes, and the last tomatoes of the season.
56. You line the root cellar with straw. Potatoes keep best in the cold and the dark.
57. In the evenings, you read old paperbacks by candlelight. Scout falls asleep with his head on your boot.
58. It is quiet. Too quiet. Some nights, that is the hardest part.
59. Day eighty-four, Scout wakes you at three in the morning with a low growl you have never heard before.
60. Gravel crunches outside. Footsteps. More than one person.
61. You do not turn on a light. You open the cellar hatch and slip down the ladder with Scout.
62. You close the hatch above your head. Flashlight off. One hand gently on Scout's back.
63. Above you, the door handle rattles. Then the barn door. Then voices, low and tired.
64. Your heart is so loud you are sure they can hear it through the floor.
65. After an hour, the footsteps fade toward the road. They took two buckets and a bag of chicken feed.
66. Not everyone out there is dangerous. But that night you learn something. You are not as alone as you thought.
67. From then on, the cellar is not just storage. It is your safe room.
68. Month four, the first frost silvers the fields. The days get short, and the nights get long.
69. The wood stove becomes the heart of the house. You split wood every morning until your hands ache.
70. Above it hangs a small battery powered carbon monoxide alarm. You test it every Sunday.
71. Day one hundred and fifty, the first real blizzard hits. Snow buries the porch steps in a single night.
72. The creek is frozen solid now. You melt snow in a pot on the stove, then boil it anyway.
73. One grey afternoon, you slip on the ice by the creek and go through up to your waist.
74. The walk home takes forever. Your fingers stop working on the zipper of your coat.
75. You feel slow and sleepy, and you cannot remember which way the house is.
76. Scout barks and pulls at your sleeve until you follow him home.
77. Shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, slurred speech, drowsiness. These are warning signs of hypothermia.
78. You had almost all of them. Out here, there is no doctor to call. That scares you more than the storm ever did.
79. That night, you write a new rule on the cellar wall. Never go to the creek alone in winter.
80. Day two hundred and ten, Scout stops eating.
81. He lies by the stove all day, and his tail barely moves when you say his name.
82. You sit up with him all night, giving him small sips of warm water.
83. You realize you have not spoken to another person in seven months. Only to him.
84. On the third morning, he gets up, stretches, and pushes his empty bowl toward you with his nose.
85. You laugh so hard you cry. It is the best day of the whole year.
86. By spring, the snow melts, and the creek runs loud and brown again.
87. The radio voice is weaker now, but it brings news. Crews are rebuilding the grid, one region at a time.
88. Cities first. Hospitals and water plants first. Farms like yours are near the end of the list.
89. You plant the spring garden with seeds you saved from last year.
90. Day two hundred and ninety, you count the cans again. Forty-one left.
91. The garden will not be ready for weeks. The math does not work.
92. The radio says there is a relief station in town. Twelve miles away. Food, water, and medicine.
93. But going means leaving the farm empty for two days. Everything you have would be left unguarded.
94. Staying means half a can a day, and hoping the beans grow fast.
95. You sit on the porch with Scout until the sun goes down. Then you decide.
96. You take one long breath, and you decide to go.
97. At sunrise, you pack the backpack. Water, the radio, dog food, a small first aid kit, and a map.
98. You lock the cellar hatch and leave a note on the front door. Gone to town. Back soon.
99. The highway is a long line of silent cars, covered in a year of dust and pollen.
100. Scout walks close to your knee the whole way. He knows this is not a normal walk.
101. Near the town limits, you hear an engine. A real engine. The first one in nine months.
102. A military truck rolls past, and a soldier raises a hand to you. You almost forget how to wave back.
103. The relief station is a high school gym full of cots, tables, and tired faces.
104. They give you rice, canned food, and a big bag of dog food. Scout gets a pat from every volunteer.
105. You sleep one night on a cot, surrounded by people. It feels strange to hear so many voices.
106. You walk home the next day with a heavy pack and a lighter heart. The farm is just as you left it.
107. Day three hundred and forty, you hear a sound you have not heard in almost a year.
108. A utility truck, far down the road, with a crew stringing new wire between the poles.
109. Every day they come a little closer. Scout runs to the fence to watch them each morning.
110. Day three hundred and sixty-five, you climb down into the cellar one last time and draw the final cross on the wall.
111. Three hundred and sixty-five black marks. One year.
112. You push open the hatch and climb out into the warm evening air.
113. And the porch light, dead for a whole year, flickers once, and comes on.
114. You stand in the yard and laugh, and Scout barks at the light like it is an old friend.
115. Inside, the fridge starts to hum. The well pump clicks. The whole house is breathing again.
116. But you do not unpack the cellar. Not one can. Not one jug.
117. Because this year taught you something. Power is not a promise. It is a gift.
118. That night, far to the south, above the trees, the sky begins to glow red again.
119. Would you have walked to town, or stayed with the farm? Tell me in the comments.
120. And if you think you could survive the next disaster, the next hatch is already waiting for you.

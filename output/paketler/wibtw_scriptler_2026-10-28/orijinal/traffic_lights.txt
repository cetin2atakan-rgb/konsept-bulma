## TITLE: Why Are Traffic Lights Always Red on Top?

[1. HOOK]

Every single day, you stop at a red light without looking at where the red light actually is. It is always on top. Not sometimes. Not in some towns and not in others. On a pole, on a [LABEL: mast arm], hanging over an intersection in a city you have never visited, the order never changes: red above, then amber, then green at the bottom. Turn the signal sideways, onto its back, mounted horizontally above a low bridge or a tight downtown block, and the rule still holds, just rotated onto an axis, with red almost always kept on the same side relative to the driver. Millions of drivers pass through this pattern every hour of every day and never once wonder why a committee, decades ago, decided that the position of a colored light mattered as much as the color itself.

[2. THE QUESTION]

So here is the actual question. Why does red always sit on top, or on a fixed side, instead of the three colors just being arranged however a city engineer felt like arranging them that week.

You might think the answer is tradition, or that red simply looks more urgent sitting higher up where drivers see it first as they approach from a distance. That is the easy, intuitive answer, the one most drivers would give without a second thought, and it is wrong, or at least badly incomplete. Height has very little to do with urgency and almost everything to do with a problem that has nothing to do with seeing color at all: the problem of not being able to see color correctly in the first place. The popular story says engineers picked red for the top position because red is the most important color and deserves pride of place, the way a general's name goes at the top of a letter. The records show something far more specific and far more interesting, tied directly to human eyesight itself, not to symbolism or rank.

For most of the first two decades that electric traffic signals existed, nobody had agreed on an order at all, and that is exactly the gap that let the real reason sneak in almost unnoticed. To understand why position eventually became a fixed rule rather than a local habit, we have to go back to a handful of competing, uncoordinated cities in the early nineteen hundreds, each one solving the same traffic problem in its own way, years before anyone was thinking about [LABEL: color vision deficiency] as an engineering problem that could be solved with geometry instead of paint.

[3. HISTORY]

The earliest American attempts at automatic traffic control were not electric and were not consistent with each other in any way. Manually operated [LABEL: semaphore] arms and hand-cranked signs existed in several cities before 1910, usually worked by a traffic officer standing on a raised platform in the middle of an intersection, swinging a sign between a stop position and a go position. None of these devices needed a color rule, because a human was reading the sign up close and interpreting it directly. The detail that matters for this story is what happened once the human officer was replaced by an electric lamp that strangers, driving at speed, had to interpret correctly from a distance and in a hurry. That story usually starts in Salt Lake City, Utah, in 1912, with a police officer named Lester Wire.

According to accounts collected by the Utah State Historical Society, Wire built a wooden box, roughly the shape of a birdhouse, fitted with red and green lights and wired into the overhead trolley and power lines running through downtown Salt Lake City. He is widely credited, at least locally, as the inventor of the first electric traffic signal. The popular story treats this as settled fact. The records show something messier: Wire never patented his device, no original box survived past the 1960s, and several historians point instead to a 1914 installation in Cleveland, Ohio, based on a patented design by James Hoge, as the better-documented claim to the first electric traffic signal in American use. So the honest version of this story is that Wire's 1912 device is a real, early, and likely contender, but the title of absolute first is genuinely disputed among historians, not just a matter of local pride.

According to accounts collected and promoted by the Utah State Historical Society and the Utah Department of Transportation, Wire built a wooden signal box, roughly the size and shape of a birdhouse, fitted with red and green lamps and wired directly into the overhead [LABEL: trolley line] and electrical lines running through downtown Salt Lake City. The box reportedly went up at the intersection of Two Hundred South and Main Street, and for years afterward Utah institutions have described it as the world's first electric traffic signal. The popular story treats this as settled fact, a clean, tidy origin point for the whole technology. The records show something messier. Wire never patented his device. By the 1960s, both of his original boxes had reportedly been lost entirely, leaving nothing physical to examine. And several historians instead point to a 1914 installation in Cleveland, Ohio, built on a [LABEL: patent] design credited to an inventor named James Hoge, as the better-documented claim to the first electric traffic signal actually placed into American use, specifically because Hoge's system was patented and because Cleveland's installation involved mains electrical wiring rather than a shared trolley line. So the honest version of this particular origin story is that Wire's 1912 device in Salt Lake City is a real, early, and plausible contender, but the title of absolute first electric traffic signal in the United States remains genuinely disputed among historians, not merely a matter of competing civic pride between two cities.

What both of these very early systems shared, though, was pure simplicity: red and green, nothing more, switching directly back and forth with no interval of any kind between stop and go. That design left drivers almost no time to react before cross traffic started moving, which in practice meant intersections controlled by these early signals were still dangerous, just dangerous in a new, electrically automated way instead of the old, chaotic way.

The next major jump came from Detroit, Michigan, in 1920, through a police officer named William Potts. Potts is generally credited with building the first four-way, three-color traffic signal, taking the existing red-and-green idea and adding a third [LABEL: amber] caution light between them, specifically so drivers would get a brief warning period before the change rather than an instant switch. Accounts differ slightly on the exact corner where it first went up, with some citing the intersection of Woodward Avenue and Michigan Avenue and others citing Woodward and Fort Street, a small discrepancy worth flagging rather than resolving with false confidence. But the core claim holds steady across independent sources: Potts installed his design in October of 1920, and within roughly a year Detroit had automatic four-way, three-color signals running from fifteen separate towers scattered across the city. A surviving example of an early Potts-style signal is reportedly held today at The Henry Ford museum in Dearborn, Michigan, though that detail comes from a single local news account and deserves independent confirmation before being treated as fully settled.

Three years later, in 1923, a Cleveland-based inventor and businessman named Garrett Augustus Morgan was awarded a United States patent for a different three-position signal, developed independently of Potts's work in Detroit. Morgan's design used a tall, T-shaped arm rather than a simple lamp cluster, and its warning position could also be set to stop traffic entirely in every direction at once, providing full [LABEL: pedestrian clearance] so people could cross without competing against turning vehicles. According to multiple historical accounts, Morgan later sold rights connected to his design to the General Electric Company for a reported forty thousand dollars, a substantial sum for the period. Patent reference numbers for Morgan's design appear inconsistently across different sources, so the exact filing date and patent number are best treated as approximate rather than precisely verified here.

By the middle of the 1920s, then, American cities had at least three real, separate inventors working in parallel, three overlapping and sometimes competing claims to the underlying idea, and absolutely no agreement whatsoever on how a three-color signal should physically look once built. One city's signal might read red, amber, green from top to bottom on the lamp mounted for the main street, with the order reversed for the cross street. A neighboring town might flip the whole arrangement for reasons nobody wrote down. A horizontally mounted signal in one place might put red on the left side as the driver faced it, and in the very next town over, red might sit on the right instead. Drivers crossing a state line, or sometimes just a city line, were effectively relearning a slightly different signal every time, at the exact moment they most needed instant, unthinking recognition rather than a pause to work out what the pattern in front of them actually meant.

This genuine chaos is what pushed the American Association of State Highway Officials and the National Conference on Street and Highway Safety to form a [LABEL: joint committee] on uniform traffic control devices in 1932, bringing state highway engineers and traffic safety officials together around one table for the first time to argue the question out. That joint effort produced the first [LABEL: MUTCD] Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices, generally dated to 1935, which existed at first only as a mimeographed document, then as a more widely printed edition two years later once demand for copies outpaced what the original limited run could supply. The manual did two things that matter enormously for this particular story. First, it fixed the three-color signal, red, amber, green, as the accepted national standard [LABEL: lens configuration], finally ending the era of two-color-only signals in most jurisdictions. Second, and far more quietly, it fixed the physical position of each color within that signal, not as a stylistic suggestion for engineers to take or leave, but as a rule every jurisdiction adopting the manual was expected to follow consistently, intersection after intersection, state after state.

Separately, and running across roughly the same decades, a related legal framework called the [LABEL: Uniform Vehicle Code] moved through state legislatures one at a time, offering states model statutory language they could adopt wholesale so that the actual rules of the road, including what a given signal meant and where its colors were required to sit, matched reliably from one state line to the next. Between the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices setting the physical engineering standard and the Uniform Vehicle Code supplying matching legal language for states to adopt, the position of red, amber, and green stopped being a local engineer's personal preference and became something much closer to a nationwide contract between the road itself and every driver who used it, a contract almost nobody driving today has ever consciously read.

[4. THE REAL REASON]

Here is the part almost nobody learns in school, and it has very little to do with making the signal look more serious, more official, or more visually dominant from a distance. The position of each color is standardized because of a biological condition that affects a meaningful share of the driving population, one that most committee members in 1935 would have understood far better than the average driver does today: red-green [LABEL: color blindness].

Most modern estimates put red-green color vision deficiency at somewhere around eight percent of men worldwide, which works out to roughly one driver in every twelve behind the wheel, with somewhat lower figures reported in some populations and somewhat higher ones in others. Women are affected far less often, with figures closer to one in two hundred, because the genes most commonly responsible for this kind of color vision deficiency sit on the [LABEL: X chromosome], and men carry only a single copy of that chromosome, with no second copy available to compensate for a faulty version. Red-green deficiency is overwhelmingly the dominant form of color blindness, accounting for the vast majority of all diagnosed cases, which is exactly why it is the form traffic engineers had to design around first. For a driver with this condition, a red light and a green light, viewed only as two glowing dots of color on a dark pole against a dark sky, can be genuinely difficult to separate by hue alone, and that difficulty gets measurably worse in poor light, in heavy rain, in direct sun glare, or at the exact moment a driver is also trying to judge distance, speed, and the behavior of three other cars at once.

This is where geometry quietly does the work that color alone cannot reliably do. If red is always the top lamp in a vertical [LABEL: signal head], and always kept on a fixed, predictable side in a [LABEL: horizontal signal], then a driver does not strictly need to correctly distinguish red from green by hue at all in order to drive safely. He can read the signal by position instead of by color: top lamp lit means stop, regardless of exactly what shade that lamp happens to render through a colorblind eye, and bottom lamp lit means go, following the same logic in reverse. The engineering insight tucked inside a document that sounds as dry as a manual is this: when you cannot guarantee that every driver on the road will correctly sort two colors from each other, you give every driver, colorblind or not, a second, completely independent channel of information, physical position, that keeps working even in the exact moment the color channel quietly fails them.

This is also, in a fairly direct sense, one of the earliest widespread, real-world examples of what modern safety engineers now describe as [LABEL: redundant signaling], the deliberate practice of encoding one piece of critical information through two separate channels at once, so that a failure in one channel does not automatically become a failure of the entire system a person depends on. The Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices and the state vehicle codes that followed its lead were not simply tidying up a messy patchwork of competing city designs for the sake of bureaucratic neatness or visual consistency. They were closing a real, measurable gap in road safety that a meaningful percentage of drivers on American roads faced every single time they approached an intersection. That is the real reason red sits on top, and the real reason it stays on the same fixed side in a horizontal arrangement. It is a quiet, almost invisible accommodation, built directly into the physical hardware of the intersection itself, for an eye condition that most of the drivers benefiting from it every day have never been formally diagnosed with and likely never consciously think about at all. It costs the city nothing extra to install, it requires no special lamp, no warning sticker, and no driver education beyond what every learner already absorbs before a first license, and that is precisely what makes it such an elegant, almost invisible piece of engineering: the safety feature lives entirely in where the hardware is bolted, not in anything a driver has to remember, read, or be taught separately.

[5. WHAT IF]

Now run the counterfactual. Imagine the 1935 manual had never fixed signal position as a rule, and every state, or every city, had simply kept inventing its own layout, the way things actually looked in the chaotic years just after Potts and Morgan filed their competing designs.

A colorblind driver moving between two states, or even two neighboring towns with different local ordinances, would face two completely different spatial codes for the same meaning. Top-lit stop in one town could become bottom-lit stop in the next. For a driver who already cannot fully trust the color itself, losing the positional shortcut too would mean losing both independent channels of information at once, right at an intersection, at speed, surrounded by other vehicles making the same split-second decisions. It is not hard to imagine the crash data from that version of history looking considerably worse than the one we actually got.

But the counterfactual has a real-world mirror worth sitting with carefully, because the position rule is not actually universal everywhere on earth in quite the clean, absolute way the American vertical rule might suggest. Horizontally mounted traffic signals exist in parts of the United States and in several other countries, usually installed wherever vertical space above the roadway is genuinely limited, under a low railroad bridge, for example, or along a historic downtown block where overhead clearance was never designed with a tall signal mast in mind. And in those horizontal arrangements, the side carrying the red lamp is not fixed worldwide the same confident way the top position is fixed inside a vertical American signal. Observers and enthusiasts who study road design closely have noted a rough, recurring pattern tied to which side of the road a given country drives on, with red commonly kept toward the side nearer the oncoming lane of traffic, though this pattern reads more like an informal convention repeatedly noticed by careful observers than a single, enforced, worldwide treaty provision everyone signed onto at once. Within the United States specifically, accessibility-related consistency requirements have pushed horizontal signal layouts toward one single fixed arrangement nationwide, and reports from road enthusiasts describe rare, accidentally reversed installations being corrected within days of being flagged, which is itself a small but telling piece of evidence for just how seriously agencies now take positional consistency, decades after the original 1935 manual first made the rule explicit on paper.

So the counterfactual cuts both ways. Fixing position almost certainly prevented real confusion and real crashes for colorblind drivers. But fixing it only within one country's borders, rather than through one single worldwide standard, means the exact spatial trick that protects a driver in Ohio is not quite guaranteed to work the same way the moment that same driver's layout logic is applied, unexamined, to a horizontal signal somewhere else in the world.

[6. TODAY]

You can see every piece of this history the next time you are sitting at a red light, and you do not need to leave your car, or even turn off the engine, to notice any of it.

Look straight up at the signal head itself. Red on top, amber in the middle, green on the bottom, a direct, unbroken line running all the way back to the 1935 Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices and the [LABEL: state vehicle code] that formally adopted its layout one legislature at a time. If you happen to pass through an older downtown block, or drive beneath a railroad bridge with limited vertical clearance, watch specifically for a horizontally mounted signal instead of the usual vertical one. Notice which side the red lamp sits on relative to your own lane of travel, and you are looking at exactly the same positional logic described earlier in this video, just rotated ninety degrees to fit the physical space actually available above that particular stretch of road.

If you want a more direct, visceral way to feel the reasoning behind the rule rather than just hear it described, there are free online simulators built for exactly this purpose, letting anyone preview roughly how a red-green colorblind eye perceives an ordinary traffic signal glowing against a dark evening sky. Run one of those simulations, and the red and green lamps blur toward each other in almost exactly the way the 1935 committee members were quietly worried about when they sat down to standardize signal layout in the first place. The position, though, stays perfectly obvious regardless of which colors blur together, because position was never meant to depend on correctly sorting hue to begin with. That is the entire point of building the redundancy in.

Even the newest intersections being installed right now, fitted with [LABEL: LED] lamp clusters instead of the [LABEL: incandescent bulb] Potts, Morgan, and the original 1935 committee would have worked with, keep the exact same vertical order out of pure institutional habit now backed firmly by law, not because any engineer today is actively re-solving the color-vision problem from scratch for each new intersection. The rule became so deeply embedded in state codes, in manufacturer specifications, and in millions of drivers' trained expectations that breaking it now would arguably be more dangerous than the original problem it was built to solve back in 1935, simply because every driver on the road today has learned, mostly without ever noticing, to trust the position of the lamp without consciously thinking about it at all. You can test this yourself in the smallest possible way: next time you approach a red light, notice how your eyes find the top lamp before you have even consciously registered the color glowing inside it.

[7. CLOSE]

This is the same basic pattern you will find hiding behind a surprising number of ordinary things that almost nobody ever questions out loud: a small, specific engineering decision, made at one particular moment to solve one narrow technical problem, that quietly outlives the original problem entirely and becomes an invisible rule everyone simply obeys without asking why. Consider the standard railroad gauge, the fixed width between two rails, that decided how wide and how tall trains could physically be built, long after the specific surveying and construction reasons behind that exact number stopped mattering to anyone. Consider the side of the road a given country chooses to drive on, locked firmly in place for generations after the original practical reasoning behind that choice faded almost entirely from public memory. Consider the QWERTY keyboard layout, carried forward unbroken through a full century of mechanical typewriters, then electric typewriters, then computers, then the glass touchscreens most people now type on, long after the specific mechanical problem that layout was originally designed around had completely disappeared. The details change from one case to the next. The underlying pattern never does.

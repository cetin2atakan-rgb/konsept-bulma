[BLOK4_DUVARDAKI_KARELER]
The first photograph in this folder is the one every serious account of this case eventually arrives at, and I want to describe it carefully because the description matters more than my opinion of it. It shows four shallow, roughly rectangular depressions pressed into the dry ground of the gully, arranged in a rough square perhaps seven feet across, each depression a few inches deep with sharply defined edges, the kind of edge that comes from a controlled, even pressure applied from directly above rather than from something dragged or dropped. A county captain, called out within the hour, measured each depression himself with a standard tape, noted the measurements on a hand-drawn diagram that is also in this folder, and signed his own name beneath the diagram, which is not something I have seen done in any earlier file in this drawer. Most reports arrive secondhand. This measurement was taken by the man who took it, on the ground, the same afternoon, with his own hands.

The second photograph shows a cluster of greasewood brush just outside the square of depressions, scorched black along one side and still smoking faintly, according to the attached report, when a second officer arrived roughly fifteen minutes behind the first. I have looked at photographs of brush fires and photographs of brush scorched by a grass fire that burned itself out quickly, and this scorching sits somewhere between the two — too contained and too evenly distributed around a clear central point to match an ordinary grass fire, too intense at that central point to match anything a man standing nearby could have started with a match or a dropped cigarette.

The third item is not a photograph but a sketch, made by the patrolman himself within an hour of the event, at the request of the captain who arrived to take his statement, showing a red marking he says he saw on the side of the object before it left — an arrow-like shape over a single horizontal line, drawn, by his own account, in a single continuous stroke of color rather than painted on in separate pieces the way a decal or an insignia usually is. The patrolman is explicit in his statement, which I have read in full, that he does not know what the marking meant and is not offering an interpretation of it, only a faithful copy of what he saw, drawn while the memory of it was still fresh enough to trust.

There is a radio transcript in this folder as well, the patrolman's own call to his dispatcher, made from his cruiser within minutes of the object departing. I am going to read you three lines of it exactly as they were logged, because the tone of them tells you something the written statement, composed later and more carefully, does not. The first line: "I think I need, uh, you better send a wrecker out here, or somethin'." A pause, the dispatcher asking him to repeat. The second line, steadier: "No, belay that. Just get somebody out here. I don't know what I just saw." The third line, logged nearly a full minute later, after what the dispatcher's own note describes as "a long silence on the open channel": "I'm all right. I'm all right. Just get somebody out here."

I want you to notice what is missing from those three lines, because I think it is the most honest part of the entire recording. He does not say what he saw. He asks for help twice and corrects himself once, and in between, for nearly a full minute, he says nothing at all into a channel he knows his dispatcher is listening to. I have read a great many radio transcripts in this job, and men under genuine, sudden strain do not usually manage polished sentences. They manage exactly what this man managed: fragments, a correction, and a silence that the equipment kept recording even though the man holding the microphone had, for most of a minute, nothing left in him to say into it.

I want to describe the second officer's arrival as well, because his statement, shorter than the patrolman's and taken separately, corroborates the physical scene without adding anything about the object itself, since he arrived after it had already departed. He describes the smell in the gully as "like a car battery left too long in the sun," a specific and oddly domestic comparison that appears nowhere else in any file in this drawer, and one I have never been able to decide whether to take as confirmation of something electrical in nature or simply as the best language an ordinary man had available to him for a smell he had no other context to place.

There is one more item I want to describe before moving on, a soil sample card from this folder, noting a reading taken at the center of the depressions two days after the event, once a team from a nearby research laboratory had been brought out to examine the site properly. The card records a background radiation level "slightly but measurably elevated above the surrounding area," a phrase the laboratory's cover note hedges considerably, attributing it to "possible contamination from testing activity unrelated to this incident," which is a plausible explanation given where this event occurred, and which I want to give its full and fair weight rather than pretend the file settles the question for me.

I want to describe one more photograph before I move past the physical evidence, because it is the one I find myself returning to most often, and it is the smallest of the set. It is a close print of a single depression's edge, taken at an angle low to the ground, showing the sharp vertical wall the pressure left in the dry soil. A ruler has been laid along the bottom of the frame for scale, and beside it, barely visible unless you know to look for it, is a second, much fainter indentation — not one of the four main marks, but a narrow, almost comma-shaped pressure line a few inches outside the square, the kind of mark a man's own report, attached to this print, speculates might have come from a fifth, smaller support that touched the ground only briefly. The report does not pursue the speculation further, and no second investigator's note in this folder ever returns to it.

I have spent more hours than I can justify trying to reconstruct what an object resting on four principal points and one smaller, secondary one might actually have looked like, and I have gotten nowhere useful, because every sketch I have made ends up looking either too much like something built by men or too much like nothing at all, a shape with no honest reference point in anything I have ever seen built or grown. I mention the attempt only because I think it illustrates something true about this whole drawer: the evidence, when it is good evidence, often outruns the imagination of the man trying to picture what produced it, and I have come to trust the measurements in this file considerably more than I trust my own ability to draw what they describe.

There is also a page of soil composition analysis that I have not yet mentioned, comparing samples taken from inside the square of depressions against samples taken ten feet outside it. The two sets of samples, according to the typed comparison, were "substantially similar in mineral composition" but differed in one measurable respect: moisture content inside the square tested lower than the surrounding soil by a margin the analyst calls "consistent with brief, localized heating rather than weather or seasonal variation," which is a careful, hedged way of saying that whatever stood on that spot was warm enough to dry the ground beneath it faster than four years of New Mexico sun had managed to dry the ground around it.

[BLOK5_ONLAR_HAKKINDA]
I want to spend a moment on the captain who did the measuring, because I think his signature under that diagram is worth more than most of the testimony in this drawer. He was not a man predisposed to believe his own patrolman's story; his own supplementary report says as much, noting that he drove out expecting to find a hoax and intending to say so plainly once he found it. He measured the depressions anyway, with the same care he says he would have given a car accident or a burglary, because measuring carefully was simply the job, regardless of what he privately expected the measurements to prove. His report does not conclude that anything extraordinary happened. It concludes only that the depressions were real, consistent with one another, and not, in his professional judgment, consistent with a hoax dug by local teenagers in the time available before he arrived.

I want to add a detail about the captain that I have not mentioned yet because I only found it on a second pass through this folder. A supplementary note, added to the file eleven years after the original report, records that the captain, by then retired, gave a brief interview to a local historical society in which he was asked directly whether he had ever changed his mind about what he had measured that afternoon. His answer, quoted in full: "I measured what was there. What was there, I can't account for, and I've stopped trying to. A man can spend the rest of his life trying to explain one afternoon, or he can go on and be a reasonably happy man instead. I chose the second one, and I'd choose it again." I think that is as honest an answer as this drawer has ever given me, from a man who had every professional incentive to either dismiss the case entirely or sensationalize it for the attention, and did neither.

I want to contrast him, briefly, with the handful of outside investigators who descended on the town in the weeks after the story broke, because the file preserves a certain amount of friction between the local officers and the arriving specialists that rarely makes it into popular retellings. One visiting investigator's own notes, included here, complain that the local department was "unhelpfully proprietary" about the site and the evidence, declining to hand over the original tape-measure diagram for weeks on the grounds that it was, in the captain's own words recorded secondhand, "our case before it was anybody else's business." I find myself, reading that exchange thirteen years later, entirely on the captain's side of it.

I also want to note, for the sake of completeness, that this case produced a small number of imitators in the weeks that followed, as these cases usually do — two separate reports from elsewhere in the state, both eventually traced to teenagers who admitted, under fairly gentle questioning, to digging shallow holes in a field and telling their friends an exciting story. I mention this not to muddy the file in front of me but to make the opposite point: investigators who caught two obvious hoaxes within weeks of the original case, using nothing more than ordinary diligence, did not catch this one, because this one did not behave like a hoax when examined with the same diligence. The presence of easily disproven copies, in a strange way, strengthens my confidence in the original rather than weakening it.

[BLOK9_INSANLAR]
I want to tell you what happened to the patrolman after his report became public, because the file is honest about it in a way that surprised me, and because I think it is the clearest single example, across four tapes now, of what this kind of file costs the person at the center of it.

He was, by every account in this folder, a careful and well-regarded officer before that afternoon, with no history of exaggeration and no particular interest in the subject he suddenly found himself associated with. Afterward, according to a brief personnel note added years later, he endured what the note calls, in its flat clerical language, "persistent and unwelcome public attention," including letters, phone calls, and strangers arriving at his home and his place of work hoping to hear the story again directly from him. He gave a small number of interviews in the years that followed, consistent each time with his original statement down to small details, and declined, according to the same note, "the great majority of such requests," eventually asking, in writing, that journalists be directed to his original report rather than to him personally.

One of the few interviews he did give, years later, to a reporter the file identifies only by publication and not by name, includes a detail I have not seen repeated anywhere else: that in the months after the event, he began parking his cruiser facing the direction of that gully whenever his patrol route allowed it, not out of any stated fear, but, in his own words as quoted, "so I'd see it coming, if it ever did come back, instead of hearing about it from someone else first." He is quoted as saying it never did come back, not once, in the remainder of his career on that patrol, and that he never decided whether to be grateful for that or quietly disappointed by it.

There is a line in a letter he apparently wrote to a researcher some years later, quoted in this folder without further comment, that I want to read to you exactly as it appears: "I have told it the same way every time because it happened the same way every time. I am tired of being asked if I am tired of telling it. I am tired of being asked if I made it up. I was a police officer for a reason, and the reason was never this." I do not think I need to add anything to that. I think it says, in four short sentences, everything this file and the three before it have been circling around without ever saying quite so plainly.

I want to add that he kept his job, and by most accounts kept it well, for a long career afterward, which separates him from several of the men I have told you about on earlier tapes. I do not know if that is because this particular department was simply more protective of its own than the agencies behind the other files, or because by nineteen sixty-four the public had grown just slightly more willing to extend an ordinary, well-regarded officer the benefit of the doubt than it had been willing to extend a technician or a junior airman in the years before him. I would like to believe it is the second reason. I am honest enough with you, after four tapes, to admit I am not certain it is.

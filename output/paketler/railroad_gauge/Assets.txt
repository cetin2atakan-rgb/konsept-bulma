## Description
Under almost every train in the United States, the rails sit exactly 4 feet 8.5 inches apart, and most of the world's railways use the same width. Nobody calculated that number for speed or stability.
It came from horse-drawn coal wagonways in the north of England, survived Britain's Victorian gauge war, and was locked in across the American South in a single weekend in 1886.

This video follows the gauge from the colliery wagonways of North East England to George Stephenson's Killingworth locomotives, the Stockton and Darlington and the Liverpool and Manchester railways, the Roman chariot myth (and why it doesn't hold up), Brunel's 7-foot broad gauge and the Gauge Act of 1846, the dozens of gauges on early American railroads, and the 1886 regauging of more than 11,000 miles of southern track in about 36 hours. Then the real reason it won: network effects, sunk cost and path dependence, what would have happened if broad gauge had won, and where the split still shows up today, from New York subway clearances to the Spain–France border.

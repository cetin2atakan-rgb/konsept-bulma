[1. HOOK + PERSONA INTRO]

Door County is hiding far more than cherry orchards and lighthouse postcards. At the peninsula's tip lies a strait so dangerous that French sailors named it the door of the dead, and so many ships went down inside it that divers are still finding wrecks on its floor today. Beneath its thin limestone spine runs the very same rock shelf that carries the water over Niagara Falls, hundreds of miles to the east. On an island off that same tip, a handful of Icelandic bachelors built what became, for a time, one of the largest concentrations of Icelandic Americans anywhere in the United States. And in the peninsula's southern townships, families who arrived speaking French founded what the federal government now calls the largest rural settlement of Belgian descent in the nation. Somewhere between the orchard rows of Egg Harbor and the dolomite bluffs above Gills Rock, this narrow strip of Wisconsin keeps hiding things that even longtime visitors drive past without ever noticing. Today we will count down twenty-five remarkable geographic facts about the Door Peninsula, Wisconsin.

I'm Walter Hale. I taught geography for thirty-eight years in a small high school in Kentucky, and I still can't drive through a county without wondering why it looks the way it does. Some of these twenty-five facts come from men who carried bricks out to build lighthouses by hand. Some come from a chapel built to look exactly like a church in Norway. And one comes from the very name of the county itself. Let's start at number twenty-five.

[FACT 25: Peninsula State Park | 45.155,-87.233]
Fact twenty-five.
Peninsula State Park sits on the Green Bay side of the peninsula, just outside the village of Fish Creek, and at roughly thirty-eight hundred acres it is one of the largest and most heavily visited state parks in Wisconsin. The state legislature set the land aside in 1909 and 1910, acting on a survey from landscape planner John Nolen, who had been hired specifically to study the Door Peninsula's shoreline and decide what was worth protecting before cottages and quarries got to it first. The park's limestone headlands, white cedar groves, and eight miles of Green Bay shoreline, about thirteen kilometers, were never cleared for farmland the way so much of the rest of the peninsula was, largely because the thin, rocky soil sitting on top of the bedrock ridge was too poor to plow. If you drive the park's Shore Road today, past Eagle Terrace and Weborg Point, you are looking at almost exactly the same bluff line those early surveyors mapped more than a hundred years ago. I used to tell my students that a park is really just a piece of land that lost a fight with a plow, and Peninsula State Park is one of the best examples of that I ever found on a map. Families have pitched tents in its campgrounds for four generations now, and the park's own golf course, built in 1921, is one of the oldest in the state still played on close to its original layout. On a clear evening in October, Chambers Island sits silhouetted against the water from the park's bluffs like a dark ship anchored offshore. The park's Eagle Tower observation deck, rebuilt in recent years after the original wooden structure was closed over safety concerns, lifts visitors high above the forest canopy, with views that reach across the water toward Michigan's Upper Peninsula on a clear day. Visitor counts here are commonly reported to top one million in a single year, more than many entire national parks draw across the same twelve months. The park's White Cedar Nature Center runs year-round programming on the peninsula's plants, birds, and geology, giving visitors a place to learn the why behind the view before they ever set foot on a trail.

[FACT 24: Eagle Bluff Lighthouse | 45.168,-87.223]
Fact twenty-four.
Inside Peninsula State Park, on a limestone headland above Green Bay, stands Eagle Bluff Lighthouse, built in 1868 after the federal Lighthouse Board decided that growing shipping traffic through the bay needed a steady light at that point. The bluff lifts the lamp room about seventy-six feet above the water, about twenty-three meters, high enough that ship captains could pick it out long before they reached the dangerous shoal water closer to shore. A single keeper and his family tended the light by hand from 1868 until 1926, when the station was automated and the job of lighting it every evening simply disappeared. The lighthouse sits on state park land managed by the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources, and the old keeper's house is now a small museum run by the Door County Historical Society. If you walk the short bluff trail from the Eagle Terrace picnic area, you come out directly above the tower roof, close enough to see the same cracked paint and the same narrow catwalk the keepers once used to reach the lamp in a storm. Restoration crews spent from 1960 to 1963 returning the station to its appearance from the keeper era, work that later helped the lighthouse earn a place on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970. The tower still shows a working light today, though the oil lamp the keepers once climbed up to tend every evening has long since been replaced by an automatic electric one.

[FACT 23: Pottawatomie Lighthouse, Rock Island | 45.433,-86.821]
Fact twenty-three.
Out past Washington Island, on the smaller and harder-to-reach Rock Island, stands Pottawatomie Lighthouse, first lit in 1836, which is widely cited as the oldest lighthouse station in the state of Wisconsin and one of the oldest anywhere on Lake Michigan. Detroit merchants had petitioned Congress for the light years earlier, arguing that this passage, the widest route connecting Green Bay and Lake Michigan, urgently needed a guide for ships. That first tower was poorly built and did not last, so the government replaced it with the lighthouse that still stands today in 1858. Civilian keepers lived on Rock Island and tended the light from 1836 until 1946, when it was finally automated and the last family packed up and left the island for good. Volunteers with the Friends of Rock Island later restored the building to look the way it did around 1910, down to the furniture inside. There is no bridge and no ferry service directly to Rock Island from the mainland, so if you want to stand at the base of Wisconsin's oldest lighthouse, you still have to take a boat from Washington Island and walk the rest of the way in. That first light was burning twelve years before Wisconsin even became a state in 1848, proof of how early shippers recognized the danger of this water route. The island carries no cars and no regular services beyond a single primitive campground, which keeps the lighthouse grounds almost exactly as quiet today as they were under the last civilian keeper. The same volunteer group still raises money for upkeep, since the state park budget alone could never cover the cost of maintaining a building this remote.

[FACT 22: Door County's lighthouse concentration | 44.834,-87.378]
Fact twenty-two.
Door County is often described as home to more lighthouses than any other county in the United States, with as many as eleven historic light stations still standing along its shoreline today, and as many as twelve counted across its full recorded history. That is a remarkable number for a single county, and it exists because the peninsula forces ship traffic to squeeze through a narrow, rock-studded channel between two very different bodies of water, Green Bay on one side and Lake Michigan on the other. Every one of those lighthouses was built to answer the same basic problem, warning captains away from a shoreline made of hard dolomite ledges hiding just beneath the surface. Cana Island Lighthouse, reached by a short causeway near Baileys Harbor, draws the most visitors of the group today. If you plan a slow drive around the peninsula's edge, from Sherwood Point near Sturgeon Bay up to Eagle Bluff and out to Cana Island, you can see four or five of these towers without ever leaving sight of the water. Wisconsin's other great lighthouse cluster sits at the opposite end of the state, in the Apostle Islands National Lakeshore on Lake Superior, which is credited with more lighthouses than any other National Park Service site in the country, underlining just how much of Wisconsin's maritime history is concentrated along these two short stretches of shoreline. Local preservation groups now coordinate an annual lighthouse festival each spring, opening several normally closed towers to the public for a single long weekend. Volunteers who staff that festival often point out that half the lighthouses on their tour map are only reachable at all because a county or a nonprofit group decided a crumbling tower was still worth saving.

[FACT 21: Plum Island Lighthouse | 45.267,-86.975]
Fact twenty-one.
Plum Island, a low strip of land sitting almost directly inside the dangerous strait at the peninsula's tip, has carried a lighthouse since the late 1840s, built specifically to guide ships through the passage locally known as Death's Door. The first tower stood too far inside the channel to be of much use to incoming captains, so the government built a stronger station in 1896 that stood closer to open water. For nearly a century, United States Coast Guard crews stationed on Plum Island referred to themselves, half-proudly, as the Guardians of Death's Door, keeping watch over one of the busiest and most feared stretches of water on the Great Lakes until the station finally closed in 1990. In 2007, the island passed to the United States Fish and Wildlife Service, and it is now managed mainly as a bird sanctuary rather than a navigation post. From the bluffs above Gills Rock, Plum Island is close enough that you can watch the current pull visibly around its shore, a plain reminder of why sailors feared this particular stretch of water above almost any other on the lake. A nonprofit group called the Friends of Plum and Pilot Island Lighthouses now works with federal wildlife managers to preserve both stations, occasionally opening Plum Island to the public for a single guided visit each summer. Visitors who do make the trip typically arrive by a chartered boat out of Gills Rock, since there is no public dock or regular ferry service to Plum Island at all. The old fog signal building on the island, once used to sound a warning through heavy weather, now sits quiet, since modern ships rely on radar and satellite positioning instead of listening for a horn in the fog.

[FACT 20: Pilot Island | 45.292,-86.942]
Fact twenty.
Two miles east of Plum Island sits Pilot Island, so small that its entire surface is barely larger than a city block, and so central to the Death's Door passage that early sailors used it the way its name suggests, as a landmark to steer by. The current brick lighthouse there dates to 1858, built after the federal Lighthouse Board recommended the site as critical to safe passage through the strait. By some long-standing accounts among Great Lakes lighthouse historians, Pilot Island witnessed more shipwrecks than any other light station on the entire Great Lakes system, a grim record that reflects just how many vessels misjudged the currents around it. A keeper lived on the island until 1962, when the light was automated and the last resident left. Today Pilot Island belongs to the United States Fish and Wildlife Service and serves as a nesting sanctuary for colonial birds, closed to the public, which means the island that once watched over so many wrecks now mostly watches over gulls and cormorants instead. At only a few acres in size, Pilot Island is barely large enough to hold the lighthouse and a small outbuilding, yet thousands of nesting birds now crowd across nearly every open surface each summer. Unlike Plum Island, Pilot Island allows no public access whatsoever, even by private boat, in order to protect those nesting colonies.

[FACT 19: Chambers Island Lighthouse | 45.192,-87.360]
Fact nineteen.
Seven miles out into Green Bay, part of the Town of Gibraltar but reachable only by boat, sits Chambers Island, named for Colonel Talbot Chambers and remembered in local tradition by an earlier Native name, Ke-che Mah-ne-doo. Congress approved funding for a lighthouse there in 1866, the government purchased forty acres the following year, and the light was first lit in 1868 to mark the western passage into the bay. A rotating lens was installed in 1899, and the station's power source changed more than once over the decades, moving from an early electric generator to acetylene gas and eventually to solar power. After the original light tower was replaced by a plain skeletal structure in 1961, the historic station fell into disrepair and was vandalized, until local residents pushed to save it, and ownership passed to the Town of Gibraltar in 1976. I used to remind my students that an island only seven miles from shore can still be a different world entirely, and Chambers Island, with its own small year-round population and its own sense of isolation, proves that point as well as anywhere I ever studied. The restored lighthouse opened as a town park in 1977 and still welcomes visitors who make the crossing by boat. Chambers Island sits on the same dolomite bedrock as the islands off the peninsula's tip, part of the broken chain of limestone islands scattered where the Niagara Escarpment dips beneath the water. With no ferry service at all, the island's small handful of year-round residents rely on private boats and a small airstrip for everything from groceries to mail.

[FACT 18: Cave Point County Park | 44.885,-87.192]
Fact eighteen.
On the Lake Michigan side of the peninsula, near the small community of Jacksonport, nineteen acres of shoreline known as Cave Point County Park sit directly on top of an exposed section of the Niagara Escarpment, the same long dolomite ridge that forms Niagara Falls far to the east. Here the open water of Lake Michigan has spent thousands of years battering the exposed rock, carving low cliffs and hollowing out wave-cut caves at the waterline, which is exactly what gives the park its name. In winter, water that seeps into the cracks of the rock freezes and expands, slowly widening the same openings that summer waves carved out, so the shoreline keeps reshaping itself in every season. The county park is bordered by Whitefish Dunes State Park on three sides, so the two sites are usually visited together. If you stand at the park's main overlook on a windy day, you can watch waves disappear into a cave at the base of the bluff and reappear as spray shooting straight up through a crack several feet away. Photographers now travel from across the country specifically to shoot sunrise over the point's wave-cut ledges, even though park signage warns that the same cold water and sudden drop-offs that carved the caves can just as easily pull an unsteady visitor in. The small parking lot fills early on summer weekends, because the narrow access road was never built for the crowds the caves now attract. Underwater, the same dolomite ledge continues out beneath the surface for a short distance before dropping off, which is part of why the water near the point turns from pale green to deep blue so abruptly.

[FACT 17: Whitefish Dunes State Park | 44.883,-87.190]
Fact seventeen.
Just south of Cave Point, Whitefish Dunes State Park protects roughly eight hundred sixty-five acres along Lake Michigan, including the largest set of Great Lakes sand dunes anywhere in Wisconsin. The dunes themselves sit on top of the same ancient dolostone bedrock as the rest of the peninsula, but the sand on top of that rock was carried in by wind blowing steadily off the open lake, piling up in long rows that still shift and grow today. The tallest of those rows, a dune nicknamed Old Baldy, rises about ninety-three feet above the beach, the highest dune anywhere in the state. Farther from the water, older rows of dunes have stabilized under full-grown forest, so a hiker walking inland is actually climbing over older and older sand hills without ever realizing the ground beneath the trees used to be an open beach. Rangers ask visitors to stay off the open dune faces entirely, since a single set of footprints can kill the beach grass holding an entire dune together, which is part of why the park now routes hikers around Old Baldy on a boardwalk instead of straight up its face. Archaeologists have identified several pre-contact village sites buried within the park's dune field, evidence that Native communities camped along this same shifting shoreline for many centuries before the park ever protected it. Rangers have clocked wind gusts off the open lake strong enough to visibly shift loose sand across the dune crests within a single afternoon. A nature center near the park entrance keeps a small display of artifacts recovered from the dune sites, giving visitors a chance to see arrowheads and pottery pulled from sand that still shifts a little more with every storm.

[FACT 16: The Ridges Sanctuary | 45.078,-87.117]
Fact sixteen.
Along the Lake Michigan shoreline near Baileys Harbor, a nature preserve called the Ridges Sanctuary protects about thirty parallel ridges and swales, a landscape shaped by Lake Michigan's retreating shoreline over roughly the last eleven hundred to fourteen hundred years. Each dry sandy ridge sits only a short walk from a wet, low swale beside it, and that constant back-and-forth between dry and wet ground packs an unusual number of distinct habitats into a very small area. Some of the wettest swales support small stands of swamp conifers, while others hold open bog plants more typical of land hundreds of miles farther north. Botanists have counted between twenty-five and twenty-eight native orchid species inside the sanctuary's boundaries, including the rare ram's head lady's slipper, making it one of the richest orchid habitats anywhere in the Midwest. If you walk the sanctuary's boardwalk trail in early summer, past Baileys Harbor's own small range light, you can cross from a dry pine ridge into a wet cedar swale and back again within a hundred paces, feeling the ground and the air both change under your feet. Local citizens founded the Ridges Sanctuary in 1937 specifically to stop commercial development along the shoreline near the Baileys Harbor range lights, making it one of the earliest grassroots land preservation efforts anywhere in Wisconsin. The sanctuary today runs its own small nature center and trains volunteer naturalists to lead guided walks through the ridges each summer.

[FACT 15: Toft Point | 45.070,-87.110]
Fact fifteen.
Just north of Baileys Harbor, a peninsula-within-a-peninsula called Toft Point protects seven hundred forty-three acres of shoreline forest, recognized by the National Park Service as a National Natural Landmark and designated a Wisconsin State Natural Area back in 1967. In 2015, Toft Point earned an even rarer label, as part of the Door Peninsula Coastal Wetlands listed as a Ramsar Wetland of International Importance, a designation shared with only a small number of sites across the entire United States. Most of the point is covered in a mixed forest of sugar maple, yellow birch, hemlock, and balsam fir, but its eastern shoreline carries a narrow band of relict boreal forest, dominated by balsam fir and white spruce, more typical of land far to the north in Canada. The University of Wisconsin-Green Bay manages the site today. If you walk the shoreline trail out toward the point itself, the sudden shift from a maple woodland into a cold, spruce-scented forest edge feels less like Wisconsin and more like the shore of a much colder lake, hundreds of miles north. The point takes its name from the Toft family, Danish immigrants who settled here in the late 1800s, and above all from Emma Toft, who spent most of her long life on the point and fought to keep it wild before it passed into conservation hands. Visitors are asked to stay on the main trail, a rule meant to protect the sensitive relict forest from overuse.

[FACT 14: Door County's karst bedrock | 44.653,-87.487]
Fact fourteen.
Underneath its farm fields and orchards, most of Door County sits on karst topography, meaning the bedrock itself is riddled with cracks, sinkholes, and underground channels dissolved into the Silurian dolomite over a very long stretch of geologic time. In a few rural stretches of the county, soil scientists have mapped the ground as practically a sheet of cracked rock covered with only a thin skin of dirt, sometimes just a few feet deep, sitting directly above those fractures. That thin cover means rainwater, fertilizer, and anything else spread on a field can reach the groundwater below in a matter of hours instead of the weeks or months it would take to filter through thicker soil elsewhere. Researchers studying a small Door County drainage basin found that nitrate levels in groundwater spiked five to six times higher during periods when water was actively draining straight down through nearby sinkholes. I remember telling my students that the ground under your feet is never just dirt, it is a filter, and in a place like Door County, that filter is full of holes. Local and state agencies have mapped many of the known sinkholes across the county, though officials admit plenty more almost certainly exist that no one has found yet. Local health officials urge anyone buying a rural property here to have the well water tested specifically because of this fractured bedrock, advice few buyers moving from thicker-soiled parts of the Midwest ever expect to hear. Farmers across the county have also adopted reduced-tillage and reduced-fertilizer practices in recent years specifically to slow how fast nutrients reach the fractured rock below their fields.

[FACT 13: The Door County fish boil | 45.261,-87.052]
Fact thirteen.
Door County's fish boil, in which chunks of whitefish are cooked outdoors in a huge pot of boiling salted water over an open fire, has a history that nobody can quite pin down with certainty. Some local historians trace the practice back to the Potawatomi, who understood the value of rendering fat out of fish through boiling long before European settlers arrived. Others point instead to the Scandinavian immigrants who began arriving in large numbers in the late 1800s, bringing a similar boiling tradition from Norway and feeding it to logging crews clearing the peninsula's forests. Whichever version came first, the modern tourist version of the fish boil traces to 1961, when Lawrence and Annette Wickman began serving it at The Viking restaurant in Ellison Bay, followed soon after by the White Gull Inn in Fish Creek. The dramatic moment most visitors remember, a sudden flare-up of kerosene thrown onto the fire to boil the pot over and skim away the fish oil floating on top, is partly practical, since it carries the oil away, and partly theater, exaggerated over the years for the crowds of watching tourists. If you find yourself at a fish boil in Ellison Bay or Fish Creek on a summer evening, you are watching a tradition that is at least part logging camp, part immigrant kitchen, and part modern show, all served out of the same pot. A single kettle can hold enough water, salt, and whitefish chunks to feed well over a hundred people in one sitting, and the person tending the fire, known locally as the boilmaster, usually times the final flare-up by experience rather than by the clock. Most restaurants today serve the boiled whitefish with melted butter and a wedge of cherry pie afterward, a pairing that has become almost as traditional as the boil itself. Some inns hold their boils only a few nights a week even at the height of summer, since the whole process, from lighting the fire to serving the last plate, can easily take the better part of an evening.

[FACT 12: Door County cherry orchards | 45.137,-87.107]
Fact twelve.
Commercial cherry growing on the Door Peninsula began in the 1890s, after fruit grower A.L. Hatch and University of Wisconsin horticulture professor Emmett Goff visited the peninsula and, in 1896, recommended concentrating on a single tart variety called Montmorency. The crop thrived in the lake-moderated climate, and by the mid-twentieth century the county had earned the nickname Cherryland, with production peaking somewhere around 1949 to the 1950s, when as many as seven hundred growers were harvesting up to fifty million pounds of cherries across roughly ten thousand acres in a good year. A combination of volatile harvests, weak distribution networks, and rising land prices driven by tourism eventually pushed many growers to tear out their orchards for cottages and resorts instead. Door County has since lost its old title as the nation's leading tart cherry region to Traverse City, Michigan, though the Wisconsin Historical Society reports that Montmorency cherries grown here still make up ninety-five percent of all tart cherries produced in the state, with around seven million pounds still harvested most years. If you drive the county roads around Egg Harbor and Sister Bay in early May, the surviving orchards still bloom in the same solid white rows that made this stretch of road famous long before anyone thought to call it a tourist destination. Several of the surviving orchards still operate as pick-your-own farms each July, letting visitors fill their own buckets from trees planted on some of the same rows first set out back in the 1890s. A university horticulture research station near Sturgeon Bay still studies cold-hardy fruit varieties on land not far from where those first commercial cherry trees went into the ground. Each spring, local tourism offices now track the blossom dates almost the way other regions track the changing leaves in fall, since a week of bad weather at the wrong moment can shape the entire visitor season.

[FACT 11: Ephraim's founding | 45.161,-87.176]
Fact eleven.
The village of Ephraim traces its founding to 1853, when Pastor Andreas Iverson led a small group of Moravian settlers north to the Door Peninsula after a land dispute broke apart their earlier congregation near Green Bay. Iverson purchased roughly four hundred twenty-five acres of land, surveyed it himself, and divided it into lots, setting part of the property aside for a future church. The group's earliest months on the peninsula were rough, living in temporary shanties on nearby Eagle Island, now known as Horseshoe Island, before moving to the permanent village site later that same year. What began as a struggling community of fewer than thirty people grew into one of the most recognizable villages on the peninsula, and the church the settlers eventually built still stands today as one of the oldest churches anywhere in Door County. Ephraim's village board later enforced some of the strictest building and zoning rules in the county, partly in an effort to protect the look of those original white clapboard buildings along the harbor. If you walk the shoreline road through Ephraim today, past Anderson Dock, the white church steeple on the hill above the water is the same one the Moravian settlers eventually built to replace Iverson's own front room. The temperance values those founders brought with them lasted for generations, and Ephraim remained a dry village, with no alcohol sold inside its limits, until local voters finally lifted the ban in the early twenty-first century. Ephraim's strict building code today still pushes most new construction toward the same white-painted wood siding the Moravian settlers favored on their very first houses.

[FACT 10: Fish Creek's founding | 45.139,-87.237]
Fact ten.
Fish Creek, on the Green Bay side of the peninsula, grew up around a settler named Asa Thorp, generally credited by the Wisconsin Historical Society as the village's founder, even though the very first settler on the site was actually a man named Increase Claflin, who arrived with his family around 1844. Thorp came to the area in the mid-to-late 1840s, bought up much of the surrounding land, started a lumbering business, and built what historical records describe as the first pier anywhere in Door County, a structure that let the growing settlement ship lumber and, later, fruit out by boat. Gibraltar township, which contains the village, was officially organized in 1857, with its first election held at Thorp's own house. I used to tell my students that most towns are not founded, they simply accumulate, and Fish Creek's history, with one settler before the founder and years of uncertainty about exact dates, is as good an example of that as any small town I ever researched. Thorp's original log cabin, built around 1849 and moved more than once since, still stands today at a small park known as Founders Square, a short walk from the harbor his pier once served. Just down the shoreline from that original pier, the Peninsula Players theater has staged professional summer productions since 1935, a run long enough to make it one of the oldest resident summer theaters anywhere in the United States.

[FACT 9: The Clearing Folk School | 45.263,-87.054]
Fact nine.
Landscape architect Jens Jensen, already famous for designing public parks in Chicago, bought land near Ellison Bay in 1919 as a summer retreat and spent more than a decade slowly developing it into something larger, a school built around the idea that people needed both a literal clearing in the woods and a figurative clearing for the mind. Jensen opened The Clearing as a working folk school in 1935, modeling its program loosely on the folk schools he remembered from his own youth in Denmark. A fire destroyed the school's main building in 1937, and Jensen rebuilt it with the help of architect Hugh Garden rather than walking away from the project. After Jensen's death in 1951, leadership passed to his longtime assistant, Mertha Fulkerson, and the Wisconsin Farm Bureau sponsored the school's operations from 1953 until 1988, when an independent board took over. The Clearing was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974 and continues to run classes in natural sciences, fine arts, and traditional crafts on the same wooded grounds above Green Bay today, just a short drive from the village of Ellison Bay. Class sizes at The Clearing remain deliberately small, often limited to a dozen or so students at a time, continuing Jensen's original belief that a folk school only works if everyone in the room can actually come to know each other. Visiting instructors and craftspeople still live on-site in small cabins scattered through the property during each session, much as Jensen intended when he first laid out the grounds. Jensen himself designed the paths and plantings around the buildings using only species native to the region, treating the grounds as much a part of the curriculum as anything taught inside a classroom.

[FACT 8: Björklunden's Boynton Chapel | 45.067,-87.087]
Fact eight.
On a four hundred twenty-five acre estate south of Baileys Harbor, a summer couple named Winifred and Donald Boynton spent from 1939 to 1947 handcrafting a small wooden chapel modeled directly on the Garmo stave church, a centuries-old building preserved at the Maihaugen open-air museum in Lillehammer, Norway. Every joint and carving inside the Boynton Chapel was built by hand rather than ordered from a catalog, and the finished building holds forty-one hand-painted frescoes that Winifred herself painted in a folk art style, along with carved butternut pew ends crafted to match traditional Norwegian woodworking patterns. The Boyntons named their estate Björklunden, meaning grove of birches, and in 1963 Winifred Boynton gave the entire property to Lawrence University, which still uses it today as its northern campus. The chapel still hosts weddings, drawing couples who want a Norwegian stave church without the cost of a transatlantic flight. If you take one of the university's limited public tours in summer, walking from the main lodge down toward the chapel, the dark wood and dragon-head carvings on the roofline look convincingly like something transplanted directly from a Norwegian hillside rather than built on the shore of Lake Michigan. Lawrence University now uses the surrounding grounds for off-campus seminars throughout the academic year, meaning the chapel that once stood only for the Boyntons regularly hosts college students studying subjects as far removed from Norwegian woodworking as marine biology and philosophy.

[FACT 7: Horseshoe Island | 45.172,-87.202]
Fact seven.
Just offshore from Ephraim, at the mouth of Nicolet Bay, sits a small island that Door County's earliest Norwegian settlers once called Eagle Island, now known on every modern map as Horseshoe Island. According to local cemetery records and long-held oral history, in 1854, barely a year after Ephraim's founding, a cholera outbreak swept through a group of newly arrived Norwegian immigrants, and several victims were buried in a small cemetery on the island rather than on the mainland, a burial ground that still exists today, largely forgotten by visitors who land there. In 1888, a family named Folda bought the island for five hundred dollars and built a substantial summer lodge they called Engelmar, giving the island a second life as a private retreat decades after its grim start. When Wisconsin established Peninsula State Park in 1909, Horseshoe Island was eventually folded into the park's boundaries as well, and today it has no public ferry or regular transportation at all, reachable only by private boat in summer or on foot across the ice in a hard winter. A one-mile loop trail named for the old Folda lodge still circles the island today, passing close to the quiet cemetery that marks where the cholera outbreak first struck. Because the island carries no services of any kind, not even a reliable supply of drinking water, visitors are expected to bring in everything they need for the day and carry every bit of it back out again. Kayak outfitters based in Ephraim now run regular guided paddles out to the island specifically so visitors can see the old cemetery and the lodge site without needing a boat of their own.

[FACT 6: Fincantieri Bay Shipbuilding | 44.830,-87.370]
Fact six.
Sturgeon Bay's shipbuilding industry traces back to 1896, when a small company called Rieboldt and Wolter set up a modest yard that grew, under a series of new owners and new names, into one of the most important shipyards on the entire Great Lakes. During the Second World War, the yard then known as Sturgeon Bay Shipbuilding and Drydock Company built and launched eighty-five steel vessels for the war effort, making Sturgeon Bay one of Wisconsin's largest industrial contributors to that war. After Manitowoc Corporation closed its own shipyard in 1967 and absorbed two Sturgeon Bay yards, the combined operation took the name Bay Shipbuilding Company in 1968, and through the 1970s it built roughly thirty Great Lakes vessels, including six thousand-foot freighters, at a time when the yard employed more than two thousand workers. New shipbuilding slowed sharply after the 1980s, shifting the yard's focus mainly toward repair work, barges, and specialized vessels, until the Italian company Fincantieri purchased the operation in 2008 and renamed it Fincantieri Bay Shipbuilding. The yard still builds the occasional large vessel today, and it is often described as the last active builder of large ships anywhere on the United States side of the Great Lakes. The yard's dry docks remain among only a handful of facilities anywhere on the Great Lakes capable of taking in a thousand-foot freighter for repairs. The yard's workforce today still numbers in the hundreds, making it among the largest industrial employers anywhere on the Door Peninsula. Tour boats on Sturgeon Bay's waterfront now point out the shipyard almost the same way they point out the historic lighthouses, treating working industry as one more landmark worth slowing down for.

[FACT 5: The Sturgeon Bay Ship Canal | 44.802,-87.368]
Fact five.
For most of the nineteenth century, any ship traveling between Green Bay and Lake Michigan had no choice but to sail all the way around the top of the peninsula and through the dangerous Death's Door passage, a detour that cost time, fuel, and more than a few ships outright. Joseph Harris Senior, a local newspaper editor who arrived in Sturgeon Bay in 1855, spent years pushing for a shortcut, and in 1864 the state granted his company a charter to dig a canal straight across the narrow neck of land separating the two bodies of water. Construction finally began on July 8, 1872, only to stall for three years once the nationwide financial panic of 1873 dried up funding, before digging resumed in 1877 and crews finally broke through to join the two waters on the evening of June 28, 1878. The federal government took over the unprofitable private canal in 1893, paying one hundred three thousand dollars for it, and soon began widening and deepening the channel for larger traffic. The finished canal cut an estimated one hundred fifty miles off the water route between Green Bay and the ports of Milwaukee and Chicago, and it let captains avoid Death's Door entirely, a single piece of engineering that reshaped how the entire peninsula connected to the rest of the Great Lakes. The United States Army Corps of Engineers still maintains the channel today, dredging it periodically to keep pace with the draft of modern Great Lakes freighters that dwarf anything Joseph Harris imagined when he first proposed the shortcut. In the city itself, Sturgeon Bay's drawbridges still lift on demand to let tall boats and freighters pass through on their way to and from the canal.

[FACT 4: Namur Belgian-American Historic District | 44.619,-87.606]
Fact four.
In the southern townships of Door County, where the peninsula narrows back toward the mainland, French-speaking Belgian immigrants began arriving in 1853, first from the central Belgian province of Brabant and later from the southern province of Namur, settling a stretch of farmland they would eventually name after the places they had left behind. The National Register of Historic Places now recognizes the resulting Namur Belgian-American Historic District, spanning parts of Door, Kewaunee, and Brown counties, as the largest rural settlement of Belgian nationality anywhere in the United States, a landscape where the farmsteads themselves, not just the churches or town halls, carry the historic designation. Town names across the settlement, including Brussels, Namur, Rosiere, and Luxemburg, still echo the immigrants' home provinces, and the community's Kermiss harvest festival, celebrated every fall, survives from traditions the settlers brought with them more than a hundred fifty years ago. I remember explaining to my students that the Peshtigo fire of 1871, one of the deadliest wildfires in American history, swept through this same settlement and destroyed much of its earliest timber construction, which is exactly why so many of the farmhouses that survive today were rebuilt afterward in brick and local limestone instead of wood. Small roadside chapels, built by individual families as acts of devotion rather than as part of any parish building program, still dot the back roads of Namur and Brussels today, a detail that even many longtime Door County visitors never notice as they drive past on their way north to the orchards and lighthouses. At Kermiss, descendants of the original Belgian families still serve the same chicken booyah stew that once fed harvest crews a century and a half ago.

[FACT 3: Washington Island's Icelandic settlers | 45.380,-86.938]
Fact three.
In 1870, a Danish merchant named William Wickman persuaded four Icelandic bachelors, carpenters and fishermen who had been working for him in the Icelandic town of Eyrarbakki, to cross the Atlantic and settle on Washington Island, at the very tip of the Door Peninsula. The Wisconsin Historical Society credits the resulting community as the oldest Icelandic settlement in the United States, though the island's own historians and at least one newspaper account describe it instead as the second oldest, a small disagreement that has never been fully resolved. What is not disputed is the scale the settlement eventually reached, since the Wisconsin Historical Society reports that by 1924, roughly ten percent of all Icelanders living in the United States called this single corner of northern Door County home. The community built its early economy almost entirely on commercial fishing, the same trade most of the original settlers had practiced back in Iceland, before Danish and Norwegian farmers arriving separately from Milwaukee introduced the island's first real agriculture. Today, Icelandic family names still appear on mailboxes and gravestones across Washington Island, and the island continues to host an annual celebration honoring that nineteenth-century crossing, a tradition passed down through generations of families who never stopped identifying with the island their ancestors reached by immigrant ship rather than by the car ferry visitors use today. Ferries still run several times a day between Washington Island and the Northport pier at the peninsula's tip, a crossing of only about thirty minutes in good weather, a fraction of the uncertain, often dangerous wait the earliest settlers sometimes faced.

[FACT 2: The Niagara Escarpment | 44.843,-87.397]
Fact two.
The entire spine of the Door Peninsula, the long limestone ridge that shapes nearly every tall bluff and headland on the Green Bay side and the wave-cut caves along the Lake Michigan shore, is a single exposed section of the Niagara Escarpment, the very same band of Silurian-age dolostone that, hundreds of miles to the east, forms the ledge over which Niagara Falls pours. Wisconsin's state geological survey dates the rock itself to somewhere between four hundred forty-three and four hundred seventeen million years ago, when a shallow, warm, tropical sea covered this entire part of North America and slowly deposited the layers of dolomite that now sit exposed along the peninsula's shoreline. The escarpment stands out across the landscape mainly because that dolostone resists erosion far better than the softer rock layers on either side of it, so softer stone wore away over millions of years while the harder dolomite layer kept standing as a ridge, eventually carved further by the Green Bay Lobe of the last great ice sheet. The same unbroken band of rock continues northeast from Door County across the Garden Peninsula of Michigan, through Manitoulin Island and the Bruce Peninsula in Ontario, and all the way into New York state, where it is finally cut through by the Niagara River. I used to show my students a map of the entire Great Lakes basin just to watch their faces when I traced that single ridge line from the orchards of Door County all the way to the base of Niagara Falls, because almost none of them ever expected the two places to share any connection at all, let alone the exact same rock. Door County's own dolomite quarries have fed regional construction projects for more than a century, cutting into the very same rock layer that, hundreds of miles away, forms the ledge of one of the most famous waterfalls in the world. Stone quarried from this same escarpment has gone into building foundations across the peninsula for generations, meaning many of the farmhouses and churches described earlier in this list are built from the very rock they stand on.

[FACT 1: Death's Door Strait | 45.283,-86.999]
Fact one.
At the very tip of the peninsula, the narrow, current-swept passage between the mainland and Washington Island carries a name that gave the entire county its identity, Death's Door, an English translation of the original French term, La Porte des Morts, or the door of the dead. The earliest written reference to the name comes from a French missionary named Emmanuel Crespel, who in 1728 described the peninsula itself using the related phrase Cap a la Mort, and later legends, most of them embellished long after the fact, attached the name to a supposed disaster involving a Native war party caught in a sudden storm while crossing the strait. Whatever its true origin, the passage earned its grim reputation honestly over the following two centuries, swallowing ships like the Fleetwing in a gale in 1888 and driving the steamer Louisiana onto the rocks at Washington Island during the violent storm known as the Big Blow of 1913, losses severe enough that the federal government eventually built not one but two lighthouses, on Plum Island and Pilot Island, just to help ships survive the crossing. Washington Island itself, lying just beyond the strait, actually carries an unrelated and far calmer story behind its own name, since it was christened in 1816 by the crew of a United States Navy schooner called the Washington, anchored there while waiting on other ships in its fleet. Every lighthouse, every shipwreck story, and every nervous captain's logbook entry from this stretch of water eventually traces back to the same narrow, churning gap of current that gave Door County the only name it has ever had. Modern car ferries crossing to Washington Island still throttle back and adjust their heading for that same current today, proof that engines and radar have tamed the strait without ever fully erasing its danger. Local dive clubs still search the strait's cold, clear water for undiscovered wreck sites, decades after the last major shipwreck here was ever recorded. Standing on the bluffs above Gills Rock on a windy afternoon, watching the current chop the surface into whitecaps even on an otherwise calm lake, it is not hard to understand why sailors gave this particular stretch of water the only name that ever stuck.

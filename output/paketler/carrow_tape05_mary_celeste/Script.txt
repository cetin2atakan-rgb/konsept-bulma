[BLOK4_DUVARDAKI_KARELER]
The first item in this folder is a deck sketch, made by the boarding officer within hours of coming aboard, because no photographer was available and he wanted a record before anything on deck was disturbed by his own crew's boots. It shows the rigging in a state he describes as "damaged but not disastrously so" — one halyard slack and trailing, a jib sail torn loose from its boom and hanging over the side where it had apparently been dragging in the water for some time, judging by the wear along its edge. The sketch is careful, precise, the hand of a man who understood that whatever he drew would likely be the only visual record anyone would ever have of the ship in that condition, and I have come to trust his draftsmanship the way I trust the captain's tape measure from the last tape — a professional doing his professional best under circumstances he had no training for.

The second item is the galley. The stove had been displaced from its mountings, knocked slightly out of true, but was not overturned and showed no sign of fire having spread from it, which the boarding officer notes specifically, because a displaced but contained stove suggested to him "a single hard knock, from a wave perhaps, rather than a prolonged violence." On a shelf nearby, undisturbed, sat a child's sewing — a half-finished seam, the needle still threaded, the kind of small domestic object that survives in a file like this one specifically because it is so ordinary that nobody thought to disturb it either at the time or in the century since.

The third item is the one I find hardest to describe without my voice doing something I don't like it doing, so I am going to describe it plainly and move past it quickly. A sounding rod — a simple length of wood or metal used to measure how much water has collected in a ship's hold — was found lying out on the deck, not stowed in its usual place, exactly where a man would leave it if he had just finished using it and meant to pick it back up again shortly afterward. Beside it, one of the ship's two pumps had been partially disassembled, its parts laid out in a rough line rather than scattered, the way a man lays out the parts of something he intends to put back together rather than the way parts end up when a ship is being abandoned in blind panic. I want to be plain about what this suggests to me: whoever left that sounding rod on the deck was in the middle of checking the hold for water, methodically, and did not finish the job.

The fourth item is the ship's log and its accompanying slate — a day-to-day working notation kept separately from the formal log, used for rougher daily entries before they were copied over properly. The formal log's final entry is dated some days before the ship was found, recording a position near an island in the Azores chain. The working slate, however, carries a later, rougher notation — unsigned, undated in any formal sense, giving a bearing and a brief phrase that the boarding officer transcribed exactly as he found it: "Monday 25th, at 8. Azores in sight." That is where the formal record ends. The ship, when found nine days later, had traveled roughly four hundred miles further along a course that, by the calculations of men far better at navigation than I am, does not comfortably match nine days of a rudderless hull simply drifting with wind and current. Something, for at least part of that gap, was still being sailed, even after whatever happened to the people meant to be sailing her.

The fifth and sixth items I want to take together, because they only make sense side by side. A search of the cargo hold found nine of the vessel's barrels of industrial alcohol — part of a much larger cargo of over seventeen hundred barrels — empty, or nearly so, their red oak staves slightly sprung, as though the wood itself had swollen and then released pressure built up from within. The sixth item is the yawl, the ship's small lifeboat, which was missing entirely, along with a section of railing near where it had been stowed, removed rather than broken, in a manner the boarding officer's report describes as deliberate rather than accidental — unbolted, not torn away by a wave. Ten people, a small boat, a deliberately cleared path to launch it, and a sea that, by every weather record available for that date and location, was calm. I want to repeat that last word, because it is the hinge this entire file turns on. Calm. Not a storm that could have swamped a crowded yawl in minutes. Not a squall that would at least give this office a tidy, ordinary tragedy to file and close. Calm water, under a clear sky, and ten people who stepped, deliberately by every physical indication in this folder, into a small boat and were never seen again by anyone, anywhere, in the hundred and five years since.

The seventh item is the one that stayed sealed for half a century before anyone opened it, and the one I am going to describe last, carefully, because I think it deserves more weight than the order I am giving it tonight would suggest. Tucked inside the spine of a family Bible recovered from the captain's quarters, undamaged, was a small sealed envelope, in the captain's own hand on the outside, reading only: "To be opened if we are found." The boarding crew did not open it. It passed, with the ship, through salvage proceedings, through the insurers' own enquiries, through more than one set of hands over the following decades, unopened, until it reached this office's predecessor sometime in the nineteen-twenties, who did, finally, open it, and placed a typed transcription of its contents in this folder rather than the fragile original. The transcription reads, in full, five words: "We are between. Forgive us." Nothing else. No signature. The hand matching, as closely as a later examiner could determine without the original for direct comparison, the same captain's hand that wrote the ten days' worth of log entries preceding it.

I want to add one more detail about the envelope, because it is the part of this item that I have never been able to stop turning over. The outer fold, according to the nineteen-twenties transcription note, showed signs of having been sealed and then reopened and resealed at least once before the ship was ever found — a second wax impression laid slightly askew over the first, as though the captain had written the letter, thought better of it, opened it again to add or reconsider something, and then sealed it a second time before whatever happened to him happened. The note does not speculate about what might have been added or removed in that interval. I have speculated about it more nights than I am willing to admit to on this tape, and I have never arrived anywhere I trust enough to write down as more than a guess.

There is also a smaller item, easy to overlook beside everything else, that I want to give its own moment: a child's rag doll, found tucked into a berth in the captain's quarters, undamaged, dry, and, according to the boarding officer's own small note appended almost as an aside, "set upright against the pillow, as though placed there deliberately rather than left where it fell." A doll a two-year-old had been playing with does not, in my experience of small children, usually end up sitting upright and composed. It ends up wherever the child dropped it. Someone, in the last ordered moments aboard that ship, took the time to set a child's doll upright before whatever came next.

I want to say something about the men who found her, because this file treats them more harshly in its early pages than I think they deserved. A salvage inquiry convened at Gibraltar shortly after the discovery spent considerable time suggesting, without ever quite proving, that the finding crew themselves might have had a hand in the disappearance, hoping to profit from the salvage award a derelict, intact vessel commands under maritime law. I looked into what became of him afterward, out of a curiosity I have developed, across five tapes now, about the men on the honest side of these files. [BLOK6_KISA_SAPMA_EMBELLISHMENT]
A short digression, because I think this file has suffered more public damage from one particular source than any other file in this drawer, and I think the damage itself is worth examining.

A little over a decade after this ship was found, a popular magazine published a sensational first-person account, written anonymously but widely believed even at the time to be the work of a well-known author of fiction, dramatizing the discovery with details that never appeared in any official record — a breakfast still warm on the table, a cat asleep in the galley, the ship's boat found perfectly intact and in its davits. None of those details appear anywhere in the actual boarding officer's sketch, the actual deposition, or the actual salvage inquiry that this office's file preserves. There was a sword aboard, and the Gibraltar inquiry did suspect blood on it for a while, but when the stains were analyzed they were not blood at all. The breakfast is never mentioned by a single primary witness. The lifeboat, as I have already told you, was missing entirely, not intact and ready.

I raise this not to accuse the author of anything worse than wanting to sell magazines, which is a fairly ordinary ambition and not, on its own, a sinister one. I raise it because that single piece of popular fiction has been repeated as established fact in nearly every retelling of this case for the better part of a century, to the point that most people who think they know this story know, almost entirely, a story that was invented for entertainment rather than the considerably stranger and better-documented story this office's own file actually contains. I think this is worth sitting with for a moment on its own terms, separate from anything else in this folder: sometimes the truth does not need embellishing to be strange, and the embellishment, once it takes hold, makes the real file harder for anyone afterward to take seriously on its own quieter merits.

The more serious, non-fictional theory that has followed this case concerns the cargo itself. Industrial alcohol, under the right conditions, can release fumes that build pressure inside a sealed hold, and a sudden release — through a jarred hatch, or the kind of hard knock the boarding officer noted in the galley — could plausibly have produced a frightening sound, or even a visible vapor, convincing an experienced captain that an explosion was imminent, enough to order everyone into the yawl as a precaution, intending to stay close by until the danger passed, only for a shift in weather or current to separate the small boat from the larger ship before anyone could row back. I find this theory more persuasive than almost any other I have encountered in this drawer, because it requires no extraordinary cause at all, only an ordinary and understandable misjudgment made quickly, by a careful man, under circumstances that gave him very little time to be anything other than cautious.

I want to add a detail that supports the fume theory more directly, because I think a fair accounting of this file requires giving its strongest ordinary explanation every advantage it is owed. A later survey of the cargo hold, conducted once the ship reached port under new management, found that the nine affected barrels were, without exception, made of red oak rather than the white oak used for the remaining casks — red oak being, as any cooper of the period could have told the captain had he thought to ask one before loading, more porous and considerably more prone to seepage when carrying a cargo as volatile as industrial alcohol. If nine barrels built from the wrong wood had been seeping fumes into a sealed hold through the rough weather of the days before, a cautious captain would have had every reason in the world to fear the worst, and no way at the time to know that the worst, in that particular case, was not actually imminent.

[BLOK7_NE_OLDUKLARI]
One opinion per file. I want to give you two readings tonight instead of one, because I do not think this file resolves as cleanly as the others, and I would be lying to you if I pretended otherwise.

The first reading is the ordinary one, and I believe a good portion of it: a genuine, well-founded fear of the cargo, a deliberate and orderly evacuation into the yawl, a sounding rod left mid-use because the captain decided the water in the hold no longer mattered next to the greater danger of fumes and fire, and a tragic separation at sea that cost ten people their lives in a small open boat, their remains never recovered because the ocean rarely returns what it takes once a calm day turns, as North Atlantic weather in December always eventually does.

The second reading is the one I cannot set down even though the first one explains most of the physical evidence adequately on its own. It is five words in a dead man's own hand, sealed and kept unopened for half a century by men who had every reason to read it immediately and chose, each of them in turn, not to. "We are between." I do not know what a careful, methodical New England sea captain, a man whose log entries read like the captain's log entries I have shown you from other ships in other decades, meant by that phrase, in the last document he is known to have written, addressed to whoever might one day read it. I do not think it was written by a frightened man in a panic, because panicked men do not seal letters and tuck them carefully into the spine of a family Bible. I think it was written by a man who believed, calmly and with some care for whoever found it, that he understood exactly what was happening to him and his family, and chose five words instead of an explanation, either because the explanation would not have been believed, or because there was no more of it that language was equipped to carry.

[BLOK9_INSANLAR]
I want to tell you about the people on that ship, because I have spent most of this tape on the ship itself and they deserve better than being an afterthought to their own file.

The captain was thirty-seven years old, an experienced and by every account well-regarded master, sailing with his wife and their two-year-old daughter aboard, a common enough practice for a captain's family in that era and trade. A second child, an older son, had been left at home with relatives for his schooling, a detail noted in this file almost in passing that I have not been able to stop thinking about since I first read it — a family divided by ordinary necessity days before the family that sailed vanished entirely, leaving the one child ashore to grow up without any of them, and without ever being given an answer better than the one this file still cannot give me.

The crew numbered seven, drawn from more than one nationality, experienced sailors by every account available, none of them, as far as any inquiry ever determined, carrying a grievance against the captain or each other serious enough to explain a mutiny, and none of them, crucially, ever found — not a body, not a piece of clothing, not a single physical trace beyond the ship itself and the small items left aboard her. The son left ashore grew to adulthood, married, and according to a brief genealogical note added to this file decades later by someone tracing the family for entirely unrelated reasons, kept a single photograph of his parents on his own mantelpiece for the rest of his life, and reportedly declined, more than once, to discuss his family's disappearance with anyone outside his own household, in terms that the note describes, secondhand, as "final, and not unkind, but final."

I want to mention, too, the man who commanded the ship that found her, because I went looking in his later years for the pattern I keep finding in these files — the men closest to them dying young, of causes that are individually unremarkable and collectively harder to dismiss than I would like. It is not there. He lived past sixty-five, and his death has no connection to this case that any inquiry at the time or since has ever established.

I include that because I almost did not. I had half written the pattern into my notes before I checked the dates, and that is exactly how a man in my position starts reading patterns where there are none. Sailors in that period did die young at rates that would shock a modern listener regardless of any file in this drawer, from fevers, from storms, from accidents an actuary of the time would have considered entirely ordinary. I offer it instead the way I have tried to offer everything else tonight — honestly, with the plain explanation given its full weight, and the uneasy one set down beside it rather than in place of it, because I think a man in my position owes you both, every time, and lets you decide for yourself which one to carry home.

Welcome. Tonight we drift through the English canals at night, aboard an old painted narrowboat, on dark and quiet water. There is nowhere you need to be and nothing you need to do. The journey is slow, and it is long, and it belongs entirely to you.

Find the softest place where you are. Let the pillow take the weight of your head, and let the blanket settle over your shoulders. If the room is dim, leave it dim. If there is a little sound outside, let it become part of the evening.

Our story moves at the pace of a quiet walk. A boat, a lantern, some water, a few gates of old timber, and a long countryside that has already grown still. Nothing is waiting at the end of it. There is only the next slow stretch of canal.

So let your breathing be easy, and let your shoulders be loose. Let the day stay where it is, a little way back along the path. Ahead of you, in the last light of an autumn evening, there is a towpath, and a boat tied beside it.

The light is nearly gone. The sky above the fields is the color of pale tea, fading at the edges to a soft gray-blue, and the first dark is gathering low under the hedges. It is a mild evening, with no wind at all, and the air feels gentle on your face.

You are walking along the towpath. The ground under your boots is packed earth, smooth and a little damp, with tufts of wet grass at the margins. Each step makes a soft sound, and the sound is swallowed at once by the quiet fields on either side.

There is a smell of fallen leaves in the air, and of cool water, and of turned earth. Somewhere beyond the hedge, a last bird calls once and is still. The whole countryside seems to be gathering itself in, like a hand slowly closing around something warm.

Beside the path lies the canal. It is narrow, and perfectly still, and it holds the evening sky upon its surface like a long dark mirror. The reeds along the edge stand without moving, and each one has a thin twin reflected in the water below.

A little way ahead, a shape rests against the bank. As you come closer, it becomes a boat, long and narrow and low, resting on the water as lightly as a leaf. Its paint is faded to soft reds and greens, and in the dusk it looks almost like part of the bank.

It is tied at the front and at the back with thick ropes, which run up to short iron rings set in the edge of the towpath. The ropes are damp and slightly slack. As the boat shifts a little on the water, the nearest rope gives a long, low creak, like a door in an old house.

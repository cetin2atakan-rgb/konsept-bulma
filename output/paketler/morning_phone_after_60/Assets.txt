## Description
Do you reach for your phone before your feet touch the floor? Here are 5 versions of that morning habit, ranked from mild to the one sequence most worth changing after 60, plus two simple fixes.
Doctor Marcus Reid is an educational narrator persona created for this channel, not a real physician. This video is general health education, not medical advice.

We go through glancing at the time, checking a screen inches from your face while lying flat, opening email and news first thing, stacking all of it on short sleep, and the pattern of checking your phone and then dozing back off. Along the way: sleep inertia, the cortisol awakening response, how screen light reaches your internal clock, and what large observational studies have found about short and fragmented sleep and later dementia risk, including what those studies do and do not prove.

Then two protective changes that take less than ten minutes: the ten-minute buffer and getting real room light or daylight first, plus three small tools (move the charger, a one-word morning note, an analog alarm clock).

How many minutes after waking do you usually pick up your phone: zero, five, fifteen, or longer? Tell me in the comments.

Research referenced:

• Tassi P, Muzet A. "Sleep inertia." Sleep Med Rev. 2000.
• Clow A et al. "The cortisol awakening response: more than a measure of HPA axis function." Neurosci Biobehav Rev. 2010.
• Chang AM et al. "Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness." PNAS. 2015.
• Lim ASP et al. "Sleep fragmentation and the risk of incident Alzheimer's disease and cognitive decline in older persons." Sleep. 2013.
• Sabia S et al. "Association of sleep duration in middle and old age with incidence of dementia." Nat Commun. 2021.
• National Institute on Aging: sleep and cognitive health materials.

The studies on sleep duration and fragmentation are observational: they show associations, not proof that any morning habit causes memory loss. No study has tested morning phone use and dementia directly. If you have memory concerns beyond ordinary forgetfulness, or sleep problems that persist, talk to your own doctor.

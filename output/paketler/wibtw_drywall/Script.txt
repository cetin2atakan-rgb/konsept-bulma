## TITLE: Why Are American Homes Built With Drywall Instead of Brick?

[1. HOOK]

If somebody knocks a knuckle against the wall of a typical American home built after nineteen fifty, the sound comes back hollow, and underneath the paint there is a sheet of gray board roughly half an inch thick, screwed into a wooden stud every sixteen inches. That one material, paper wrapped around a core of a soft white mineral, covers the overwhelming majority of new interior walls across the United States. A crew of two people can close up an entire bedroom in a single afternoon. Travel to a home in Germany, the United Kingdom, or France, and a knock on that same interior wall often comes back solid, because the wall itself is brick, block, or a layer of real wet plaster troweled onto a masonry surface. Same job, a wall that separates one room from another, built from two completely different traditions, on two sides of the same ocean.

[2. THE QUESTION]

So why did American homes end up wrapped in paper and gypsum, while so much of Europe kept building walls out of brick, block, and wet plaster? The easy answer, the one most people reach for first, is that American construction is simply cheaper and flimsier, that builders in the United States chose the lazy option and called it innovation. That assumption does not match the engineering record either, since a modern wood-framed house built to current code can be engineered to withstand hurricane-force wind and seismic shaking as reliably as a masonry one, provided it is anchored, braced, and fastened correctly; the wall covering on the inside has very little to do with how strong the structure underneath actually is. That answer feels satisfying, but it does not hold up once the actual history gets examined, because the switch to drywall in America was not laziness. It was a specific set of economic pressures, a patent, a company, and a global war that all happened to land on the same side of the Atlantic. The sequence starts, strangely enough, not with a crisis, but with one civil engineer trying to solve a much smaller problem in the eighteen nineties.

Before any of that, it helps to understand what American walls looked like before this material existed at all. For roughly two and a half centuries, American interior walls were built the English way: thin strips of wood called lath nailed across the studs, then two or three coats of wet plaster troweled over the lath by hand, left to cure for days between coats. It was the same wet-trade tradition masons and plasterers had carried across the ocean from Britain, and it required real skill, real time, and a lot of water. [LABEL: lath and plaster]

[3. HISTORY]

The story of how that changed begins with a man named Augustine Sackett. Sackett was a civil engineer and a graduate of Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute in Troy, New York, and by the late eighteen eighties he was experimenting with a cheaper way to make interior walls than traditional lath and plaster. Working alongside fellow Rensselaer graduate Fred Kane, Sackett tried pressing thin layers of straw, paper, and tar into a rigid board. The early versions caught fire too easily to be practical, so the two men shifted to a different core: plaster, the same gypsum-based material used in wet plastering, pressed between layers of heavy paper. The paper gave the board tensile strength so it would not crack when nailed, and the gypsum core gave it the fire resistance the straw version lacked. Sackett filed for a patent on this board around eighteen ninety, and on May twenty-second, eighteen ninety-four, the United States granted him Patent Number five hundred twenty thousand one hundred twenty-three, titled Inside Wall Covering. That patent is the document historians point to as the real birth certificate of what is now called drywall. [LABEL: gypsum core]

Gypsum itself is calcium sulfate that has naturally absorbed water into its crystal structure. Manufacturers crush it, heat it in a process called calcining to drive off most of that bound water, then mix the resulting powder with fresh water so it can be poured flat and set between two sheets of heavy paper. The same chemistry that let Sackett cast his board into a flat panel is what later gave the material its most useful accidental property, since the water driven out during calcining gets chemically rebound once the board hardens, ready to be released again, slowly, as steam, if the wall is ever heated by fire. [LABEL: calcining]

Sackett's invention did not immediately take over the country. He built a handful of plants to manufacture what came to be called Sackett board, but for its first two decades the product stayed a minor curiosity rather than a standard building material. Meanwhile, a separate but related shift was already reshaping American construction in a different way: how the wooden skeleton behind the wall itself got built. Historians trace a method called balloon framing to Chicago in the eighteen thirties, with Augustine Taylor's construction of Saint Mary's Church in eighteen thirty-three often cited as an early example, though the Chicago History Museum and other researchers note that the attribution is genuinely disputed between Taylor and a builder named George Washington Snow, and that similar light-frame techniques likely existed elsewhere before either man. What is not disputed is why the method mattered: instead of joining massive hand-hewn timbers with hand-carved mortise and tenon joints, the way European and early American heavy-timber construction had always worked, balloon framing used thin, uniform two-by-four studs held together with cheap, machine-made nails, which only became affordable once American sawmills and nail factories scaled up around the same period. Contemporary carpenters mocked the method, insisting a church framed this way would blow away in a strong wind, which is reportedly where the nickname balloon frame came from. [LABEL: balloon framing] [LABEL: mortise and tenon]

The nickname stuck for a second reason beyond the mockery: the open stud cavities in a balloon-framed wall ran uninterrupted from the foundation to the roofline, so a fire that started low in the wall could travel upward almost like a chimney. By the early twentieth century, builders had largely replaced balloon framing with a safer variant called platform framing, which capped each floor with its own horizontal plate before the next story went up, cutting off that chimney effect. Both systems kept the sixteen-inch spacing between studs that carpenters had long favored, partly because a standard four-foot strip of lath spanned it neatly, and gypsum board manufacturers would later size their four-foot-wide panels around that same module. [LABEL: platform framing]

The two threads, cheap wood framing and Sackett's gypsum board, did not fully connect until a company stepped in to scale production. Around the turn of the century, roughly thirty independent gypsum and plaster companies merged to form the Chicago-based United States Gypsum Company, known today as USG, so that a single large company now controlled much of the country's gypsum mining and processing capacity. The popular story often told in building-trade circles is that USG invented drywall outright. The records show something different: USG did not invent the product, it bought it. In nineteen oh nine, under the leadership of company president Sewell Avery, who had taken the role in nineteen oh five and would run USG for decades, the company purchased the Sackett Plaster Board Company and folded Sackett's patent and manufacturing know-how into its own operation. In the mid-nineteen tens, USG improved the board into a single-layer panel and sold it under the name Sheetrock, a brand name so dominant it eventually became a generic term for drywall itself the way some people say the brand name for a bandage. [LABEL: Sheetrock] USG's own retrospectives date the first sales of that style of wallboard to nineteen sixteen and seventeen, marketed at the time mainly as a fireproof option for temporary and institutional buildings rather than as a wholesale replacement for plaster in a family home, which is part of why the slow quarter-century of acceptance that followed should be read less as a failure of the product and more as a narrow initial sales pitch that only widened once wartime necessity forced the issue.

Even with a major manufacturer behind it, gypsum board spent roughly a quarter century as the overlooked option. Industry retrospectives describe builders treating it as a cut-rate substitute for real plaster, and homebuyers in the nineteen tens, twenties, and thirties largely agreed, seeing a plastered wall as the mark of a properly built, respectable home, and a gypsum-board wall as something used in cheap housing or temporary structures. That perception held for decades, through the nineteen twenties housing boom and into the Depression, when slow economic activity in home construction gave builders little reason to change proven methods. [LABEL: plasterer]

The real break came with the Second World War. Skilled plasterers and finish carpenters, along with a huge share of the general workforce, were drafted or shifted into war production, and the trade press of the era describes acute shortages of both labor and shipping capacity, including shortages of the shipping that carried gypsum rock from Nova Scotia quarries to American plants. Plastering a room the traditional way required a skilled tradesman and days of drying time between coats; it was a trade that simply could not be staffed at wartime speed. Builders who needed to finish housing for war workers and military families turned to gypsum board because two moderately trained workers could hang and finish a room in a fraction of the time a plasterer needed, and because it used less of the skilled labor the war had pulled away. When the war ended in nineteen forty-five, the shortage did not disappear, it got worse in a different way: millions of veterans came home at once, all needing housing immediately, feeding the suburban building boom of the late nineteen forties and nineteen fifties. Builders who had spent the war years hanging gypsum board had no appetite to go back to the slower, costlier wet trade, and an entire generation of new homes, including the famous mass-produced subdivisions built for returning veterans, went up with drywall as the default rather than the exception. The most visible proof of that shift sits on Long Island, New York, in the development called Levittown, where builder William Levitt used assembly-line construction methods, with specialized crews moving from house to house, each repeating a single task, to erect thousands of near-identical homes financed through Federal Housing Administration and Veterans Administration loans between nineteen forty-seven and the early nineteen fifties. [LABEL: Levittown]

[4. THE REAL REASON]

Strip away the individual steps and the mystery resolves into something far less dramatic than a grand design: it comes down to the economics of labor, time, and lumber. The real reason drywall beat plaster in America has very little to do with one material being inherently better and almost everything to do with which material could be installed faster, by fewer skilled hands, on top of a wall system the country had already standardized on for other reasons. Wet plaster is, by nature, a slow, skill-dependent process. A plasterer apprenticed for years to learn how to trowel multiple coats evenly, and each coat needed real drying time before the next one went on, which meant a plastered room tied up a skilled worker and a building site for days. A sheet of gypsum board, by contrast, could be nailed or screwed to the studs by two people with modest training in an afternoon, with only the seams needing a thin coat of joint compound and a few days to finish. Multiply that time difference across millions of postwar houses and the savings in labor hours become enormous, which is the blunt commercial reason drywall won once wartime shortages forced the comparison into the open. [LABEL: joint compound]

That labor advantage only mattered as much as it did because of a second, quieter factor: the wood-frame wall drywall was designed to cover was already the default structure almost everywhere in the country, for reasons that trace straight back to the same balloon-framing shift discussed earlier. The United States had, and still has, enormous forested land relative to its population, which made softwood lumber abundant and historically cheap compared to fired brick or quarried stone, especially across regions far from traditional clay or stone deposits. European nations, by contrast, had spent centuries clearing forests for shipbuilding, heating, and earlier construction booms, leaving stone, fired brick, and later concrete block as the more economical structural materials in much of the continent, alongside building traditions, often reinforced by local codes after urban fires, that favored masonry walls for their fire resistance and durability in dense city centers. Gypsum board is, fundamentally, a wood-frame-compatible product: it needs studs spaced at a regular interval to screw into, and it works best as an interior skin over a light timber skeleton, not as a free-standing structural wall the way brick or block can be. Because the United States had already committed to cheap, light wood framing for its exterior walls for its own economic reasons, the interior finish that worked fastest and cheapest with that same wood framing had a running head start that brick and plaster, tied to a masonry tradition the country had mostly moved away from, never fully recovered from. [LABEL: wet trade]

Fire codes added one more practical push rather than a barrier. Gypsum itself contains chemically bound water that releases slowly as steam when heated, which is part of why the material resists fire better than its paper facing would suggest, and by the mid-twentieth century a thicker version formulated for fire resistance, eventually sold as Type X board, could earn a documented one-hour fire rating in standardized testing. That meant builders could satisfy increasingly strict fire-separation requirements, between a garage and a house or between multifamily units, with a wood-framed wall and the right drywall, instead of being forced into a masonry wall to meet the same code.

There is a secondary economic reason tied to the same wood-frame wall. A hollow stud cavity behind a sheet of drywall can be filled with a fiberglass or cellulose insulation batt, giving a wood-framed house a continuous layer of thermal insulation that a solid masonry wall, by its nature, does not have room for without bolting on an entirely separate furring and insulation system. [LABEL: insulation batt] As postwar suburban houses spread farther from city centers and needed to be heated through cold winters and, later, air-conditioned through hot summers as cheaply as possible, that built-in insulation cavity made the wood-frame-and-drywall combination cheaper to run as well as cheaper to build in the first place, reinforcing the same choice from yet another direction. The cheaper structural system and the cheaper interior finish reinforced each other, and that mutual reinforcement, not some claim that gypsum board was simply the superior invention, is the unglamorous core of the real reason.

[5. WHAT IF]

Picture a different fork in that same history: suppose the wartime labor shortage of the nineteen forties had hit differently, or suppose the United States had entered its postwar building boom with the same depleted timber reserves much of rebuilding Europe faced after two world wars stripped forests for fuel and construction. In that version of events, American builders might have leaned harder into concrete block and fired brick the way many European countries did during their own reconstruction, and gypsum board might have stayed the niche, slightly downmarket product it was treated as through the nineteen twenties and thirties, confined mostly to temporary housing and cheap partition walls rather than becoming the national standard. It is a plausible counterfactual precisely because the postwar decisions on both continents were driven less by which material was objectively best and more by which raw materials and labor pools happened to be available at the moment millions of housing units needed to go up at once. A real-world parallel sits on the other side of that same ocean: West Germany's own postwar reconstruction, working with timber reserves that had been stripped for wartime fuel and facing its own acute housing shortage, leaned heavily on brick and masonry to rebuild quickly, while East Germany went further still with factory-made precast concrete panels, arriving at a mass-production answer to a nearly identical housing emergency that ended up looking almost nothing like Levittown's wood-and-drywall suburbs.

That counterfactual points toward a fair criticism of the path actually taken, because drywall's dominance comes with real, well-documented tradeoffs rather than being an unambiguous upgrade. A gypsum-and-wood-frame wall transmits sound far more easily than a dense masonry wall, which is part of why older brick apartment buildings often feel quieter between units than newer wood-framed construction, and acoustic engineers routinely have to add extra insulation and specialized panels to wood-framed walls to approach what solid masonry does without any added material at all. [LABEL: acoustic insulation] Drywall also dents, chips, and takes on water damage far more readily than brick or block, meaning a flooded wood-framed wall section often needs to be cut out and replaced entirely, where a masonry wall might simply be dried and repainted. In regions that face hurricanes or wildfires, masonry and concrete construction can outperform wood framing clad in gypsum board in ways that matter for insurance rates and survivability, which helps explain why concrete block still dominates house construction in South Florida, where hurricane codes and local supply tilted the economics the other way. Insurers in hurricane-prone states price construction type into their premiums and offer discounts for wind-resistant features, a small but telling sign that this tradeoff is still being priced today, not just argued about in hindsight.

[6. TODAY]

All of that history is still sitting inside the walls of almost any American home built in the last seventy years, and a few simple observations at home reveal it directly. A firm knuckle-knock between the vertical wooden studs, typically every sixteen inches, produces a hollow, almost drum-like sound, while a knock directly over a stud sounds noticeably more solid, because that is where the screws anchoring the board actually bite into wood. In raking light from a lamp held sideways against the wall, the faint seams between sheets of drywall, and the slightly raised ridges where joint compound was feathered over them, often become visible even through paint, a quiet signature of exactly where each four-by-eight or four-by-twelve sheet was hung. Walls separating a garage from living space, or walls between apartments in multifamily buildings, are frequently built from the thicker, denser Type X board specifically because local fire codes still require that one-hour rating a plain wall would not meet, so a homeowner drilling into that particular wall will often find it noticeably heavier and harder than drywall elsewhere in the same house. Bathrooms and other damp rooms commonly use a greenish-tinted, moisture-resistant version of the same board behind tile, built on the same lesson as fire-rated board: changing what goes into the paper and the core changes what the finished wall can survive. [LABEL: Type X] [LABEL: stud] [LABEL: moisture-resistant board]

Pulling off a switch plate or an outlet cover reveals another clue hiding in plain sight. The drywall screws holding the board to each stud sit in a shallow dimple, recessed just below the paper surface so the screw head can be buried under a thin layer of joint compound without a bump showing through the paint, and finishers refer to that spacing as the screw pattern, which loosely traces the same sixteen-inch stud layout carpenters settled on generations earlier. [LABEL: screw pattern] On an exterior wall, pulling back a baseboard sometimes reveals a thin plastic sheet, a vapor barrier, stapled to the studs just behind the drywall to control the moisture that would otherwise condense inside the wall cavity, one more layer added to the original Sackett system as building science matured over the following century. [LABEL: vapor barrier]

Even the tools of the trade carry the history forward. The metal corner bead protecting the outside edge of a wall, the mesh tape bridging every seam, and the thin skim coat sanded smooth before paint are all direct descendants of the two-person, fast-finish system that beat out hand-troweled plaster during the labor shortages of the nineteen forties, still being used by crews today for the same reason: it remains faster and cheaper than the alternative it replaced. [LABEL: corner bead] [LABEL: mesh tape]

[7. CLOSE]

Once that sixteen-inch module is visible, it turns up all over the house. A standard sheet of plywood is four feet by eight feet, three stud bays wide and six tall. Fiberglass insulation batts are sold fifteen inches wide, exactly the gap between two studs set sixteen inches apart, and twenty-three inches wide for walls framed at twenty-four. Even the four-by-eight sheet of drywall leaning against the wall at the hardware store is cut to land its edges on a stud without a single trim cut. None of those sizes were chosen on their own; each one was fitted to a wall skeleton that was already standing. [LABEL: plywood sheet] [LABEL: insulation batt width]

American walls are gypsum and paper instead of brick and mortar because an eighteen nineties patent, an abundance of cheap lumber, and a labor shortage during one war all lined up, and every product that followed was built to fit the result. Switching back would not mean changing one material. It would mean changing all of them at once.

[1. HOOK + PERSONA INTRO]

The Driftless Area is hiding far more than quiet dairy farms and switchback roads through the hills. Underneath this corner of the Midwest sits a crater punched into the bedrock by a meteor nearly five hundred million years ago, still invisible from the surface. Hidden in its coldest limestone hollows are snails that have not changed since the last ice age was still gripping half the continent. Carved into its bluffs are bird shaped and bear shaped mounds built by hands no one living can name, and tucked into one quiet valley is the very farm country where the United States first learned how to stop its own soil from washing into the river. This entire region sits inside a doughnut hole that the great ice sheets kept missing, advance after advance, leaving behind a landscape that looks nothing like the flat Midwest around it. Instead of flat farmland stretching to the horizon, you get ridges and hollows, limestone caves, and rivers so crooked that one of them is sometimes called the most crooked river in the entire country. Today we will count down twenty-five remarkable geographic facts about the Driftless Area. I'm Walter Hale. I taught geography for thirty-eight years in a small high school in Kentucky, and I still can't drive through a ridge-and-valley county without wondering why it looks the way it does. Let's start at number twenty-five.

[FACT 25: Trempealeau Mountain, the Island That Isn't an Island | 44.0061,-91.4432]

Fact twenty-five. Just north of La Crosse, Wisconsin, a lone rock hill rises straight out of the Mississippi River, and it is one of only three true rock islands on the entire length of that river. It is called Trempealeau Mountain, and it stands about four hundred twenty-five feet, or roughly one hundred thirty meters, above the water. French voyageurs paddling past it centuries ago gave it the name "la montagne qui trempe à l'eau," meaning the mountain that is soaked in the water, and that French phrase is where the county, the river, and the nearby village all got their names. Long before the French arrived, the Ho-Chunk called it a soaking mountain, the Dakota described it as a mountain separated by water, and the Ojibwe knew it as a bluff standing in the water. Three different nations, looking at the same strange hill, all landed on the same idea. The reason it looks separated at all comes back to the Driftless Area's unusual history. Ice age floods, swollen with glacial meltwater from the edges of this unglaciated zone, cut a wide gorge through the sandstone and left this one chunk of bedrock stranded in the channel while the softer rock around it washed away. Today the mountain sits inside Perrot State Park, and visitors can hike a trail along its base, though the steep sides keep most people from climbing all the way to the flat summit. I used to put a map of the upper Mississippi on my classroom wall and ask my students to find the one bump in an otherwise flat blue line, and almost nobody ever guessed it was a mountain sitting in the middle of a river. If you drive the Great River Road north out of La Crosse on Wisconsin Highway 35, you will see Trempealeau Mountain rising on your left, a lone green hill standing out in the river, apart from the bluff lines on either side of the valley.

[FACT 24: A Cave Found by Falling Pigs | 43.5544,-91.4286]

Fact twenty-four. Just across the Minnesota line from the Driftless hill country of Wisconsin and Iowa, near the small town of Harmony, there is a limestone cave that stays a constant forty-eight degrees year round, no matter what the weather is doing outside. It is called Niagara Cave, and locals say it was discovered in 1924 when a farmer's pigs went missing and were eventually found trapped at the bottom of a sinkhole that opened into the cave below. Tours began a decade later, in 1934, and visitors today walk past towering limestone passageways, ancient marine fossils left behind when this entire region was covered by a shallow tropical sea hundreds of millions of years ago, and a sixty foot underground waterfall deep inside the rock. The cave sits in Fillmore County, Minnesota, in an area so riddled with sinkholes and underground drainage that local geologists call it karst topography, and the same limestone bedrock that makes the Driftless Area's soil so good for farming is exactly what makes it so easy for water to dissolve passages like this one underground. Because the region was never buried under glacial ice and never had its cracks and crevices sealed shut with glacial clay, rainwater here still finds its way down through the rock the same way it has for thousands of years, carving caves instead of simply running off into lakes the way it does across most of the glaciated Midwest. A new trail finished in recent years now connects Niagara Cave to the town of Harmony and continues on toward the Iowa border, giving hikers an aboveground way to trace the same limestone country the water has been carving from below.

[FACT 23: An Underground River You Can Ride a Boat Through | 43.1544,-91.3310]

Fact twenty-three. A few miles west of McGregor, Iowa, there is a cave you cannot walk through, because most of it is full of water. It is called Spook Cave, and it was discovered in 1953 when a local man investigating a strange underground noise found water running through the rock, and by 1955 the family that found it was running flat bottomed boat tours straight into the hill. Visitors climb into a boat, duck under a low stone ceiling, and float along an underground stream that eventually drains into Bloody Run Creek, a cold tributary that empties into the upper Mississippi. A small lock and dam keeps the water inside the cave at a boatable depth through the summer season, though the stream drops to a shallow trickle come winter. In most of the country, caves are something you walk through with a flashlight and a jacket, but in the Driftless Area you can sometimes paddle through one, because the region's limestone is so thoroughly hollowed out by thousands of years of unblocked groundwater flow. That groundwater is the same cold, mineral rich water that feeds the trout streams this region is famous for, which means the cave and the fishing creek a few miles away are, in a very real sense, the same water system, just visited through two different doors. Clayton County, Iowa, where Spook Cave sits, has more mapped sinkholes and karst features than almost any county in the state, a direct result of ice sheet after ice sheet sliding past this landscape without burying it.

[FACT 22: A Six Hundred Foot Bluff with a View of Three States | 43.8058,-91.2309]

Fact twenty-two. Rising straight up from the eastern edge of La Crosse, Wisconsin, is Grandad Bluff, a limestone overlook that climbs roughly six hundred feet, or about one hundred eighty meters, above the city streets below. From the top, on a clear day, you can see into three different states at once: Wisconsin at your feet, Minnesota across the river to the west, and Iowa's bluffs rising to the southwest where the Mississippi bends out of sight. The bluff became a city park in the early twentieth century, and today visitors drive up a narrow road called Bliss Road, too tight for large vehicles, to reach a summit with coin operated binoculars and roughly five miles of hiking trails. Wisconsin Trails readers have voted the view from Grandad Bluff the most scenic in the entire state more than once, and on a busy weekend the overlook can draw thousands of visitors watching barges work their way up and down the river far below. What makes the bluff remarkable from a geography standpoint is not just its height, but the fact that it exists at all in its current sharp edged form. Across most of the glaciated Midwest, hills this old would have been ground down and rounded off by passing ice sheets thousands of years ago. Because the Driftless Area was repeatedly skipped by advancing glaciers, La Crosse's bluffs kept their steep, almost cliff like faces instead of being smoothed into gentle slopes, which is part of why the three state view from the top feels less like a Midwestern hill and more like something out of the Appalachians.

[FACT 21: The Highest Bluff on the Entire Mississippi River | 43.0144,-91.1846]

Fact twenty-one. Just south of McGregor, Iowa, Pikes Peak State Park sits on a bluff that is widely described as the highest point directly overlooking the Mississippi River anywhere along its entire length. The bluff itself rises about five hundred feet, or roughly one hundred fifty meters, above the water, and its summit stands about eleven hundred thirty feet above sea level. The park takes its name from the same explorer honored by the famous Colorado mountain, Zebulon Pike, who passed through the area in 1805 while surveying the upper Mississippi for the United States government, decades before Iowa was a state at all. From Point Ann, one of the park's main overlooks, visitors can look south and watch the Wisconsin River pour into the Mississippi at Wyalusing on the opposite bank, two of the Midwest's largest rivers joining in full view of a single overlook. The bluff's height is not an accident of one single hill, but a product of the whole region's unusual relationship with the Mississippi. Because this stretch of river has had tens of thousands of years to cut down through layer after layer of ancient limestone and sandstone without a glacier ever filling the valley back in with debris, the bluffs along it have grown taller and steeper over time instead of being buried and reset the way river valleys are in glaciated country. Clayton County, Iowa, where the park sits, advertises itself as home to more such bluffs than almost anywhere else along the entire Mississippi corridor.

[FACT 20: Where Two Great Rivers Meet | 43.0036,-91.1104]

Fact twenty. At Wyalusing State Park, near the small town of Bagley, Wisconsin, the Wisconsin River finally ends its roughly four hundred thirty mile run across the state by pouring into the Mississippi River, and the park's bluffs put visitors directly above the spot where it happens. The main overlook, called Point Lookout, sits roughly between two ridges known as Wisconsin Ridge and Sentinel Ridge, and from either one hikers can watch the greenish water of the Wisconsin River mix slowly with the Mississippi's broader, muddier current below. The two rivers do not blend instantly. For a stretch downstream, you can often still see a visible line between the clearer water coming off the Wisconsin and the wider Mississippi current it is joining, before the two finally mix completely. Below the bluffs, the rivers have built a wide, braided delta filled with sloughs, backwaters, and small islands that are mostly cut off from the main channel, and canoeists can paddle marked water trails through them for hours without seeing open river. Wyalusing became one of Wisconsin's earliest state parks, established in 1917, specifically because the view of this confluence was considered too important to leave in private hands. A river confluence is one of the few places on a map where you can stand still and watch two different stories merge into one, and this one happens to be framed by some of the tallest, oldest bluffs in the entire Driftless Area, bluffs that were never flattened because the glaciers that shaped the rest of Wisconsin simply never reached this far southwest.

[FACT 19: The Crookedest River in the Country | 43.7275,-90.5712]

Fact nineteen. The Kickapoo River winds through the heart of Wisconsin's Driftless Area for about one hundred thirty miles, or roughly two hundred ten kilometers, between the town of Wilton and the point where it finally empties into the Wisconsin River near Wauzeka. The strange part is the distance as a bird would fly it: straight line, that same stretch is less than half as long, somewhere around sixty miles. The river's name comes from an Algonquian word often translated as "one who goes here, then there," which locals say describes the Kickapoo perfectly, since it bends through nearly every point on the compass before it is through. Paddlers and boosters alike have long called it the crookedest river in the world, or at least in North America, though that claim is more local pride than official record. Near the town of Ontario, the river runs past Wildcat Mountain State Park, a thirty-six hundred acre park of sandstone bluffs, hardwood forest, and canoe landings that sits right beside the larger Kickapoo Valley Reserve. The reason the Kickapoo twists so dramatically comes back to the same unglaciated landscape that shapes the rest of this region. Rivers in glaciated country often flow across land that was flattened and reset within the last few thousand years, giving them straighter, younger paths. The Kickapoo, by contrast, has been cutting its own channel through old, uneven bedrock for a very long time, with no ice sheet ever coming through to erase its work and start the valley over, which is exactly how a river ends up folding back on itself again and again instead of running in a straight line to the sea. Paddlers who put in near Ontario or La Farge often describe the same stretch of river twice in one afternoon, because the current loops so far around a ridge that two different bends of the Kickapoo end up running almost parallel to each other, separated by only a few hundred feet of hillside, before the river finally commits to a new direction entirely.

[FACT 18: The Spring Creeks That Keep the Trout Cold | 43.6500,-90.9500]

Fact eighteen. Wisconsin holds roughly thirteen thousand miles of streams classified as trout water, and a large share of that total, along with thousands more miles in the Driftless corners of Minnesota and Iowa, is packed into this one region. The reason so much of it exists in the first place comes down to what is under the ground rather than what is on top of it. Thousands of natural springs across the region push cold groundwater up out of limestone aquifers, keeping many streams near the low fifties in temperature even in the middle of summer, which happens to be close to perfect for trout. The same limestone groundwater that keeps the water cold also carries dissolved minerals that feed the aquatic insects trout depend on for food, so cold and food rich tend to arrive in the same package here. Streams like Timber Coulee Creek, near the town of Coon Valley in Vernon County, have become some of the most closely watched brook trout and brown trout fisheries in the entire Midwest, and the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources now owns or holds easements on many thousands of acres of streambank across the region specifically to protect this kind of cold, spring fed water. I used to tell my students that if you want to find a map of where the last ice age did not reach, you do not need a geology textbook. You can just ask a fly fisherman where the good trout water is, because glaciers tend to bury or warm up the kind of steady, spring fed streams that trout need, and the thickest cluster of trout water left in the entire Midwest sits almost exactly on top of the Driftless Area's unglaciated boundary.

[FACT 17: Snails That Have Not Changed Since the Ice Age | 43.1000,-91.3500]

Fact seventeen. Scattered across wooded hillsides in the Driftless Area, mostly in northeastern Iowa, are a small number of slopes where cold air seeps out of sinkholes and cracks in the limestone even in the middle of August, cooling the ground beneath the trees by as much as fifty degrees compared to the air a short walk away. Scientists call these algific talus slopes, and in winter the same vents actually warm the slope slightly, keeping conditions strangely stable and cold year round. That odd, steady chill has let a handful of plants and animals survive here that disappeared from the rest of the Midwest thousands of years ago, when the last ice age ended and the climate warmed. The best known of them is the Iowa Pleistocene snail, a tiny species whose lineage is thought to be around four hundred thousand years old, and whose last strongholds on earth are these cold slopes, many of them inside the Driftless Area National Wildlife Refuge. Alongside the snail survive rare plants like the northern monkshood, a purple flowered relative of buttercups that survives in only a few scattered places in the country, with most of its known populations tucked into this one unglaciated corner of Iowa and Wisconsin. Because these slopes depend on a very specific, fragile combination of sinkholes, underground ice, and cold air flow, something that formed over thousands of years and cannot simply be rebuilt, conservationists treat the handful of known algific slopes as irreplaceable, protecting them from logging, grazing, and anything else that might disturb the delicate cave system feeding cold air up through the rock.

[FACT 16: The Prairies Only Goats Could Graze | 43.7500,-91.1500]

Fact sixteen. On the steep, south and west facing bluffs above the Mississippi River, a different kind of landscape survives that most people associate with states hundreds of miles to the west: dry prairie grassland, clinging to slopes too steep for a tractor and often too steep for cattle to graze comfortably. Longtime residents call these patches goat prairies, because old timers joked that only a goat could manage the footing, and botanists today call them bluff prairies, remnants of the open grassland that once stretched across far more of the upper Midwest before farming and fire suppression closed most of it in with trees and brush. These slopes face intense sun, thin rocky soil, and constant wind, conditions harsh enough that they were rarely worth the effort to plow, which is exactly why fragments of ancient prairie survived here while nearly identical grassland disappeared from flatter farmland nearby. Biologists in the La Crosse area say these surviving goat prairies support rare butterflies, moths, and native bees found almost nowhere else in the state, along with small specialists like the winged snaggletooth snail, a species that depends on this exact kind of dry, rocky, sun baked slope to survive. Most remaining goat prairies sit on private land today, scattered across bluff country from La Crosse south through Vernon and Crawford counties, and conservation groups now work directly with landowners to keep invasive brush from swallowing up what is left, since once a goat prairie grows over with trees, the specific combination of heat, light, and soil that let it survive for thousands of years is extremely difficult to recreate.

[FACT 15: The Valleys That Gave the Region Its Nickname | 43.6975,-91.0032]

Fact fifteen. Travel through southwestern Wisconsin long enough and you will start hearing locals use a word that sounds almost French, because it is: coulee. A coulee is a steep sided valley, usually cut by a stream that only flows part of the year, and the term comes from the Canadian French word coulée, itself rooted in the verb meaning to flow. Around towns like Coon Valley, in Vernon County, these coulees are so common and so central to daily life that the whole surrounding stretch of Wisconsin has picked up the nickname Coulee Country, while the very similar landscape just across the river in Minnesota is usually called Bluff Country instead. What makes a coulee different from an ordinary valley is how sharply its walls rise, often climbing hundreds of feet in a short distance, with little of the gentle, rounded slope you would expect in land that had once been smoothed over by an ice sheet. That sharpness is the whole point of the word, and the whole point of the region. Because the Driftless Area was never buried and reset by glacial ice, its rivers and streams have had an extraordinarily long, uninterrupted stretch of time to cut straight down into old limestone and sandstone bedrock, carving these narrow, high walled coulees instead of the broad, shallow valleys typical of glaciated farmland just a short drive away. Drive Wisconsin's back roads through Vernon or Crawford County today and you will wind through coulee after coulee, each one a small, self contained valley with its own creek, its own dairy farms, and often its own tiny unincorporated crossroads at the bottom.

[FACT 14: A Quarter Million Acres Saved from the Drainage Shovel | 43.4186,-91.1979]

Fact fourteen. The Upper Mississippi River National Wildlife and Fish Refuge stretches two hundred sixty-one miles along the river through Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa, and Illinois, protecting more than two hundred forty thousand acres of backwaters, marshes, islands, and floodplain forest hemmed in by the steep bluffs the Driftless Area is known for. The refuge exists because of one determined conservationist. In 1923, a sportsmen's group leader named Will Dilg learned that engineers were considering a plan to drain large stretches of the river's backwaters for farmland, and he pushed instead for Congress to protect the entire corridor as a federal refuge. Congress agreed the following year, passing the Upper Mississippi River Wild Life and Fish Refuge Act in June of 1924, creating one of the oldest refuges of its kind in the country. Today the protected stretch is recognized internationally as a Wetland of Importance and a Globally Significant Bird Area, and more than three and a half million visits happen there every year, as people come to fish, hunt, paddle, and watch migrating birds pass through on their way between Canada and the Gulf Coast. The bluffs that frame the refuge rise anywhere from one hundred to six hundred feet above the river, and those bluffs are precisely why this stretch of the Mississippi kept its tangled, braided backwater character in the first place. A river running through flat, glaciated farmland tends to get straightened, ditched, and drained for cropland over time. A river boxed in by steep, unglaciated Driftless bluffs has far less flat ground nearby worth draining, which left its backwaters wild enough that a refuge was still worth creating a century later. Pilots flying low over the refuge today still describe it as one continuous maze of green islands and brown water channels stretching for mile after mile between the bluff lines on either side, a view that would simply not exist if the valley had been scraped flat and refilled by glacial ice the way most of the river country upstream and downstream of the Driftless Area eventually was.

[FACT 13: Wisconsin's Second Oldest Town | 43.0510,-91.1401]

Fact thirteen. Prairie du Chien, Wisconsin, sitting where the Wisconsin River meets the Mississippi, is generally considered the second oldest permanent settlement in the state, after Green Bay, with records of French traders establishing themselves there as early as the 1760s and 1780s. For more than a century the town served as a major rendezvous point for explorers, missionaries, and fur traders moving along the Fox and Wisconsin rivers, the main water route connecting the Great Lakes to the Mississippi system before railroads existed. The United States built Fort Crawford there in 1816, determined not to let the British hold the strategic river crossing again after they had occupied it during the War of 1812. The fort later became the site where the Sauk leader Black Hawk formally surrendered in 1832, ending the conflict that bears his name, and in the years before that it was also where Army surgeon William Beaumont carried out groundbreaking early studies of human digestion on a patient recovering from a gunshot wound, research still cited in medical textbooks today. River junctions almost always become important towns, because whoever controls the point where two trade routes meet controls an enormous amount of commerce, and Prairie du Chien sitting at the junction of the Fox-Wisconsin river route and the Mississippi made it one of the most fought over pieces of real estate in the entire Upper Midwest for nearly two hundred years. The original fort was eventually rebuilt after flood damage, and today it operates as a museum run by the local historical society.

[FACT 12: The Town That Moved Itself Uphill | 43.2822,-90.7965]

Fact twelve. Soldiers Grove, Wisconsin, sits along the Kickapoo River, a river so prone to flooding that the town was seriously damaged by major floods in 1907, 1912, 1917, 1935, and 1951, with little relief in sight even after Congress approved a flood control plan in 1962. Construction on a dam meant to finally control the river began in the 1970s, but the project stalled and was abandoned in 1975, leaving the town as exposed as ever. Rather than keep rebuilding in the floodplain, village leaders proposed something unusual: moving the entire business district uphill, out of the Kickapoo's reach, for good. Federal agencies initially turned the idea down, so in 1977 the village used its own money to buy land on higher ground anyway. A severe flood in July of 1978 changed the federal government's mind, and by that fall the Department of Housing and Urban Development had granted the town nine hundred thousand dollars to make the relocation happen. Construction on the new business district began in 1979 and wrapped up by 1983, ultimately relocating thirty-six businesses, three municipal buildings, and twenty-two homes to safer ground. In the middle of the 1970s energy crisis, the village added one more ambitious requirement: every new building in the relocated downtown had to get at least half of its heating from the sun, earning Soldiers Grove the nickname Solar Village. When another major flood hit the Kickapoo valley in August of 2007, Soldiers Grove's new downtown, sitting safely above the water, came through essentially undamaged, nearly three decades of planning finally proving its worth.

[FACT 11: America's First Rail Trail | 43.8344,-90.4337]

Fact eleven. The Elroy-Sparta State Trail, running roughly thirty-two miles between the towns of Elroy, Wilton, and Sparta in western Wisconsin, is widely credited as the first rail line in the country to be converted into a recreational trail for hikers and bikers. The corridor began as part of the Chicago and North Western Railway's main line, carrying trains over the hilly Driftless terrain from the 1870s until the mid 1960s, when the railroad finished a more efficient bypass elsewhere and abandoned this older, curvier route instead. The State of Wisconsin bought the abandoned corridor for a modest sum and opened it as a public trail by the mid to late 1960s, and in 1971 the United States Department of the Interior designated it the first rail trail in the nation to receive status as a National Recreation Trail. Today the crushed limestone path still runs through three hand dug rock tunnels, the longest of them stretching nearly thirty-eight hundred feet straight through solid Driftless bedrock, built originally because the region's steep, unglaciated hills left railroad engineers no gentler way around them. Riders pass through five small towns along the route and can rent lantern style bike lights at either end specifically to get through the darker tunnels safely. In 2008, the Rails-to-Trails Conservancy inducted the Elroy-Sparta corridor into its Hall of Fame, recognizing it as the template that an entire nationwide movement of converting old railroad grades into biking and walking trails eventually grew out of, a movement that exists in no small part because this one stretch of hard to railroad, impossible to flatten Driftless terrain needed tunnels in the first place. Thousands of similar rail trails now exist across the country, following old grades through much gentler, glaciated farmland, but almost none of them need a lantern to get through their own right of way, because almost none of them were ever forced to tunnel straight through solid rock just to keep a nineteenth century train moving across this kind of unbroken ridge country.

[FACT 10: The Cornish Miners Who Built a Hill Town | 42.8611,-90.1795]

Fact ten. Mineral Point, Wisconsin, got its name from exactly what prospectors found there starting in 1827: rich deposits of lead ore sitting close to the surface of a hill overlooking the surrounding valley. Word of the discovery reached far beyond Wisconsin, and through the 1830s and 1840s, skilled miners from Cornwall, in southwestern England, began arriving specifically to work the lead diggings, bringing centuries of underground mining experience from home. By 1845, roughly half the town's population traced its roots back to Cornwall, and many of those families settled in a cluster of homes at the base of the hill known today as Pendarvis, where small stone and timber cottages built in a distinctly Cornish style still stand as a museum run by the Wisconsin Historical Society. The Cornish miners brought more than just digging skill. Their stonecutting and quarrying traditions shaped much of the limestone and sandstone architecture still visible across Mineral Point's downtown today. The town's fortunes shifted when many experienced miners left for California following the 1848 gold rush, and mining locally pivoted from lead toward zinc before fading further after the First World War. Mineral Point has kept its Cornish identity alive deliberately ever since, forming a local Cornish heritage society in 1991, holding an annual Cornish festival, and officially twinning the town with Redruth, Cornwall, in 1997. None of it would have happened without the lead deposits themselves, and those deposits sit this close to the surface specifically because the Driftless Area's bedrock was never buried under glacial till, leaving the region's mineral veins unusually easy for nineteenth century miners to find and work with simple hand tools.

[FACT 9: The Architect Who Built Into the Hillside | 43.1378,-90.0677]

Fact nine. Just south of Spring Green, Wisconsin, on a ridge overlooking the Wisconsin River, sits Taliesin, the roughly eight hundred acre estate the architect Frank Lloyd Wright built, rebuilt, and kept expanding on his family's own land across most of his long career. Wright named the property after a Welsh bard, and the name is usually translated as "shining brow," a reference to the way the buildings' wings and terraces were designed to follow the natural curve of the hilltop rather than flatten it first. Wright's mother's family had settled this stretch of the Driftless Area generations earlier, and Wright drew directly on the landscape itself when building, using local limestone pulled from nearby quarries and river sand mixed straight into his plaster. The connection between Wright's architecture and this particular landscape was no accident. The Driftless Area's steep, uneven, glacier free hills gave Wright a kind of site that simply did not exist across most of the flattened, glaciated Midwest, terrain with real topographic character to design around rather than against. In 2019, Taliesin was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of a group of Wright buildings recognized collectively for their architectural significance, adding to a National Historic Landmark designation the estate had already held since 1976. A nonprofit called Taliesin Preservation has managed tours and restoration work at the site since 1990, maintaining buildings that still show, more than a century after Wright began building here, how differently an architect designs when the land itself refuses to be flat. Visitors who tour the hilltop today often remark that the house seems to disappear into the ridge from a distance, low rooflines and native stone walls blending into the same bluff line that shapes nearly every other overlook in this part of the Wisconsin River valley.

[FACT 8: Wisconsin's Largest Amish Settlement | 43.7366,-90.7682]

Fact eight. Near the small town of Cashton, straddling Monroe and Vernon counties in the heart of the Driftless Area, sits the largest Amish settlement in the state of Wisconsin, a community founded in 1966 that has grown to somewhere around two thousand five hundred residents across roughly twenty church districts today. Families here farm the same steep, narrow coulee valleys that make modern mechanized agriculture difficult for anyone, Amish or otherwise, which is part of why this terrain appealed to a community that largely avoids large motorized equipment in the first place. Horse drawn buggies share the region's winding back roads with cars and dairy trucks, and roadside stands selling furniture, baked goods, and produce have become a defining feature of a drive through Vernon County. Smaller Amish communities have taken root nearby as well, including one around the town of Hillsboro and another near Viroqua, and together Wisconsin now ranks among the largest Amish populated states in the country, a surprising fact to many people who associate Amish settlement mainly with Pennsylvania and Ohio. I used to tell my students that where you find Amish farming communities, you can often read something about the land itself, because Amish families have historically sought out places where smaller scale, labor intensive farming still makes practical sense, exactly the kind of steep, irregular, glacier free hillside farmland the Driftless Area has in abundance, and exactly the kind of land that large scale, flat field, heavily mechanized agriculture elsewhere in the Midwest has mostly left behind.

[FACT 7: The Valley That Looked Like Norway | 43.6661,-90.8554]

Fact seven. Beginning in the late 1840s, Norwegian immigrants began arriving in the hill country straddling Wisconsin and Iowa in large numbers, drawn in part by a landscape of steep ridges and narrow valleys that reminded many of them of the fjord country they had left behind. A historical marker in Vernon County notes that Norwegian settlers reached the Westby area by 1848 and the Coon Creek valley the following year, often arriving in tight clusters of families from the exact same valley or parish back in Norway, recreating entire neighborhoods from the old country inside new Wisconsin coulees. By 1860, Wisconsin held nearly half of all Norwegian immigrants living in the United States, and Vernon County became one of the most densely Norwegian counties anywhere in the country. Across the Mississippi in northeastern Iowa, Decorah grew into an even larger center of Norwegian American life, home to Luther College, founded in 1861, and for decades the Decorah-Posten, a Norwegian language newspaper that at its peak reached roughly forty-six thousand readers scattered across the country. Today Decorah's Vesterheim Norwegian-American Museum preserves and tells that immigration story in detail, from the ships that carried the first settlers across the Atlantic to the farm tools and folk art families carried with them into the coulees. The steep, hilly land that drew Norwegian farmers here in the first place was, once again, a direct product of the region's unglaciated geology, terrain unlike almost anywhere else available to new immigrants in the flat, glaciated Midwest at the time.

[FACT 6: Night Ski Jumping Under the Lights | 43.6661,-90.8554]

Fact six. In the small Norwegian founded town of Westby, Wisconsin, skiing was simply how people got around in winter for the better part of a century, since the hilly Driftless terrain made sleds and skis far more practical than trying to push a wagon through drifted coulee roads. Local Norwegian immigrants founded the Snowflake Ski Club in 1922, and the club has kept a ski jumping tradition alive in the community ever since, running one of its signature events each February on a groomed hill measuring one hundred eighteen meters. The tournament has become one of the only night ski jumping competitions held anywhere in the United States, with competitors launching off the jump under rows of bright floodlights while spectators watch from the dark hillside below. The Snowflake Ski Club describes itself as one of only two remaining fully volunteer large hill ski jumping clubs left anywhere in the Western Hemisphere, a title that reflects just how much the sport has faded elsewhere even as Westby keeps it going. Visiting international jumpers are often put up directly in local residents' homes rather than hotels, a tradition organizers say grew naturally out of the close knit, Norwegian rooted hospitality that shaped the town from its earliest settlement. The hill itself sits built into one of the region's many steep Driftless ridges, terrain that would be unusual to find almost anywhere else in the glaciated farm country of the Upper Midwest, and without those ridges, a tradition built specifically around jumping off a hill into open air would likely never have taken root here at all. Every February, the floodlights go up, the hill gets groomed by volunteers using the same hand tools their grandparents used, and for one cold weekend the whole town effectively becomes a ski resort again, a hundred years after the first Norwegian families decided this particular stretch of coulee country was close enough to home to stay.

[FACT 5: The Birds and Bears Built Into the Bluffs | 43.0936,-91.1915]

Fact five. On the Iowa side of the Mississippi, near the town of Harpers Ferry, Effigy Mounds National Monument protects more than two hundred ancient earthen mounds built atop the bluffs overlooking the river, including thirty-one effigy mounds shaped deliberately in the forms of birds and bears. Two of the bird shaped mounds have wingspans measured at one hundred twenty-four feet and two hundred twelve feet, while the Great Bear Mound, the largest effigy in the park, stretches one hundred thirty-seven feet long and seventy feet wide across the shoulders. The National Park Service dates most of the effigies to somewhere between roughly eight hundred fifty and fourteen hundred years ago, built by the ancestors of American Indian nations who still live in the region today, though with no written records surviving from the mound builders themselves, their exact purpose, whether ceremonial, territorial, or something else entirely, remains genuinely unknown even to the archaeologists who have studied the site for decades. Earlier mound counts from the mid twentieth century ran lower than the figure the National Park Service uses today, a reminder that careful survey work has kept adding newly identified mounds to the map for decades, and that the full extent of this bluff top monument may still not be completely known even now. What makes the monument's location especially fitting is the bluff top terrain the mounds were built on in the first place. Builders chose high, dramatic overlooks above the Mississippi, exactly the kind of steep, uneroded bluff that the Driftless Area's unglaciated bedrock preserved for them to work with, rather than the flattened, buried landscape a glacier would have left behind. Effigy mounds built in this concentration are found almost nowhere else in the country, and that fact alone tells you something about how unusual this stretch of river bluff country really is, a landscape distinctive enough, more than a thousand years ago, that people chose to shape their most important monuments directly into its hilltops.

[FACT 4: The Crater Hiding Under a Norwegian College Town | 43.3031,-91.7852]

Fact four. Underneath the town of Decorah, Iowa, buried several hundred meters below the surface, sits a crater left behind by a meteorite that struck the earth roughly four hundred seventy million years ago, during a period geologists call the Middle Ordovician. Geologists with the Iowa Geological Survey first grew suspicious of something unusual in the 2000s, after examining drill core samples that revealed an oddly shaped shale deposit forming a nearly circular basin about three and a half miles, or five and a half kilometers, across, a shape an amateur geologist named Jean Young had actually flagged years earlier after noticing strange rock structures turning up in local well drillings. Researchers eventually found shocked quartz grains in rock samples from the site, considered the strongest possible evidence of a genuine meteorite impact, since shock features like that essentially only form under the extreme pressure of a cosmic collision. In 2013, an airborne survey conducted jointly by the United States Geological Survey and state geological agencies in Iowa and Minnesota, originally intended mainly to map mineral resources, produced detailed images confirming the crater's buried structure once and for all, using gravity measurements and airborne electromagnetic readings taken from a small plane flying low over the Decorah countryside. The confirmed crater turned out to be nearly five times the size of the famous Barringer Meteor Crater in Arizona, a sizable impact structure by any measure, hidden the entire time beneath a quiet Norwegian American college town that had no idea what was sitting under its streets. I used to tell my students that some of the biggest geographic discoveries in this country were not found by explorers at all, but by geologists reading drill samples pulled up from ordinary water wells, and the Decorah crater is about as good an example of that as exists anywhere in the United States.

[FACT 3: The Last Battle of the Black Hawk War | 43.6500,-91.1000]

Fact three. In early August of 1832, near the mouth of the Bad Axe River in present day Vernon County, Wisconsin, United States Army soldiers and state militia caught up with Sauk and Meskwaki families led by the war leader Black Hawk, who had spent the previous months trying to lead his people back across the Mississippi after a disputed series of land cessions had pushed them out of their home territory in Illinois. The resulting encounter, remembered today as the Battle of Bad Axe, left at least one hundred fifty Sauk and Meskwaki people dead, with some historians' estimates running as high as three hundred, including noncombatants attempting to cross the river, and ended the broader conflict historians call the Black Hawk War. Black Hawk himself escaped the immediate fighting but surrendered not long afterward at Fort Crawford in Prairie du Chien, roughly forty miles downriver, bringing the war to its final close. The terrain itself played a direct role in how the final encounter unfolded. The Driftless Area's steep bluffs and narrow river bottoms near the Bad Axe left retreating families with few open escape routes, hemmed in by terrain that would have looked very different across flatter, glaciated farmland elsewhere in the region. Today a Wisconsin historical marker near Victory, Wisconsin, identifies the battle site, and the Bad Axe River itself still runs through Vernon County before reaching the Mississippi nearby. The events of 1832 remain a documented and sobering part of the region's history, one that local historical societies continue to research and interpret, and the terrain that shaped the war's final days is the same unglaciated, deeply cut landscape that shapes nearly every other fact on this list.

[FACT 2: The Valley Where America Learned to Save Its Own Soil | 43.6800,-90.9800]

Fact two. In 1933, the Coon Creek watershed, covering roughly one hundred forty square miles across parts of Vernon and La Crosse counties in Wisconsin, became the first soil conservation demonstration project in the entire United States, chosen specifically because its steep, Driftless hillside farms were eroding at an alarming rate. Heavy rains on the region's steep coulee slopes, combined with decades of farming methods designed for flatter ground, had stripped topsoil off hillsides at a pace that genuinely worried federal agricultural officials, and the conservationist Aldo Leopold, along with soil scientist Hugh Hammond Bennett, helped select Coon Creek as the place to test new solutions at scale. The resulting land use plan, developed with local farmers directly rather than imposed on them, introduced practices still standard across American agriculture today: contour strip farming, which plants crops in curved bands that follow a hillside's natural slope instead of running straight up and down it, along with fenced woodlands, grassed waterways, and terracing on the steepest ridge top fields. A later study found that erosion across the watershed had dropped by at least seventy-five percent compared to pre-project levels, a result significant enough that the methods tested in this one Wisconsin valley went on to shape soil conservation policy nationwide for the rest of the twentieth century. Farmers in the watershed kept the contour strips long after the original federal demonstration project ended, and driving the ridges above Coon Creek today, you can still see entire hillsides striped in alternating bands of corn, hay, and pasture, each band curving along the slope exactly the way the original nineteen thirty-three survey crews laid them out. Coon Creek is proof that geography and policy are never really separate subjects, because the United States Department of Agriculture did not invent modern soil conservation in a laboratory. It learned it on these exact hillsides, specifically because the Driftless Area's steep, unglaciated slopes made the consequences of bad farming practices show up faster and more visibly here than almost anywhere else in the country.

[FACT 1: The Doughnut Hole the Ice Sheets Never Filled | 43.5569,-90.8898]

Fact one. For every fact on this list, from meteor craters to Amish farms to crooked rivers, there is one underlying reason none of it looks like the rest of the Midwest, and that reason is the simple fact that the glaciers never came. Over the past two and a half million years, continental ice sheets advanced across what is now Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, and Illinois again and again, and through at least the last several hundred thousand years of those advances, the ice moved around this particular pocket of land rather than over it. Geologists have found scattered traces suggesting much older ice may once have brushed its edges, but nothing that buried and reshaped it the way the surrounding country was reshaped, leaving behind what researchers sometimes describe as a stippled doughnut hole sitting right in the middle of the glacial map. The reason the ice diverted is still debated, but the leading explanations point to ancient high ground to the north and east, including the Wisconsin Dome of extremely old Precambrian bedrock, along with deep natural channels like the Lake Superior basin that apparently gave advancing ice an easier path around this region rather than through it. Crucially, the Driftless Area is not high ground itself. Older surveys actually describe it as lower in elevation than much of the drift covered country immediately surrounding it, which means its survival was never about height. It was about timing, direction, and the accident of which way a given glacier happened to be flowing. I used to put this exact question to my students every year, because it is the kind of thing that seems backward at first: why would the ice skip a patch of land that is not even higher than the country around it? The honest answer, the one geologists still argue over in the details, is that a moving ice sheet does not simply flow downhill like water. It follows whatever paths of least resistance the surrounding bedrock and topography give it, and in this particular corner of the continent, those paths consistently ran around this one stubborn patch of land instead of through it. Because no ice sheet ever scraped across this landscape, grinding down hills, filling in valleys, and leaving behind the sheets of glacial sediment known as drift, the region kept its older, far more rugged terrain completely intact: the sharp bluffs, the narrow coulees, the limestone caves, the trout streams, and the thin, mineral rich soil that both farmers and rare ice age snails have depended on ever since. Every other fact we have counted down tonight is, in one way or another, a direct consequence of this one quiet, repeated accident of glacial geography.

There is another Driftless Area beneath the familiar one, a landscape of meteor craters, ice age relict snails, buried battle sites, and valleys where farmers first learned to save their own soil, and most people driving through on their way somewhere else never know any of it is there.

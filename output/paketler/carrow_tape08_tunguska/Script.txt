[BLOK4_DUVARDAKI_KARELER]
The event itself, before I show you what this file adds to the public record, is already remarkable enough on its own terms, and well enough documented outside this drawer that I want to give you the plain account first. A massive aerial explosion, witnessed by people hundreds of miles away, flattened an estimated eighty million trees across roughly eight hundred square miles of forest, knocking them down in a radiating pattern centered on a single point, with no crater ever found at that center despite decades of expeditions sent specifically to look for one. The explosion is estimated, by later analysis using the flattened tree pattern alone, to have released energy in the range of a large modern weapon, over a region so remote that the first scientific expedition did not reach it for nearly twenty years.

The first item in this folder that goes beyond the public record is a set of photographs taken by a later expedition, decades after the event, showing the pattern of fallen trees from the air — a vast, radiating starburst, trees lying with their tops pointing outward from the center in every direction, except for a small stand near the very middle of the blast zone left standing upright, scorched but not felled, the way a stand of trees sometimes survives directly beneath an explosion whose force passes mostly above and around it rather than through it. I have looked at aerial photographs of the pattern many times, and the standing trees at the center, surrounded by a sea of fallen ones, remain the single most visually unsettling image in this entire drawer, more than any sketch of any creature I have shown you.

I want to describe the standing trees a little further, because I think the detail rewards it. A later forestry analysis, included in this folder, measured the angle of the scorching on their remaining branches and compared it against the angle of the fall pattern in the surrounding forest, concluding that the blast's downward force, directly above that small stand, had been almost entirely vertical, passing through the canopy rather than across it, in a way the report describes as "consistent with a detonation point nearly overhead rather than at an angle," which is itself one of the pieces of evidence used to argue for an airburst rather than a surface impact. I mention the technical detail because I think it is rare, in this drawer, to find a piece of physical evidence that supports the ordinary explanation this cleanly, and I want to give it full credit exactly where it is due.

The second item is a soil and resin sample card, taken from the trees nearest the center during a later expedition, recording trace mineral content the card describes as "anomalous for the regional geology, consistent with extraterrestrial or cosmic origin," a plain, almost clinical way of saying that whatever came down there left a fingerprint in the ground that did not belong to that part of the world before it arrived. The card does not speculate further, in the same restrained, frustrating habit I have come to expect from every laboratory card in this drawer regardless of the decade it was written in.

The third item is a set of interview transcripts taken from the handful of local herders and hunters who were close enough to witness the event directly and survived to be interviewed decades later, elderly by the time anyone with a tape recorder finally reached them. Their accounts agree on several details that never made it into most popular retellings: a column of what one described as "fire that split the sky in two" before the main explosion, a heat so intense at a distance of many miles that one witness reported his shirt scorching where he sat, and, in nearly every account independently, a sound that did not arrive until long after the light did, consistent with a blast originating high above the ground rather than at it, a detail later analysis has generally supported.

I want to add one witness account in particular, because I think it is the single most evocative sentence in this entire folder. An elderly herder, interviewed decades after the fact through an interpreter, described the moment the light appeared not in terms of fire or explosion at all, but by saying, as it was translated to the investigator and recorded exactly this way in the transcript, that the sky "opened like a second sun had been born and immediately regretted it." I do not know how much of that phrasing belongs to the herder and how much belongs to whatever translation passed it along to the page in front of me tonight. I know that it has stayed with me since I first read it more than any technical measurement in this folder has.

The fourth item, and the one I want to describe most carefully, is a page from a later, more thorough soil survey that this office's own predecessor apparently commissioned under a scientific cover decades after the event, once the technology existed to look for what an earlier expedition could not have found. It records a small quantity of recovered material, embedded deep in the resin of one of the surviving central trees, described only as "a dense, dark fragment, non-magnetic, resistant to standard acid testing, approximately the size of a coin." The fragment itself is not photographed in this folder. A single typed line notes that it was "transferred for further analysis" to a facility whose name, once again, is blacked out, and no further record of it appears anywhere else in this drawer that I have been permitted to find.

I want to note the handwriting on that single typed line as well, because it is the one detail in this item I have not been able to let go of. Beneath the typed sentence about transfer for further analysis, in the same pencil hand I have shown you before on earlier tapes, is a short unsigned addition: "Ask Carrow before closing." I do not know who or what Carrow refers to. It is not a name that appears on the roster of this office, past or present, as far as that roster has ever been made available to me, and it is not a term I have found used anywhere else in this drawer across eight tapes of reading. I am telling you about it because I think a name like that, appearing once, in pencil, unexplained, deserves to be on the record rather than left in a margin only I have ever read.

There is a fifth item, smaller than the others, that I want to describe because of how it was produced rather than what it directly shows. A magnetic survey of the surrounding swampland, conducted using equipment far more sensitive than anything available to the original expeditions, identified three small depressions in the terrain that the survey team initially hoped might be impact craters after all. Further investigation determined all three to be ordinary geological features, karst-like sinkholes unrelated to the event, a conclusion the report reaches honestly and without any apparent pressure to find something more dramatic. I include this item mainly because it is one of the few moments in this entire drawer where an investigation reached a boring conclusion and simply stopped there, without this office's own habitual blank disposition line attached to it, which I have come to find almost refreshing after eight tapes of files that refuse to close cleanly.

I also want to mention a sixth item, a weather station log from a town thousands of miles from the blast, noting unusual atmospheric effects in the nights following the event — a persistent glow in the night sky at latitudes where such a thing was not ordinarily seen, bright enough, according to the log, to read a newspaper outdoors well after midnight for several nights running before fading gradually over the following two weeks. This detail is well documented outside this drawer as well, generally attributed to dust and ice particles thrown high into the atmosphere by the blast itself, scattering sunlight across the night sky from a sun that, at those latitudes and that time of year, sits only just below the horizon. I give it its full scientific due. I also cannot help noting that it is one of the longer, stranger afterglows in the public record of any event in this entire drawer.

[BLOK5_ONLAR_HAKKINDA]
I want to say something about the scientist who led the expedition responsible for most of what this file actually contains, because I think his career deserves more attention than this file gives it directly. He spent a significant portion of his working life pursuing this single event, across multiple expeditions over nearly two decades, through conditions that by every account were genuinely brutal, and died, according to a brief biographical note appended to this folder, without ever publishing a conclusion he considered final, still gathering data and still revising his own estimates of the blast's size and origin at the time of his death. I want to add a detail about his final expedition specifically, because it is the one this office's own predecessor appears to have quietly funded under the scientific cover I mentioned earlier, without his apparent knowledge of who was actually paying for a portion of his equipment. I do not think he ever knew this office existed, or that a portion of his life's work had been quietly subsidized by an agency with its own, considerably narrower reason for wanting that resin sample analyzed. [BLOK6_KISA_SAPMA_COMET]
A short digression, because I think the scientific explanation for this file deserves the same fairness I have given every official explanation before it, and because, unlike some of the others, this one is widely and reasonably accepted by people with no connection to this drawer at all.

The leading scientific account holds that a small asteroid or comet fragment, perhaps a few dozen meters across, entered the atmosphere at extremely high speed and exploded several miles above the ground due to the immense pressure and heat of its passage, before any solid remnant could reach the surface intact — an airburst, in the modern term, which would explain both the radiating pattern of fallen trees and the complete absence of an impact crater, since the object, by this account, was essentially vaporized in the air rather than striking the ground at all. I find this explanation considerably more persuasive on its own terms than most of the official explanations I have given you across this drawer, because it requires no classified program, no government decision, nothing at all beyond the ordinary physics of a rock entering an atmosphere too fast for its own structural integrity to survive the passage.

What this explanation does not, on its own, account for is the fragment described in the fourth item I showed you a moment ago — a dense, non-magnetic, acid-resistant material recovered decades later from the resin of a surviving tree, transferred to an unnamed facility and never mentioned again in any record I have access to. An airburst event, consuming its own source material in the atmosphere, should leave behind largely dust and debris scattered thinly across a wide area, not a single discrete, analyzable fragment embedded at the very center of the blast, dense and resistant enough to be worth a laboratory's further attention decades after the fact. I do not think the comet theory is wrong. I think it may be incomplete, in exactly the specific and narrow place where this office's own interest in the file appears to begin.

I want to be fair to the theory one more time before I leave it, because I think it deserves one further defense. It is entirely possible that a dense fragment could survive an airburst intact, particularly if the object was not uniform in composition, a looser, icier exterior burning away in the atmosphere while a harder core continued downward largely unaffected, eventually coming to rest gently enough, at the end of a much slower fall than the initial explosion, to be found decades later lodged in living resin rather than buried in an impact crater. I think this is a reasonable, mundane way for exactly this kind of fragment to exist without requiring anything beyond ordinary physics, and I want to say plainly that I am not certain my own unease about the pencil note and the unnamed facility outweighs a plain scientific possibility that fits the evidence just as well without requiring any of this office's shadows to explain it.

[BLOK7_NE_OLDUKLARI]
One opinion per file. I want to hold this one loosely, because I think this is the file in this drawer where the honest scientific explanation and my own instinct come closest to actually agreeing with each other, and I do not want to manufacture a disagreement where very little of one exists.

I think a natural object, almost certainly of cosmic origin, entered the atmosphere that morning and exploded above the ground, exactly as the comet theory describes, and I think that explains the overwhelming majority of what happened in that forest. What I think this office's own involvement adds is narrower and stranger: that something recovered from the wreckage, decades after the fact, interested someone enough to transfer it quietly for further study and then let the file go cold publicly, the ordinary mystery of an unexplained explosion allowed to stand as the full story while a much smaller, much quieter question about one recovered fragment was pulled out of it and filed somewhere else entirely, under a name I have never been permitted to see.

I want to add one further thought, because I think this file, sitting as it does at the very start of this office's recorded interest in anything at all, tells me something about how this whole arrangement might have begun in the first place. If a single recovered fragment from an otherwise explainable natural event was enough to justify decades of quiet funding, careful cover stories, and a drawer number lower than almost anything else in this building, then I think whatever this office actually exists to track did not begin with a dramatic encounter or a frightened family. It began, perhaps, with one small, dense, unexplainable object, pulled out of an ordinary disaster by someone curious enough to keep asking what it was, long after everyone else had settled for calling the whole event a comet and moving on.

## TITLE: Why Are Airplane Windows Rounded Instead of Square?

[1. HOOK]

Somewhere above your head on your last flight there was a small oval pane sealed into the metal skin of the airplane, and there was not a single sharp corner anywhere on its frame. That shape was not a styling choice made by some designer who liked soft curves. Every commercial jet built since the nineteen fifties uses rounded or oval windows, every single one, from every manufacturer, in every country that builds airplanes. A perfect rectangle would photograph better, would let in more light, and would be simpler and cheaper to cut into a sheet of metal. For a shape with no obvious benefit over its more practical rival, that is a strange kind of universal agreement, and it did not happen by accident. Four window frames on one specific airplane, designed in England in the final years of the nineteen forties, are the reason you cannot find a square airplane window anywhere on earth today.

[2. THE QUESTION]

So why are airplane windows rounded instead of square.

The intuitive answer most people reach for is aerodynamics, the idea that smooth curves let air flow past an airplane more easily than sharp corners do, and that a machine built to slice through the sky should be covered in curves wherever possible. That answer feels right. It is also wrong. A window set into the side of a fuselage barely disturbs the air outside the airplane at all. The air rushing past the skin of a jet at cruising speed does not notice whether a small opening near a passenger's shoulder is round, oval, or square, because the opening is flush with the surface and far too small to matter to the airflow. Aerodynamics did not round off the windows.

[LABEL: pressurized cabin]

The real answer has nothing to do with what the air is doing outside the airplane and everything to do with what is happening inside it. Airliners cruise at altitudes around thirty-five thousand feet, where the outside air is too thin for a human body to survive in for more than a few minutes. To keep everyone on board alive and comfortable, the cabin is sealed and filled with denser air, which means the fuselage behaves like a long metal balloon that inflates every time the airplane climbs and relaxes every time it descends. Multiply that stretching and relaxing by thousands of flights over an airplane's working life, and the strain on every opening cut into that metal skin, every door, every hatch, every window, becomes a serious engineering problem. To see how that strain settled the question of window shape for good, it helps to go back to a small aircraft company in England at the start of the jet age, and to the accidents that followed its airplane into the sky.

[3. HISTORY]

On the twenty-seventh of July, 1949, at the de Havilland Aircraft Company's airfield in Hatfield, England, a sleek new airliner called the Comet made its first flight, with company test pilot John Cunningham at the controls. It was the world's first jet-powered aircraft built to carry paying passengers, and it was genuinely remarkable for its time. Designed under the direction of chief designer Ronald Bishop, it flew faster and higher than any piston-engine airliner in service, high enough that its cabin had to be pressurized far more aggressively than earlier propeller aircraft had required. The de Havilland Comet entered commercial service with the British Overseas Airways Corporation, generally known as BOAC, on the second of May, 1952, flying the route between London and Johannesburg. Its cabin windows were cut as near-rectangles, with only a slight rounding at the corners rather than a true curve, a shape that looked modern, let in generous light, and matched the styling of the ocean liners and railway carriages passengers of the era were used to.

[LABEL: static load]

At the time the Comet entered service, the engineering standards used to judge an airplane's structural safety were built mainly around static load, meaning how much force a part could withstand once, under the heaviest expected conditions, such as a hard landing or a steep turn. Repeated, cyclical loading of the kind a pressurized fuselage experiences on every single flight, inflating and relaxing again and again over years of service, was understood in theory by materials engineers, but had never been tested at the altitude, the pressure difference, or the flight frequency that a commercial jet now demanded. The Comet was, in that sense, flying ahead of the testing methods that existed to check whether it was safe to keep flying that way for years at a time.

[LABEL: fuselage]

Exactly one year after that first commercial flight, on the second of May, 1953, a Comet registered G-ALYV, operating as BOAC Flight 783, broke apart in the air shortly after takeoff from Calcutta, in India, during a severe thunderstorm. Forty-three people died. At the time, investigators leaned toward the storm itself as the cause, since violent weather was a known hazard and wreckage recovery from the crash site was limited.

Investigators at the time treated the Calcutta loss as a closed case, attributed to structural overstress inside violent turbulence rather than to any flaw in the airplane itself, and that explanation has generally held up on its own. It is worth being precise here: the Calcutta accident was never formally tied to the fatigue mechanism this video is about. The two later losses were.

Then, on the tenth of January, 1954, a Comet registered G-ALYP, operating as BOAC Flight 781, climbed out of Rome, Italy, on the final leg of a flight that had begun in Singapore. Near the island of Elba, off the coast of Tuscany, the aircraft broke up in midair and fell into the Mediterranean Sea. All thirty-five people on board were killed, including the Australian journalist Chester Wilmot. The entire Comet fleet was grounded for inspection, a set of modifications was made, and after investigators could not find a conclusive mechanical fault, the aircraft was cleared to fly again. At that point only a little over twenty Comet 1 aircraft existed worldwide, which meant every single hull lost was a meaningful fraction of the entire jet airliner fleet on the planet.

[LABEL: metal fatigue]

That clearance did not hold for long. On the eighth of April, 1954, a Comet registered G-ALYY, leased from BOAC and flying as South African Airways Flight 201 under the command of Captain Wilhelm Karel Mostert, broke up over the Mediterranean near Naples, Italy, while climbing toward thirty-five thousand feet. All twenty-one people aboard were killed. This time the entire Comet fleet was grounded permanently, and the investigation moved to the Royal Aircraft Establishment, a government research institution at Farnborough, in England, working under its director, Sir Arnold Hall. A formal Court of Inquiry, chaired by Lord Cohen, opened soon afterward to examine both the Elba and Naples accidents together, since investigators suspected from early on that the two losses shared a common cause rather than being two unrelated failures.

Investigators spent months trawling the seabed off Elba, and starting on the twelfth of February, 1954, began recovering pieces of G-ALYP from the ocean floor. Royal Navy vessels assisted in the recovery effort, dragging the seabed off Elba over many weeks to bring up as much of the wreckage as possible. Those fragments were laid out and reassembled on a hangar floor at Farnborough, piece by piece, in a reconstruction method that was unusually thorough for its time, treating the broken aircraft almost like physical evidence at a crime scene rather than simple wreckage to be cleared away, and it became a model for how later air accident investigations around the world were conducted.

Alongside that physical reconstruction, the Farnborough engineers ran a separate experiment on a sister aircraft, a Comet registered G-ALYU. They placed the entire fuselage inside a specially built water tank and repeatedly pressurized and depressurized it using water rather than air, which made it possible to simulate an enormous number of flights without the danger or expense of doing it with compressed air, since water does not expand violently the way a failing pressurized gas would. Engineers could run many cycles in a single day inside that tank, compressing years of an airliner's working life into a few months of continuous testing. Each pressurization cycle inside the tank stood in for one flight. The aircraft being tested, G-ALYU, had already flown over a thousand real passenger flights before the test began, and the Farnborough team continued pressurizing it in the tank on top of that real flight history. After a little over three thousand of those combined real and simulated cycles, the fuselage of G-ALYU burst open in the tank, far earlier than its designers had expected, since the Comet's structure had been assumed capable of surviving many times that number of pressurization cycles before any sign of weakening.

[LABEL: fatigue crack]

The popular story that gets repeated today says the Comet crashed because its square passenger windows cracked under pressure, and that version is close enough to the truth to be useful, but it is not quite what the wreckage showed. The records from the Farnborough investigation and the reconstructed fuselage point most directly to a rectangular opening in the roof of the cabin, the cutout built to house an automatic direction finder aerial, a piece of radio navigation equipment, rather than to a passenger window beside a seat. Some accounts from engineers involved in the investigation instead point to a similar rectangular cutout nearby, an escape hatch or observation dome in the roof. The exact opening has been argued over for decades, and this script will not pretend to settle it. What is well documented, and agreed on across the investigation, is that the failure began at a sharp inside corner of a rectangular cutout in the pressurized fuselage, at a rivet hole where a crack had quietly been growing for a long time before anyone noticed.

[LABEL: rivet hole]

That is the detail worth sitting with. The flaw that doomed those flights was never really about which specific opening started the crack. It was about the shape shared by every square or near-square opening cut into that pressurized metal skin, including the passenger windows, which is why, once the problem was understood, de Havilland changed every one of them.

[4. THE REAL REASON]

The real reason comes down to a basic and slightly unglamorous fact about how metal behaves under repeated stress, something engineers already knew about in theory but had not yet reckoned with at the scale a pressurized jet airliner demanded.

[LABEL: stress concentration]

Picture pulling on a stretched sheet of plastic wrap with both hands. If the sheet is smooth and unbroken, the pulling force spreads evenly across the whole surface and nothing happens. Now picture a tiny notch cut into one edge of that same sheet. Pull again, and the sheet does not tear evenly along its whole width, it tears starting exactly at the notch, almost instantly, because the force that was spread across the whole sheet is suddenly forced to squeeze through that one small point. Engineers call this effect stress concentration, the tendency of force to pile up disproportionately around any sharp corner, hole, or notch in a loaded material, rather than spreading out evenly the way it would across a smooth, continuous surface.

A sharp corner cut into a sheet of aluminum behaves the same way as that notch in the plastic wrap, just with far higher forces involved and far less obvious drama. When the Comet's engineers cut nearly square openings into the pressurized fuselage for windows, hatches, and the automatic direction finder aerial, the inside corners of those openings, no matter how small the radius, became places where the stress inside the metal was many times higher than in the flat sheet around them, even though the overall pressure load on the fuselage was perfectly normal and well within the aircraft's design limits.

[LABEL: pressurization cycle]

[LABEL: cyclic stress]

To picture why the corner matters so much more than the flat metal around it, imagine the stress inside a loaded sheet of metal as something like water flowing through a wide, shallow riverbed. Across most of the sheet, that flow spreads out evenly, moving at a gentle, manageable pace. The moment the riverbed narrows, at a sharp corner or a drilled hole, all that same flow has to squeeze through a much smaller channel, so its speed, and its force, rises sharply right at that pinch point. A smoothly curved corner widens the channel gradually, the way a river bends around a slow curve, while a sharp right-angle corner narrows it almost instantly, the way a river suddenly narrows between two cliffs. The metal does not fail where the overall load is highest. It fails where the load has been squeezed into the smallest possible space.

That would not have mattered much for a single flight. The danger came from repetition. Every time the Comet climbed to altitude and the cabin pressurized, the metal around those corners stretched slightly more than the metal around them, and every time it descended and depressurized, it relaxed again, over and over, thousands of times across the aircraft's working life. Metal subjected to that kind of repeated flexing, even flexing far too small to notice or measure by eye, gradually develops microscopic cracks at exactly the points where stress concentrates most. Engineers call this process metal fatigue, the slow weakening and eventual fracture of a material caused not by a single overwhelming force but by a great many small, repeated cycles of stress.

[LABEL: crack propagation]

Once a fatigue crack starts at one of those corners, it does not stay small. Each additional pressurization cycle makes the existing crack slightly longer, and a longer crack concentrates even more stress at its tip than a short one did, so the crack grows faster and faster the longer it survives, a process engineers call crack propagation. On the Comet, that crack grew silently inside the fuselage skin for what was probably hundreds of flights, invisible from outside the aircraft and undetectable by the inspection methods available at the time, until it finally reached a length where the weakened skin could no longer hold the pressure difference between the cabin and the thin air outside, and the fuselage failed catastrophically, all at once, in the air.

The practical fix, once Farnborough identified the mechanism, was almost embarrassingly simple to describe, even though it required rebuilding a large part of the airplane to apply. Round off every corner. A smooth, continuously curving edge, with no sharp corner anywhere along it, does not create a concentrated point for stress to pile up, it spreads the same load evenly around the whole curve, the way the unbroken plastic wrap spread the pulling force evenly before the notch was cut into it. That is the entire engineering idea behind the oval airplane window: not aerodynamics, not appearance, but a shape with nowhere for structural stress to concentrate and start the kind of crack that grows, unseen, flight after flight, until it does not.

[5. WHAT IF]

[LABEL: design life]

It is worth asking what might have happened if de Havilland and the Royal Aircraft Establishment had not traced the Comet's accidents back to window and cutout shape in 1954. The jet age was still in its first years, and other manufacturers were watching the Comet closely, both as a commercial rival and as a design reference. If the stress concentration problem had gone unexplained, or had been blamed permanently on weather or pilot error, as the early investigation into the Calcutta accident briefly suggested, it is plausible that square or near-square windows could have persisted into the first generation of American jets as well, simply because nobody outside de Havilland and the Royal Aircraft Establishment had the wreckage, the water tank data, or the forensic hangar reconstruction needed to connect the pattern. Given how many more jets, and how many more pressurization cycles, were about to enter service through the rest of the nineteen fifties and sixties as commercial aviation expanded, a problem left unsolved at this scale could have taken a far higher toll before anyone solved it independently.

It is also worth resisting the temptation to tell this story as a simple morality tale about a single doomed shape. Square and rectangular openings are not inherently dangerous in a pressurized aircraft, and they are still used today, in cargo doors, in overwing emergency exits, and in cockpit windshields, which are nearly rectangular on most modern jets. What changed after 1954 was not a blanket ban on corners, but a far more disciplined approach to every corner that remained: generous curved radii at every inside edge, reinforced doublers around cutouts, closer attention to rivet hole placement, and far more rigorous fatigue testing before an aircraft ever carried a passenger. The Comet's real legacy was not that square shapes are evil and round shapes are safe. It was that no shape, square or otherwise, should go into a pressurized fuselage without being tested for how it behaves after tens of thousands of repeated stress cycles, not just one.

[LABEL: fail-safe structure]

That more rigorous standard, sometimes called fail-safe structural design, where an aircraft is built so that a single crack or fatigue failure in one area does not bring down the whole structure, became standard practice across the aviation industry in the years that followed, directly because of what Farnborough found inside that water tank.

There is also a harder question worth asking honestly: did the Comet's accidents cost de Havilland the jet airliner market it had helped create. The grounding in 1954 cost the company roughly two years of lost momentum at exactly the moment American manufacturers were developing their own jets, and by the time the redesigned Comet 4 reached the skies in 1958, Boeing's new 707 was close behind it. It would be too simple to say stress concentration alone decided that commercial outcome, since cost, range, and passenger capacity mattered just as much, but the delay certainly did not help a British company trying to stay ahead of rivals with far larger home markets.

[6. TODAY]

The next time it is possible to look closely at an airplane window while boarding or settling into a seat, the whole history of the Comet is sitting right there in the shape of the frame. The pane itself is oval, not round, slightly taller than it is wide, with a continuous curve running all the way around it and no corner anywhere for stress to gather. Look at the frame around that pane too, which is usually thicker and more heavily reinforced than the surrounding wall panel, a detail that traces directly back to the doublers and reinforcement rings engineers started adding around every cutout once the stress concentration problem was understood.

[LABEL: aluminum skin]

[LABEL: corner radius]

It is also worth noticing the other openings nearby. The emergency exit over the wing, if a seat happens to be near one, is a larger rectangle, but look closely at its corners, and they will show a deliberately generous curve, not a sharp right angle, for exactly the same reason the window beside it is oval. The same logic shows up again at the cockpit windshield near the front of the aircraft, which looks almost rectangular from outside but always carries a rounded corner radius built into its frame rather than a true ninety-degree angle. None of these are accidents of styling. Every curved corner on a modern jet is a small, quiet monument to a lesson learned from a handful of wrecked Comets scattered across the floor of the Mediterranean Sea in 1954.

Airplane manufacturers also inherited a second habit from this episode that has nothing to do with shape directly. Modern aircraft are now subject to extensive, scheduled structural inspection programs that specifically hunt for the earliest stages of fatigue cracking around rivet holes, cutouts, and joints, long before a crack could ever grow large enough to threaten the fuselage. That inspection discipline, as much as the rounded window itself, is a direct descendant of the water tank at Farnborough and the fuselage of G-ALYU bursting open after roughly three thousand simulated flights.

[LABEL: structural inspection]

There is a smaller detail too, easy to miss unless it has already been pointed out: a tiny hole, usually called a breather hole, near the bottom of most cabin windows, between the inner and outer panes. That hole exists to balance air pressure between the layers of the window itself, so that only the outer pane has to bear the full force of the pressure difference at any moment, and it is a direct engineering descendant of the same nineteen fifties realization that pressurized openings need to be treated with far more care than a static load calculation alone would suggest. Window shape and window construction both trace back to the same handful of accidents and the same water tank at Farnborough.

None of this requires any special knowledge to notice. It only requires knowing, the next time a thumb absentmindedly traces the edge of that oval frame somewhere over an ocean, that the curve under that thumb was paid for by investigators piecing together wreckage on a hangar floor, and by a fuselage bursting open in a tank of water, so that every curve since would not have to be paid for the same way again.

[7. CLOSE]

The de Havilland Comet was eventually redesigned, with oval windows, a strengthened fuselage, and thicker skin around every opening, and reentered service as the Comet 4 in 1958, flying the first transatlantic jet passenger service for BOAC later that same year. But the underlying lesson it taught the entire aviation industry has shown up again and again in engineering, far outside airplanes.

During the Second World War, some welded Liberty ships, mass-produced cargo vessels built in a hurry, cracked and occasionally broke apart at sea, and investigators traced some of those failures partly to sharp square corners at hatch openings in the hull, the same stress concentration problem the Comet would face a decade later, just in steel at sea instead of aluminum in the sky. Long before either of those, railway engineers learned that square-edged axles and shafts failed from fatigue far sooner than shafts with smoothly curved transitions between different diameters, a lesson that reshaped how machine parts are still designed today. And in a far smaller, far less dangerous way, the glass screens on modern phones and the cases that hold them are now almost always built with rounded corners rather than sharp right angles, partly because a sharp inside corner in rigid material is still, after a century of engineering experience, one of the easiest places for a crack to start.

The details change. The pattern does not. A small shape, repeated thousands of times under stress, can turn out to matter far more than anyone expected when it was first drawn on paper.

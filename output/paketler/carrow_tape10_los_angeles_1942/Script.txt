I want to describe the shape itself a little further, because the folder includes a magnified reproduction of just that portion of the uncropped print, prepared decades later once the technology existed to enlarge it without losing the image entirely to grain. At that magnification, the shape resolves into something closer to a flattened oval than a sphere, with a faint darker band running roughly through its widest point, a detail that, once again, I cannot help noticing echoes a pattern I have shown you on more than one earlier tape without ever being able to tell you decisively what that pattern actually is.

The second item is a radar operator's log from a coastal defense station, tracking an unidentified target approaching from over open water in the hours before the alarm was sounded, at a speed the log describes as "inconsistent with surface vessel, inconsistent with weather phenomena, holding formation and bearing with unusual steadiness." The target, according to the log, slowed and nearly stopped as it neared the coastline, before the visual sighting and subsequent gunfire began, a detail that does not match the behavior of a loose weather balloon drifting on the wind, which does not hold a steady bearing and does not, on its own, slow down as it approaches land.

I want to add that this particular radar station's equipment had, according to a separate maintenance log included in the same folder, been inspected and calibrated only five days before this event, specifically in response to the heightened alert following the attack eleven weeks earlier, which I raise because it removes one of the easiest explanations available to me — a station already known to be malfunctioning, producing an unreliable reading that any careful investigator would discount. This was, by every maintenance record I can find, a freshly serviced station reporting a clean, consistent, anomalous track, which is a considerably harder thing to wave away than an old set of equipment nobody trusted in the first place.

The third item is a civil defense warden's statement, given the following day, describing the object visible in the searchlight beams not as a steady point of light but as something that "seemed to flicker and change shape slightly as the lights found it, the way a moth looks different depending on which way its wings are turned," a detail he volunteered without being asked to characterize the shape at all, simply because, in his own words, "it seemed important that whoever read this understood I wasn't looking at a fixed thing."

The fifth item is a tally this office's predecessor apparently compiled some time after the event, counting the ordnance expended that night against any confirmed wreckage recovered afterward. Over fourteen hundred rounds, fired over roughly an hour, by multiple batteries independently reporting the same target, against a result the tally records in three words: "no aircraft recovered." I have read a great many disproportionate numbers across this drawer. I do not think I have read one quite this disproportionate before.

There is a sixth item I want to describe, a set of civilian-submitted photographs beyond the single famous one, collected by this office's predecessor in the weeks afterward through what the folder describes only as "routine solicitation of the public record." One, taken from a rooftop several blocks from the published photograph's vantage point, shows a second, independent cluster of beams converging on a separate point in the sky at what the accompanying note calculates as roughly the same moment, suggesting, if the timing holds, that whatever was being tracked that night may have been moving between two widely separated points faster than any aircraft of the era could plausibly travel, or that more than one target was being tracked simultaneously by crews who had no way of communicating with each other in real time about what the other was seeing.

I want to say something about the gun crews and radar operators themselves, because I think this file treats them with more sympathy than most retellings of this night manage. These were trained men, under real and recent threat, doing a job they had rehearsed and expected, within weeks of taking up their posts, to actually need. I want to describe the broader command response that night as well, because I think it is easy to imagine a single battery firing blindly and lose sight of how coordinated, by the standards of the time, the overall response actually was. Multiple batteries across the metropolitan area reported the same general target within minutes of each other, coordinated through a central command post that logged each engagement with a timestamp, and that log, included in this folder, shows a pattern of engagement, cease-fire, and re-engagement over the course of roughly an hour that reads less like panic and more like a command structure repeatedly believing it had re-acquired a real target worth engaging again. Panic does not usually organize itself into that kind of repeated, coordinated pattern across multiple independent units reporting to a single post. Genuine, if mistaken, tracking of something does.

I want to add a detail about one battery commander specifically, because his own report stands out from the others in its restraint. Rather than claiming a confirmed hit or a confirmed enemy craft, he wrote only that his unit had "engaged a target believed hostile, results unconfirmed, no debris recovered within our sector," a sentence that reads, eleven weeks after Pearl Harbor and two days after an actual enemy shelling of this same coastline, as a genuinely cautious statement from a man who had every emotional reason available to him that night to claim a confirmed kill and did not take it.

A short digression, because the official explanation for this night has shifted more than once over the years, and I think the shifting itself is worth examining as closely as any single theory within it.

In the days immediately following, one senior naval official stated publicly that the entire episode had been a false alarm, attributable to war nerves, and nothing more. A separate, more detailed later review proposed that a stray weather balloon, possibly combined with the glare of the searchlights themselves and the understandable anxiety of gun crews primed to expect an attack, was sufficient to explain the sighting, the prolonged barrage, and the lack of any recovered wreckage. I want to give this explanation its full and genuine consideration, because I think war nerves were real, and widespread, and entirely capable of turning an ambiguous light into a fully formed enemy aircraft in the mind of a frightened, undertrained observer.

What this explanation struggles with, in my reading, is scale and duration. A single misidentified balloon does not usually hold multiple independent radar stations' attention long enough, or consistently enough, to produce over an hour of sustained fire from multiple batteries that, by every account, believed they were tracking and periodically re-acquiring the same target rather than firing blindly into open sky. Nor does a balloon account for the radar log's own description of a target that slowed, held bearing, and behaved, in the log's own careful phrase, in a manner "inconsistent with weather phenomena." I think the war-nerves explanation accounts for a great deal of the human reaction that night. I do not think it accounts for the instrument readings that preceded any human reaction at all.

I want to add one further consideration, because I think fairness requires it. Multiple independent radar stations operating with the technology available in nineteen forty-two were genuinely prone to a range of errors that later decades of refinement would eliminate — temperature inversions, flocks of birds, even, in some documented cases from the same period elsewhere, returns bounced off distant terrain under unusual atmospheric conditions. I do not dismiss the possibility that some combination of these ordinary technical limitations contributed to what the operators believed they were tracking that night. I simply do not think it explains all of it, particularly the visual corroboration from multiple separated vantage points describing a consistent, if ambiguous, shape rather than a shifting artifact of the kind these technical errors usually produce.

I want to add one further comparison, because I think it is useful in weighing this file against the others I have shown you. A balloon theory requires, essentially, a single ordinary object and a chain of independent human and mechanical errors compounding on top of it — the radar misreading its behavior, multiple visual witnesses misreading its shape in a consistent way, multiple separate gun crews each independently deciding to engage it and keep re-engaging it for the better part of an hour. I have seen this exact pattern before in this drawer, on the file about a hillside in West Virginia, where an owl was asked to explain a comparably long chain of independent, compounding misidentifications, and I did not find it persuasive there either. I am applying the same standard here that I applied then, and I am reaching the same kind of conclusion for largely the same reason: a single mundane cause rarely survives contact with this many independent, mutually reinforcing details for this long without the ordinary explanation starting to strain under its own weight.

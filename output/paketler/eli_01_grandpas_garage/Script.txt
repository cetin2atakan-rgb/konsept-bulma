[1. HOOK]
Your grandfather fixed his own engine with a five dollar stick.
Today, a shop would charge you hundreds of dollars for the same job.
It is a wooden stick with a rubber suction cup on the end, and if you found one in a drawer, you would probably throw it away.
That stick, and twenty-four other tools just as strange, kept American cars, tractors and farms running for half a century.
Most of them disappeared without anyone noticing.
Some of them are still the best tool for the job.
Today we are going to open the drawers of an old American garage, one tool at a time, and I will tell you exactly what each one was for.
Let's start with that stick.

[25. Valve Lapping Stick]
It is called a valve lapping stick, and it was used to make an engine breathe properly again.
Inside every engine, the valves close against metal seats thousands of times a minute.
Over the years, those surfaces get pitted and stop sealing, and the engine loses power.
The fix was slow and simple.
A mechanic smeared a gritty paste called lapping compound on the valve, pressed the suction cup onto its face, and spun the stick back and forth between his palms like a man trying to start a fire.
Every few turns he lifted it, checked the gray ring forming on the metal, and kept going until that ring was even all the way around.
A whole set of valves could take an afternoon.
Machine shops do this with grinders now, but you can still buy a lapping stick for a few dollars, and old engine builders still swear by it.

[24. Spark Plug Cleaner]
At a lot of service stations in the forties and fifties, there was a small metal cabinet on the counter with a rubber hole in the front.
It was a spark plug cleaner.
The attendant pushed a dirty plug into the hole, pressed a lever, and a blast of air carrying fine abrasive grit scoured the carbon off the tip.
Many of these machines also had a little window and a pressure test, so you could watch the plug spark under compression before it went back in the engine.
Cleaning and regapping plugs was cheaper than buying new ones, and a careful driver might reuse the same set several times.
Modern plugs last a hundred thousand miles, so the cabinet quietly vanished from the counter.

[23. Feeler Gauge]
In the back pocket of almost every mechanic was a little fan of thin steel blades, each one stamped with a number.
That was a feeler gauge.
Each blade is a precise thickness, measured in thousandths of an inch.
You slid them into a gap until one fit with a slight drag, and that told you exactly how wide the gap was.
On older engines you used it constantly: spark plug gaps, breaker point gaps, and the clearance on valves that had to be adjusted by hand.
Get the gap wrong by a few thousandths and the engine would idle rough, ping, or burn a valve.
It is one of the few tools on this list that never went away, because nothing measures a tiny gap more simply.

[22. Ignition Points File]
Next to the feeler gauge, you would often find a tiny flat file, no bigger than a nail file.
It was for the ignition points.
Before the mid nineteen seventies, most cars fired their spark plugs using a set of small metal contacts that opened and closed inside the distributor.
Every time they opened, the coil sent a spark.
Those contacts slowly burned and pitted, and when the car started to miss, a driver would pop the distributor cap, run that little file across the points to clean them up, and reset the gap with the feeler gauge.
It got many people home from a breakdown on the side of the road.
Electronic ignition replaced points across Detroit in the nineteen seventies, and the points file became a mystery to anyone younger.

[21. Timing Light]
It looked like a ray gun from an old science fiction movie, with a pistol grip and wires running to the battery.
It was a timing light.
You clipped one lead to the number one spark plug wire, pointed the light at the spinning pulley on the front of the engine, and pulled the trigger.
Every time that cylinder fired, the light flashed, and the flash froze a painted mark on the pulley so it looked like it was standing still.
Then you turned the distributor by hand until the mark lined up with the right number on a little scale.
That was how you set ignition timing, and it made the difference between an engine that pulled hard and one that knocked going up hills.

[20. Vacuum Gauge]
A round dial with a rubber hose was one of the cheapest diagnostic tools a man could own.
It was a vacuum gauge.
You connected the hose to the intake manifold and started the engine.
On a healthy engine at idle near sea level, the needle sat steady, usually somewhere around seventeen to twenty-one inches.
What made it powerful was the way the needle moved.
A needle that dropped every few seconds pointed to a sticking valve.
A slow drift could mean a carburetor problem, and a low, steady reading suggested worn rings or late timing.
An experienced mechanic could read that needle the way a doctor reads a pulse, and many old timers still keep one in the drawer.

[19. Compression Tester]
This one is a gauge on the end of a short hose with a threaded tip.
It is a compression tester.
You pulled a spark plug, screwed the tip into the empty hole, and had someone crank the engine a few times.
The gauge showed how much pressure that cylinder could build.
Then you did the same on every cylinder and compared the numbers.
If one cylinder was much lower than the others, you knew there was trouble inside that one: a burned valve, a broken ring, or a leaking head gasket.
It let a mechanic look inside an engine without taking it apart, and it is still one of the first tests a good mechanic runs today.

[18. Mechanic's Stethoscope]
It looks exactly like a doctor's stethoscope, except for a long metal probe where the chest piece should be.
It is a mechanic's stethoscope.
An engine makes dozens of sounds at once, and it is hard to tell where a knock or a tick is coming from.
With the probe pressed against different parts of a running engine, the sound of each part came straight up into your ears.
A bad bearing growls.
A loose lifter ticks.
A failing water pump whines.
Before that, many old mechanics did the same thing with a long screwdriver, pressing the tip to the engine and the handle to their ear.

[17. Grease Gun and Zerk Fittings]
Under an older car you would find dozens of little nipples screwed into the steering and suspension joints.
Those are zerk fittings, and the tool that fed them was the lever-action grease gun.
The fitting was patented by an inventor named Oscar Zerk around nineteen twenty-nine, and it changed car maintenance.
Every thousand miles or so, owners were told to have the chassis lubricated.
The mechanic pressed the gun onto each fitting and pumped until fresh grease pushed the old dirty grease out of the joint.
It was called a lube job, and it was part of every oil change.
Most modern cars use sealed joints with no fittings at all, but tractors, trailers and farm equipment are still covered in them.

[16. Battery Hydrometer]
It looked like a turkey baster with a little float sealed inside the glass.
It was a battery hydrometer.
Old car batteries had removable caps on top, and you could draw up a little of the liquid inside each cell.
The float rose or sank depending on how strong the acid was, and the reading told you how charged that cell was.
A fully charged cell read around one point two six five.
If one cell read much lower than the others, the battery was failing, even if the car still started that morning.
Most batteries today are sealed, so there is nothing to draw out, but people who maintain old trucks and tractors still use one.

[15. Antifreeze Tester]
Right next to the battery hydrometer, there was a second one that looked almost the same, except it had a row of little colored balls inside.
It tested antifreeze.
You drew coolant up from the radiator and counted how many balls floated.
More floating balls meant more protection against freezing.
In the northern states, this was a ritual every fall, because a frozen engine could crack its block overnight and ruin the whole car.
The gas station attendant would test your radiator and tell you how cold it could get before you were in trouble.
It is still sold today for a few dollars, and it is still one of the smartest things to check before the first hard freeze.

[14. Wheel Bearing Packer]
A cone-shaped tool with a grease fitting on top doesn't look like much, but it saved a lot of greasy hands.
It was a wheel bearing packer.
Older cars and trailers ran on tapered roller bearings that had to be repacked with fresh grease every so often.
The old way was to push grease into the bearing with your palm until it came out the other side, and it took a while to learn.
With the packer, you set the bearing between two cones and pumped the grease gun, and grease was forced through every roller at once.
If you have a boat trailer, you probably still need one, because those bearings live in water and fail quietly.

[13. Brake Adjusting Spoon]
This is a flat piece of steel with a slight bend at the end, like a small spoon that someone stepped on.
It is a brake adjusting spoon.
Older drum brakes had a little toothed wheel inside the brake called a star adjuster.
As the brake shoes wore down, the pedal sank lower and lower, and someone had to take up the slack.
You pulled a rubber plug from the back of the brake, slid the spoon in, and clicked the star wheel a few teeth at a time until the wheel just started to drag.
Later drum brakes adjusted themselves when you backed up, and most modern cars have disc brakes in front, so the spoon became a puzzle in the bottom of the toolbox.

[12. Piston Ring Compressor]
It is a band of thin spring steel with a ratchet and a little key.
It is a piston ring compressor.
Every piston has springy rings around it that seal against the cylinder wall.
To put a piston back into an engine, those rings have to be squeezed flat, or they catch on the edge of the cylinder and snap.
You wrapped the band around the piston, tightened it with the key, set it on top of the cylinder, and tapped the piston down with the wooden handle of a hammer.
If you have ever rebuilt an engine on a garage floor, you have used one, and you remember the first ring you broke.

[11. Tire Bead Breaker]
On farms and in country garages, you would find a heavy steel lever with a hooked foot, or a stand you hit with a sledgehammer.
It was a tire bead breaker.
A tire seals against the wheel at a thick edge called the bead, and after years on the road it bonds to the rim almost like glue.
Before tire machines were everywhere, getting a tire off meant breaking that seal by hand, one hard stroke at a time.
Truck and tractor tires were the worst.
Men who did it for years still talk about how many knuckles it cost them.

[10. Hot Patch Vulcanizer]
Back when cars ran on inner tubes, a flat tire was something you fixed on the side of the road.
One of the strangest ways to do it was a hot patch.
The kit came with a small clamp and patches that had a little metal tray of fuel on the back.
You clamped the patch over the hole in the tube and lit the fuel with a match.
It burned for a minute or two, and the heat melted the patch rubber into the tube and sealed it.
When it cooled, the repair was almost as strong as the tube itself.
It sounds dangerous, and it was a little, but it got a lot of families home.

[9. Bumper Jack]
If you ever changed a flat on an older car, you remember this one.
It was a tall notched steel post with a ratchet that hooked under the bumper.
You set the base on the ground, slipped the hook under the bumper, and worked a handle up and down until the car rose one notch at a time.
It was also one of the most dangerous tools ever put in a trunk.
On soft ground or a slope, the whole car could slide off the post.
As bumpers changed in the nineteen seventies and eighties, carmakers switched to jacks that lift from the frame.
Old timers still tell you never to put any part of your body under a car on a bumper jack.

[8. Hand Crank]
Before electric starters, every car came with an iron crank that fit into the front of the engine.
To start the car, you set the controls, then grabbed the crank and gave it a hard pull upward.
The danger was kickback.
If the engine fired early, the crank could spin backward hard enough to break a wrist.
That is why old timers taught everyone to keep the thumb on the same side as the fingers, instead of wrapping it around the handle.
The electric self-starter arrived on the nineteen twelve Cadillac and spread quickly, but tractors and stationary engines kept their cranks for decades.

[7. Pump Oiler]
A small metal can with a long spout and a thumb lever on the side sat on almost every workbench.
It was a pump oiler.
Instead of tipping and pouring, you aimed the spout and pressed the lever, and a few drops went exactly where you wanted them.
Hinges, chains, sewing machines, clippers, the moving parts of farm tools, all got a drop or two from the same can.
A good one never leaked and lasted a lifetime.
Spray cans took over in later decades, but plenty of men still prefer the oiler, because spray goes everywhere and a drop goes where you point it.

[6. Draw Knife]
It is a long blade with a handle on each end, sharpened on one side.
It is a draw knife.
You sat on a bench, clamped a piece of wood in front of you, and pulled the blade toward your body to peel off long curls of wood.
It was how axe handles, fence posts, wagon parts and tool handles were shaped by hand.
In the right hands, it could take bark off a log in minutes.
Woodworkers still use them today, and once you try one, it is hard not to smile at how fast it works.

[5. Spokeshave]
The draw knife had a little brother.
It is a short blade set in a body with a handle on each side, and it is called a spokeshave.
It was originally made for shaping wagon wheel spokes, and that is where the name comes from.
Where the draw knife took off big curls, the spokeshave took off thin shavings and left a smooth, round surface.
Carpenters used it for chair legs, paddles, and the curved handles of tools.
It is still made today, and it is still the best tool for shaping a round piece of wood by hand.

[4. Brace and Bit]
Before cordless drills, this was how a carpenter made a hole.
It is a crank-shaped handle with a pad on top and a chuck on the end, called a brace.
You pressed your chest or your hand on the top pad, turned the crank, and the bit bored through wood slowly and cleanly.
The better ones had a ratchet, so you could work in a tight corner by swinging the handle back and forth instead of turning it all the way around.
A brace never needed a battery, never overheated, and gave you a feel for the wood that a power drill never does.
Plenty of old boxes at farm auctions still have one in the bottom.

[3. Post Drill]
In some farm shops, there was a drill press that ran on muscle.
It was called a post drill, and it was bolted to a wall or a wooden post.
You turned a big hand wheel on the side, and a set of gears turned the drill bit and fed it slowly down into the metal.
A heavy flywheel kept it turning smoothly between strokes.
With one of these, a farmer could drill holes in iron and steel for repairs, years before the farm had electricity.
Today they sell to collectors, and some of them still work as well as the day they left the factory.

[2. Fence Stretcher]
On any farm with livestock, one tool sat near the door because it was needed so often.
It was the fence stretcher.
It hooked to the wire on one end and to a post or a chain on the other, and with a lever or a ratchet, you pulled the wire tight before you stapled it.
A loose fence lets cattle walk right through, so getting the wire tight mattered.
The design has hardly changed in a century, and every farmer you ask still has one hanging somewhere.

[1. Treadle Grindstone]
And the most important tool in the old farm garage didn't run on gas or electricity at all.
It was a big round sandstone wheel, set in a wooden frame, turned by a foot pedal or a hand crank.
It was the grindstone.
The bottom of the wheel ran through a trough of water, which kept the stone clean and the blade cool.
Axes, hatchets, scythes, knives and hoes all came back to life on that wheel.
On many farms, it was the job of the youngest boy to turn the crank while his father held the blade.
Ask anyone who grew up that way, and they will tell you they can still hear the sound of it.

[1. HOOK + PERSONA INTRO]

Southeast Oklahoma is hiding far more than pine ridges and quiet county roads. Here the Ouachita Mountains run east to west, against the grain of almost every other range in America. A reservoir here is still tangled in federal water-rights litigation. A county cemetery holds elephants carved into tombstones. A sandstone slab with runic letters has puzzled scholars for seventy years, and a cave in the Winding Stair country sheltered outlaws that history still argues about. Today we'll count down twenty-five remarkable geographic facts about Southeast Oklahoma, from the Ouachita high country down through the Kiamichi and Red River valleys.

I'm Walter Hale. I taught geography for thirty-eight years in a small high school in Kentucky, and I still can't drive through a county without wondering why it looks the way it does. Let's start at number twenty-five.

[FACT 25: Circus City, USA | 34.05,-95.48]

Fact twenty-five.

Drive into Hugo, the seat of Choctaw County in the far southeastern corner of the state, and you will find a town that calls itself Circus City, USA, a nickname most visitors never expect from a farm town near the Red River. In the late 1930s, a local businessman named Vernon Pratt is credited with convincing the Miller family's circus to winter its show in Hugo instead of Mena, Arkansas, and the idea stuck. Over the following decades, at various points as many as twenty circuses based their winter quarters around the town, and two of them, Kelly-Miller and Carson and Barnes, still do. The clearest evidence of that history sits inside Mount Olivet Cemetery, in a section called Showmen's Rest. Set aside around 1960 to honor circus performers and crew, the plot is marked by monuments topped with carved elephants, including one large central stone showing a performing elephant balanced on two feet. Ringmasters, elephant trainers, and sideshow performers are buried there under stage names as often as their legal ones. One grave belongs to a trainer known as Elephant John, who reportedly lived in the elephant barn rather than a house. I used to tell my students that every town has at least one fact that sounds invented until you check it, and Hugo's circus cemetery was always my favorite example. If you drive along United States Highway 70 into town today, you will still pass the Endangered Ark Foundation, home to one of the largest herds of Asian elephants left in the country, descendants of the same winter-quarters tradition that built this unlikely reputation in the first place.

[FACT 24: The World's Highest Hill | 35.05,-94.62]

Fact twenty-four.

On the edge of Poteau, the LeFlore County seat, a sign at the base of Cavanal Hill makes a strange claim: that this is the highest hill in the world. The sign lists the elevation as 1,999 feet, deliberately kept one foot under the 2,000-foot mark some use to separate a hill from a mountain, though surveys actually put the true summit closer to 2,385 feet, or about 727 meters, above sea level. The claim depends entirely on definition. The United States Board on Geographic Names once used 1,000 feet of local relief as the line between hill and mountain, but it abandoned that standard in the early 1970s, and no single rule has replaced it since. By some of those older definitions, Cavanal would qualify as a mountain outright, and it is in fact shorter than several other points in the state, including Sugar Loaf Mountain nearby. None of that has stopped Poteau from building its identity around the title. A paved road climbs to the summit, where a small monument and overlook let visitors take the photograph the town expects them to take. The hill's name comes from the French word for cavernous, a label eighteenth-century French traders in the area apparently found fitting for its shape. Geologists studying the wider Ouachita uplift note that several unnamed ridges nearby actually clear Cavanal's own summit by a comfortable margin, which makes the title more about marketing than measurement. Either way, the view from the top, looking out over the Poteau River valley and the pine ridges beyond, earns the drive regardless of what the sign at the bottom claims.

[FACT 23: The Storm Lost to the News | 34.30,-95.45]

Fact twenty-three.

Antlers, the Pushmataha County seat, carries a scar from April 12, 1945, that most of the country never heard about. A tornado, later rated an F5 on the Fujita scale after the fact, tore through town that afternoon, killing around sixty-nine people and injuring more than three hundred and fifty. Roughly a third of Antlers lay in ruins afterward, with about 375 homes and 250 other buildings destroyed along a damage path roughly half a mile wide. The United States Army sent troops from Camp Maxey in Texas to help with rescue and cleanup in the days that followed. What makes the story unusual is not just the death toll, which still ranks among the worst in Oklahoma history, but the timing. That same afternoon, President Franklin Delano Roosevelt died in Warm Springs, Georgia, and national newspapers gave almost all their attention to Washington instead of a small lumber town in the Kiamichi hills. Survivors in Antlers have said for decades that the rest of the country simply never learned what happened to them. The town rebuilt along the same grid of streets, and a memorial near the county courthouse now marks the anniversary each April. If you stop in Antlers today, the Pushmataha County Historical Museum, housed in a 1914 railroad depot, keeps photographs of the aftermath that the wire services of 1945 largely passed over. It is a reminder that even a storm powerful enough to flatten a town can still slip through the cracks of a single news cycle.

[FACT 22: A Small Park's Big Shadow | 34.55,-95.34]

Fact twenty-two.

South of the town of Clayton in Pushmataha County sits one of the smaller parks in the Oklahoma state system, built around a sixty-six-acre lake impounded in 1935 on Peal Creek. Clayton Lake State Park covers about 510 acres, with roughly two miles of shoreline, cabins, campsites, and a swimming beach that mostly draws local families rather than long-distance travelers. For one afternoon in April of 2024, though, this quiet park became one of the most valuable pieces of real estate in North America, because it sat squarely inside the path of totality for that year's total solar eclipse, with about one minute and twenty seconds of complete darkness at midday. I used to tell my students that geography decides who gets to see rare things and who does not, and a total eclipse is about as clean an example as exists, since the path of totality is only ever about a hundred miles wide as it crosses the continent. Clayton Lake happened to fall inside that narrow band purely because of where the moon's shadow traced its arc that day, not because of anything special about the park itself. Astronomers and amateur eclipse chasers who had tracked the math for years made a point of driving into rural Pushmataha County rather than staying in a larger Oklahoma city outside the band. The park's regular visitors, meanwhile, got a once-in-a-lifetime sky show on top of their usual weekend fishing trip. Clayton Lake will not sit inside a path of totality again for decades, which makes that April afternoon a strange kind of geographic lottery win for a park most Oklahomans could not otherwise place on a map.

[FACT 21: The Lawman's House on the Hill | 34.87,-94.65]

Fact twenty-one.

Southwest of Heavener, in LeFlore County, a modest two-story frame house has stood on a hill above Conser Creek since 1894. It belonged to Peter Conser, who served as chief of the Lighthorsemen for the Choctaw Nation's Mosholatubbee District, a position that functioned much like a county sheriff does today, enforcing Choctaw law across a district that covered a large stretch of what is now northeastern LeFlore County. Conser later moved from law enforcement into Choctaw government itself, serving as a representative and then a senator in the Choctaw Council before Oklahoma statehood in 1907 folded the Nation's government into the new state. The house was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1971, and for years the Oklahoma Historical Society operated it as a small museum open to visitors who made the drive out from Heavener. Budget pressures eventually forced it to close, and more recent preservation surveys have listed the Conser House among the state's most endangered historic sites, its wood siding and foundation showing the wear of more than a century without steady upkeep. The house is easy to miss if you are not looking for it, tucked off a county road with no highway signage pointing travelers toward it. That quiet is itself part of the lesson. The Lighthorsemen were the Choctaw Nation's own law enforcement, operating years before Oklahoma had county sheriffs of its own, and Conser's house is one of the few physical reminders left standing of a tribal government that policed, legislated, and governed this corner of the Ouachita foothills on its own terms.

[FACT 20: The Stone Nobody Can Agree On | 34.89,-94.60]

Fact twenty.

In a park above the town of Heavener, a twelve-foot sandstone slab carries eight runic letters cut deep into its face, and almost nothing about it is settled. For close to a century, local residents simply called it Indian Rock and assumed Choctaw carvers had made the marks. Starting in the 1950s, a Heavener native named Gloria Farley began arguing instead that Norse explorers had carved the inscription in the eleventh century, and she spent decades bringing outside researchers to look at the stone. Professional opinion has mostly moved the other way since. A Canadian Parks Service archaeologist once dismissed it outright as a modern artifact, and a 2011 historical analysis concluded that nineteenth-century evidence points strongly to a Scandinavian immigrant carving it sometime after settlers arrived in the area, not a medieval Viking. A runic scholar from Uppsala University in Sweden later estimated only about a twenty percent chance the letters date to the tenth or eleventh century, favoring a nineteenth-century origin instead. Part of the disagreement comes from the letters themselves, which mix two different runic alphabets in a way that does not match known Viking-age inscriptions cleanly. Even the reading is disputed. One proposed translation renders the letters as a Scandinavian name and date, while a Smithsonian linguist once suggested the word GNOMEDAL, possibly meaning a sundial or monument valley. The state park built around the stone sides with the Viking theory in its signage, and in 2003 Oklahoma's tourism commission allowed archaeologists to search a collapsed cave nearby for any additional evidence. None of it has produced a final answer, and the Heavener Runestone remains exactly what it has been for a hundred years, a genuine mystery rather than a solved one.

[FACT 19: The Spring the Outlaws Trusted | 34.78,-94.78]

Fact nineteen.

High on the northern face of Winding Stair Mountain, a freshwater spring still runs more or less the way it did in the 1800s, when horse thieves used it as a reliable water source for camps and corrals hidden near the ridge top. The route that reaches it today, now called the Horsethief Springs Trail, follows parts of old logging roads built in the 1920s layered on top of the original outlaw path, and it connects to the longer Ouachita National Recreation Trail for a loop of roughly eleven to thirteen miles, depending on which stretch a hiker chooses. In the 1930s, a Civilian Conservation Corps crew is said to have built a stone structure around the spring itself, both to contain the water and to stop the surrounding slope from washing out, although no single government record pins down the exact year that work was finished. Trailheads sit at Cedar Lake and along the Talimena Scenic Byway, and guides describe the loop as one of the better places in Oklahoma to see fall color, since the elevation changes bring out hardwoods at a different pace than the lowland river valleys. The thieves did not choose this ridge because it was remote for its own sake. They chose it because fresh water was reliable there year-round, which made it one of the few places in the Winding Stair country where a hidden camp could actually last through a dry Oklahoma summer, long before anyone built a highway anywhere near it.

[FACT 18: The Cave of Uncertain Outlaws | 34.93,-95.24]

Fact eighteen.

Just north of Wilburton, in Latimer County, Robbers Cave State Park and its adjoining wildlife management area cover more than 8,000 acres combined, built around a sandstone cave that local tradition has linked to some of the biggest names in frontier outlawry. The park's own lore holds that Jesse James and Belle Starr both used the cave as a hideout, and Starr, sometimes called the Queen of the Oklahoma Outlaws, did live in a cabin roughly twenty miles to the north. The honest record is thinner than the legend. One careful review of the area's history found no documented evidence tying many of the robberies locals attribute to this stretch of hills to any specific named outlaw, which means the cave's reputation rests more on folklore passed down through generations than on court records or newspaper accounts from the time. The more believable part of the story is that Civil War deserters from both sides sheltered here, since the sandstone overhang offered exactly the kind of hidden cover they needed. Local tradition adds other famous names, including the Dalton Gang, the Younger brothers, and the Rufus Buck Gang, but those connections rest on the same thin paper trail as Jesse James. Today the park's lodge carries Belle Starr's name, and families come for the lake, the climbing routes up the sandstone, and hiking trails rather than for outlaw history specifically. A legend does not need to be entirely true to earn a place on the map, and in Robbers Cave's case, the story visitors repeat at the lodge each evening has outlasted whatever thin paper trail the actual robberies left behind.

[FACT 17: The Mound Builders Before the Mound Builders | 34.97,-94.80]

Fact seventeen.

Long before Spiro's traders built their famous ceremonial center, an earlier Woodland-period society lived across this same stretch of southeastern Oklahoma, leaving behind dark-earth mounds, distinctive pottery, and stone tools archaeologists now group under the name Fourche Maline culture, active from roughly 300 BCE to 800 CE. The name comes from Fourche Maline Creek, and researchers at the University of Oklahoma now trace the culture's modern descendants to today's Caddo Nation. Many of the clearest Fourche Maline sites once sat directly in the valley that became Lake Wister, fed by the Poteau River and Fourche Maline Creek itself, and when the reservoir was planned, teams working under federal relief programs in the 1930s and 1940s raced to excavate what they could before the water rose. A great deal of what they recovered has still never been fully analyzed, sitting in university collections rather than museum displays. Late in the culture's long run, its people began organizing the construction of early platform mounds at ceremonial gathering places, a practice that later Mississippian societies like Spiro would take much further. I used to tell my students that every famous ancient site has a quieter predecessor nobody put on a brochure, and the Fourche Maline culture is Oklahoma's clearest example. There is no single roadside marker for it the way there is for Spiro or the Heavener Runestone. Its evidence sits mostly underwater now, beneath the surface of a reservoir built to hold back floods, which is as fitting a metaphor for an overlooked culture as any geography teacher could ask for.

[FACT 16: The School Named for a Dartmouth Founder | 34.02,-94.90]

Fact sixteen.

Near the small community of Millerton in McCurtain County, several weathered brick and frame buildings mark what remains of Wheelock Academy, a boarding school for Choctaw girls that began in 1832 as a mission founded by Christian missionaries Alfred and Harriet Wright. Its name honors Eleazar Wheelock, the same man who founded what became Dartmouth College, a connection the missionaries chose deliberately when they named their new school in Indian Territory. In 1842, the Choctaw Nation's own Education Act made Wheelock the first academy the Nation funded directly, a model that eventually grew into roughly thirty-five academies and seminaries supported and controlled by the Five Tribes across Indian Territory. The National Park Service designated it a National Historic Landmark in the mid-1960s, and it was added to the National Register of Historic Places soon after. The school itself closed in 1955, and only a handful of its original buildings still stand, several of them in fragile condition despite the landmark status. The Choctaw Nation still owns the property and has restored one building, LeFlore Hall, into a small museum that offers free guided tours on weekdays. What strikes most visitors is how isolated the site feels today, a cluster of quiet buildings off a county road with no town built up around it, when for over a century it served as one of the most important Choctaw institutions in the region, educating generations of girls in a system the Nation ran largely on its own terms, separate from the federal boarding schools that operated elsewhere in Indian Territory.

[FACT 15: Where the Civil War Actually Ended | 34.02,-95.25]

Fact fifteen.

Most schoolbooks place the end of the Civil War at Appomattox in April of 1865, but the fighting did not stop everywhere that week. Near the ruins of Fort Towson, close to the old trading community of Doaksville in what is now Choctaw County, Confederate General Stand Watie held out for more than two additional months. Watie, a Cherokee leader who commanded the First Indian Brigade of the Confederacy's Army of the Trans-Mississippi, finally signed a cease-fire with Union representatives on June 23, 1865, seventy-five days after Robert E. Lee's surrender in Virginia. Several sources describe him as the last Confederate general in the field to give up his command, and the surrender that day also brought in Cherokee, Creek, and Seminole units, along with an Osage battalion that had fought alongside the Confederacy. Fort Towson itself had been a United States Army post since 1824, built to help keep peace along the border with Mexican Texas, and Doaksville grew up beside it as a trading town that briefly served as a Choctaw Nation capital under its 1850 constitution. Both the fort and the townsite are now preserved as a historic site managed by the Oklahoma Historical Society, with a historical marker describing the surrender near where it took place. Little of either Fort Towson or Doaksville survives above ground today, mostly stone foundations and interpretive signs scattered across open fields. History's official end dates are often just the point where historians in the East stopped paying attention, and the final page of the Civil War, as it turns out, was written in a Choctaw Nation trading town most Americans have never heard of.

[FACT 14: The Woodpecker's Last Stronghold | 34.45,-94.85]

Fact fourteen.

In northern McCurtain County, the McCurtain County Wilderness Area protects roughly 14,000 acres of shortleaf pine forest that was never logged, making it one of the largest stretches of virgin pine and hardwood forest left standing anywhere in the country. The Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation manages the tract, which also happens to be the oldest wildlife management area the state agency operates. What makes this forest matter beyond its age is a single endangered bird. A state wildlife survey decades ago found that the area held somewhere between 84 and 90 percent of Oklahoma's entire population of red-cockaded woodpeckers, and more recent counts suggest the share may now be even higher, since the species depends almost entirely on open, park-like stands of old-growth pine, the kind of habitat industrial timber operations across the rest of the Ouachita region eliminated decades ago by harvesting trees before they reached the age needed to develop the heart-rot cavities the woodpecker nests in. Wildlife officials describe the wilderness area as the species' last reliable foothold in the state, and management there focuses specifically on restoring the open pine and bluestem grass understory the birds need, through controlled burns rather than letting hardwood brush crowd the pines. Public access is limited by design. The tract is open mainly for controlled deer and turkey hunts rather than casual hiking, a restriction meant to keep disturbance to the woodpecker colonies low. Few visitors to Broken Bow Lake, just a short drive south, realize that one of the rarest birds in North America depends entirely on a patch of pine forest most of them will never see signposted from the highway.

[FACT 13: The Cold River Below the Dam | 34.15,-94.70]

Fact thirteen.

Below the dam that forms Broken Bow Lake, the Mountain Fork River runs cold and clear year-round, a condition almost entirely created by engineering rather than nature. Water released from deep in the reservoir stays near fifty degrees even in the middle of an Oklahoma summer, and that steady, cold flow is what allows the state to stock the river with trout in a part of the country where trout normally could not survive the heat. The upper river carries Oklahoma's own scenic river designation, a status meant to protect its undeveloped stretches from the kind of channelization and dam-building that reshaped most other Oklahoma waterways through the twentieth century. Canoeists and kayakers use the upper sections above the lake, where the water moves faster over gravel bars, while anglers concentrate below the dam near the community of Hochatown, a stretch now dense with cabins and outfitters built almost entirely around the fishing economy the cold tailwater created. None of this existed before 1970, when the dam and spillway were finished and Broken Bow Lake filled the valley to a depth of up to 180 feet in places. Just upstream, the river makes the sharp, nearly 180-degree bend that gave Beavers Bend State Park its name, a curve pronounced enough that early Choctaw and later American settlers used it as a landmark long before anyone built a dam nearby. A river that could not have supported trout a century ago now supports a trout-fishing economy almost entirely because of the dam a mile upstream, which is as direct a lesson in how engineering reshapes geography as this region offers.

[FACT 12: The Lake Still Being Fought Over | 34.62,-95.40]

Fact twelve.

Sardis Lake, built in the late 1970s and early 1980s in Latimer and Pushmataha counties as a municipal and industrial water reservoir, spent decades sitting at the center of one of the most significant water-rights disputes in Oklahoma history. The state borrowed heavily from the Army Corps of Engineers to build the dam, then stopped making payments on that debt in the 1980s, which eventually drew a federal lawsuit. In 2010, the Oklahoma Water Resources Board approved a deal that let Oklahoma City access as much as ninety percent of the lake's water in exchange for paying off the state's outstanding debt to the Corps. The Choctaw Nation and the Chickasaw Nation objected immediately, arguing that an 1830 treaty, the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, had already granted their governments authority over water resources across this part of their historic territory, and that the state had negotiated the deal without consulting them at all. Both nations sued within about a year of the agreement, the state countersued in 2012, and the two sides spent several more years in mediation before reaching a landmark settlement in 2016, later ratified by the United States Congress. Under that settlement, the state kept administrative authority over the lake, the tribes' treaty-based water rights were formally preserved rather than erased, and Oklahoma City still received a substantial share of the water, conditioned on conservation requirements. I used to tell my students that a map never shows who actually controls the water running across it, and Sardis Lake is one of the clearest American examples of that gap between a line on a map and the authority that line is assumed to represent.

[FACT 11: Oklahoma's Last Untamed River | 34.20,-94.90]

Fact eleven.

While nearly every other major river in southeastern Oklahoma has been dammed somewhere along its course, the Glover River in McCurtain County still runs free for its entire length, roughly 33 miles from its headwaters to its junction with the Little River near Wright City. Conservation groups describe it as the only river left in the state that remains undammed and largely undeveloped from source to mouth, a status that has made it a target for protective legislation more than once, including a 2022 bill that would have formally barred new dams or water transfers on the river. That bill stalled in the state legislature and never became law, which means the Glover's free-flowing status today rests more on circumstance than on any permanent legal shield. The river's character shifts sharply along its run. Its upper stretches cut through steep, high bluffs with Class I and II rapids that demand real paddling skill, while the lower river, floatable for most of the year, winds through flatter, more forgiving terrain as it nears the Little River. A private timber company, Weyerhaeuser, owns much of the land the river crosses, and paddlers need a land-access permit to cross that property legally, which keeps traffic on the Glover lighter than on more publicized Oklahoma rivers. Flash flooding is a genuine danger after heavy rain, and outfitters generally recommend floating the Glover only between October and June, since the upper river often runs too low to paddle at all by midsummer. For a river with no major town built directly on its banks and no dam anywhere along its length, the Glover remains one of the least visited, least altered waterways left in the entire state.

[FACT 10: The Park Named for a Choctaw Citizen | 34.13,-94.68]

Fact ten.

Beavers Bend State Park, built on the site of an older Choctaw settlement in McCurtain County, takes its name from John T. Beavers, a Choctaw intermarried citizen who once owned land along this stretch of the Mountain Fork River. Construction on the earth dam downstream began in 1961, and by 1970 both the dam and a separate concrete spillway were finished, allowing Broken Bow Lake to fill the river valley behind it to a depth of as much as 180 feet, or roughly 55 meters, in its deepest sections. The finished reservoir stretches across about 14,000 acres and runs some 22 miles through the Ouachita foothills of McCurtain County, making it one of the largest lakes in southeastern Oklahoma by surface area. The park built around its upper end predates the dam by decades. Established in 1937 with help from Civilian Conservation Corps labor crews, Beavers Bend offers cabins, a lodge along the lakeshore, and more than a dozen miles of hiking trails that range from easy riverside walks to longer backcountry routes through pine and hardwood forest. Scuba divers come for the lake's clear water and submerged timber, while anglers fish for largemouth and smallmouth bass as well as catfish in the open water. The park's name describes a geographic feature as much as it honors a person, since the bend itself refers to a point where the Mountain Fork River curls back on itself in an almost complete circle before straightening out again downstream, a shape easy to trace on any detailed map of the park even today.

[FACT 9: The River Named for a Screaming Bird | 34.55,-95.35]

Fact nine.

The Kiamichi River rises on Pine Mountain in the Ouachita Mountains near the Arkansas border and runs somewhere between 165 and 177 miles, depending on which survey measured it, before it empties into the Red River near Hugo, draining an area of close to 1,830 square miles along the way. Its name is most likely French in origin, possibly describing the horned screamer, a loud tropical bird species that early French traders may have associated with cries they heard along the river, though the exact origin is still debated among historians who have studied Oklahoma place names. The river enters Pushmataha County near the town of Clayton and then bends south through Choctaw County on its way to the Red River, and for much of the twentieth century the Frisco railroad followed its course closely enough that the tracks and the river valley are still described together in local histories. Before statehood, much of this valley belonged to Kiamitia County, a Choctaw Nation county named after the river, until Oklahoma statehood replaced tribal county boundaries with the state's own. Much of the Kiamichi remains undeveloped, with no major dam interrupting its main channel, which makes it a popular destination for float trips out of small outfitters in river communities like Moyers. Anglers come for bass and catfish rather than the trout stocked downstream of the dammed lakes elsewhere in the region. The river's trade reach once extended further than its current reputation suggests, since the valley fell within the broader trading territory of the ancient Spiro Mounds center, whose influence stretched well beyond the Arkansas River valley where the mounds themselves still stand.

[FACT 8: The Hills Geologists Still Argue About | 34.75,-95.30]

Fact eight.

Hidden within the central Ouachita Mountains of southeastern Oklahoma, a cluster of low ridges called the Potato Hills forms a roughly oval exposure of rock far older than the surrounding landscape, ranging from the Middle Ordovician-age Womble Shale up through younger Mississippian-period formations. The name describes their shape from above more than their height, a scatter of rounded, potato-like ridges rather than a single dramatic peak. Geologists have debated how these hills actually formed for almost a century. One early interpretation, proposed in 1929, described them as a window exposing older rock through a thrust sheet that slid in from the north. Later researchers have proposed competing explanations instead, including simple vertical uplift, a fan-shaped stack of thrust faults, or even two separate, opposing periods of compression that folded the rock from different directions at different times. At the center of that debate sits the Windingstair Fault, a major break in the Ouachita rock that runs steep near the surface but flattens out the deeper it goes, acting as the leading edge of thrusting across much of this stretch of the mountains. The geology is not purely academic. Shallow natural gas was discovered in the Bigfork Chert formation here in 1960, and a larger gas accumulation turned up in 1998 within the Jackfork Formation's Ratcliff Sandstone, giving the Potato Hills a quiet role in Oklahoma's energy industry despite having no town, highway marker, or visitor center built around them. Few people outside petroleum geology circles have ever heard of the Potato Hills, even though the rock beneath them has been argued over in academic journals for nearly a hundred years.

[FACT 7: The Fault Line Hiding Under the Byway | 34.80,-95.10]

Fact seven.

The Winding Stair Mountains, the ridge line the Talimena Scenic Drive follows along the Oklahoma-Arkansas border, sit on top of what geologists call the Ouachita Overthrust, a massive sheet of Cambrian through Lower Pennsylvanian rock pushed into place during a second, later period of mountain building across eastern Oklahoma, long after the rock itself first formed. The leading edge of that thrust sheet is the Choctaw Fault, which cuts roughly east to west across the middle of both Latimer and LeFlore counties, a break in the earth most drivers cross without any sign telling them it is there. A related structure, the Windingstair Fault, runs nearby and is described in survey reports as steep near the surface before flattening out with depth, part of the same system of north-directed thrusting that built the Potato Hills a short distance to the west. None of this is visible to a casual traveler. The ridges above the fault look like an ordinary forested mountain range from the Talimena overlooks, with no outcrop or roadside marker hinting at the violence of the collision that stacked these rock layers millions of years ago. I used to tell my students that the most dramatic geology is often the geology you cannot see, because the forces that built a landscape usually finished their work long before any road, or any person, arrived to appreciate it. The Winding Stair Mountains are a textbook case. The overlooks along the byway sit on rock that was shoved northward along these faults, and the view from the pull-out gives no hint of it at all.

[FACT 6: Fifty-Four Miles With No Snowplows | 34.83,-94.98]

Fact six.

The Talimena Scenic Drive runs 54 miles, or about 87 kilometers, from Mena, Arkansas, to Talihina, Oklahoma, following the ridge tops of the Winding Stair and Rich Mountain along Arkansas Highway 88 and Oklahoma State Highway 1. Rich Mountain itself reaches 2,681 feet, or about 817 meters, making it Arkansas's second-highest peak, though the Oklahoma end of the drive climbs through equally steep terrain along the Winding Stair ridge. The current route opened in 1969, and Luci Baines Johnson, daughter of President Lyndon Johnson, formally dedicated it the following year, on June 7, 1970. The road picked up its federal forest-byway status in 1989, Arkansas state scenic byway status in 1998, Oklahoma state status in 2002, and finally National Scenic Byway designation in 2005, a slow, multi-decade accumulation of recognition for a road most Oklahomans already considered scenic long before Washington agreed. Twenty-two designated pull-outs line the route, and grades in places reach at least thirteen percent, steep enough that the road is closed during ice and snow, and signs at both ends warn drivers about sudden fog on the ridge. Some of the ridge-top forest along the drive was simply never logged, not out of preservation policy but because the terrain made commercial timber removal too difficult to bother with, leaving pockets of old growth that stand out against younger, managed forest lower on the slopes. Drivers who make the full run from Mena to Talihina cross from Arkansas into Oklahoma without a single stoplight, climbing above 2,000 feet for most of the drive on a road that still closes rather than fight an Ouachita ice storm.

[FACT 5: The Capitol That Outlasted a Nation's Borders | 34.69,-95.30]

Fact five.

In the small community of Tuskahoma, along the Kiamichi River in what is now Latimer County, a two-story brick building completed in 1884 once housed the entire government of the Choctaw Nation. Tuskahoma became the Nation's political capital in 1882, the last stop in a series of moves we will trace in the next fact. The Capitol Building served as the seat of Choctaw government from 1884 until 1907, when Oklahoma statehood dissolved the tribal government's formal political authority and absorbed its territory into the new state. Unlike many tribal government buildings lost to fire, demolition, or simple neglect elsewhere in Indian Territory, the Tuskahoma Capitol survived intact, and it was added to the National Register of Historic Places decades later in recognition of that survival. The building's grounds now host the Choctaw Nation's annual Labor Day festival, one of the largest tribal gatherings in the state, along with the annual Trail of Tears Memorial Walk, a ceremony that links the building directly back to the removal that first brought Choctaw government to Oklahoma soil nearly fifty years before the Capitol itself was built. Visitors can tour the restored interior, where original courtroom and council chamber spaces have been preserved largely as they stood during the Nation's final decades of government before statehood. What makes the building remarkable is less its architecture, which is modest by the standards of state capitols elsewhere in the country, and more the fact that it stands at all. Dozens of similar tribal government buildings across the old Indian Territory were torn down, converted to other uses, or simply abandoned once statehood ended their original purpose in 1907, and Tuskahoma's Capitol is one of the clearest surviving physical links to a Choctaw government that once ran its own courts, schools, and legislature from this exact spot along the Kiamichi River, decades before Oklahoma existed as a state at all.

[FACT 4: A Capital Named for the Mound Left Behind | 34.69,-95.29]

Fact four.

The Choctaw Nation was the first of the major southeastern tribal nations forced west under federal removal policy, with removals beginning under the 1830 Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek and continuing over the better part of the following decade. Historians estimate that somewhere between 12,500 and 20,000 Choctaw people were removed from Mississippi, Louisiana, and Alabama in that process, and that several thousand, by some estimates as many as 6,000, died along the way or in the difficult years immediately after arrival in what is now southeastern Oklahoma. When the Choctaw Nation organized its first government on this new land, it adopted a constitution in 1834 and named its capital Nanih Waiya, after the mound the Nation had been forced to leave behind in Mississippi, a mound Choctaw oral tradition describes as central to the people's origin. Sources differ slightly on the new capital's exact location relative to modern Tuskahoma, with the Choctaw Nation's own historical account placing it about two miles south of the current Tuskahoma Capitol grounds, while other historical references place it roughly two miles east, a small discrepancy that reflects how little survives of the original 1834 settlement itself. That first capital did not last. Choctaw government moved to Doaksville under an 1850 constitution, then to Boggy Depot under an 1857 constitution, before finally settling permanently at Tuskahoma in 1882. What stayed constant through every move was the act of carrying a name, and the memory attached to it, across each new location, a small act of continuity inside a much larger story of forced displacement. Every year, the Choctaw Nation holds its Trail of Tears Memorial Walk on the grounds of the Tuskahoma Capitol, a ceremony that deliberately connects the modern Nation's government seat back to the removal journey that first brought Choctaw families onto this land nearly two centuries ago. The walk is not a reenactment so much as a remembrance, and Choctaw Nation citizens from across the country still return to Tuskahoma each year specifically to take part in it, carrying forward a naming tradition that began with a single grieving decision made in 1834, to call a brand-new capital after a mound its people could never go back to.

[FACT 3: The Westernmost Capital of a Vanished Trade Network | 35.25,-94.62]

Fact three.

On a bend of the Arkansas River near the town of Spiro in LeFlore County, twelve earthen mounds mark what was once one of the most important ceremonial and trading centers in all of ancient North America. Built by Caddoan-speaking people within the broader Mississippian cultural world, Spiro is usually grouped alongside Cahokia near present-day Saint Louis, Moundville in Alabama, and Etowah in Georgia as one of four major centers of that era, and historians describe it as the westernmost outpost of what is sometimes called the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex. The Oklahoma Historical Society dates permanent settlement at the site to around 800 of the common era, with activity continuing until roughly 1450, though some researchers place its cultural peak somewhat later, between 1200 and 1350. At its height, Spiro's leaders maintained trade and ceremonial connections stretching across much of the Mississippian world, and the site's protected area today covers about 150 acres holding all twelve surviving mounds. The site's modern history took a dark turn in the 1930s, when treasure hunters purchased the rights to tunnel directly into Craig Mound, the second largest at Spiro, blasting and digging their way toward artifacts to sell rather than study. The destruction they caused helped push Oklahoma to pass some of the nation's earliest archaeological preservation laws, and the University of Oklahoma then ran a scientific excavation at the site through 1941, eventually recovering more than six hundred burials along with an enormous collection of engraved shell, copper, and stone artifacts. Why the center declined around 1450, after roughly six centuries of continuous importance, remains one of the real unanswered questions in North American archaeology, and researchers still debate it today. I used to tell my students that the most important place in a region is not always the one with the biggest modern town built on top of it, and Spiro is the clearest proof of that lesson I know. Today it is Oklahoma's only prehistoric Native American archaeological site open to the public, managed quietly by the Oklahoma Historical Society, while the town of Spiro itself, population barely two thousand, sits almost unaware that it shares its name with one of the most significant ceremonial centers in the entire ancient Mississippian world.

[FACT 2: The Mountains That Never Erupted | 34.75,-95.25]

Fact two.

Mountain-building episodes almost always leave behind volcanic rock, metamorphic transformation, or both, as colliding plates melt and reshape the crust caught between them. The Ouachita Mountains are a rare exception. Formed during the Paleozoic era when intense lateral compression folded a thick pile of sedimentary rock layers into the Ouachita Fold and Thrust Belt, the range shows almost none of the volcanism or large-scale metamorphism common to orogenic belts elsewhere in the world, with only limited, low-grade metamorphism appearing in a few isolated spots. What the compression did produce, instead, is one of the hardest, most distinctive rock types found anywhere in North America. Novaculite, a fine-grained, extremely dense siliceous sedimentary rock, formed under that pressure and now caps many of the region's ridgelines, so hard that it resists erosion far better than the softer shales and sandstones around it, which is part of why the Ouachita ridges still stand as sharp, folded lines across the landscape today instead of having worn down to rounded hills millions of years ago. Novaculite is prized enough that Arkansas whetstones cut from it have been sold around the world for over a century, and Native peoples across the region quarried it for thousands of years before European contact specifically because its fine grain made excellent cutting edges for tools and projectile points. The absence of volcanic rock also means the Ouachitas lack the dramatic peaks associated with volcanic mountain ranges elsewhere in the country, trading that drama for something rarer instead, a nearly 225-mile stretch of folded, compressed sedimentary rock running from near Little Rock, Arkansas, to Atoka, Oklahoma, that formed through pressure and folding rather than eruption. A few small igneous intrusions do exist on the Arkansas side, such as Magnet Cove, but they pushed in tens of millions of years after the mountains had already formed. Geologists studying orogenic belts worldwide frequently cite the Ouachitas specifically because that absence is unusual enough to be scientifically useful, a natural laboratory for studying what compression alone can build.

[FACT 1: The Mountains Running the Wrong Way | 34.75,-94.70]

Fact one.

Nearly every major mountain range in the continental United States runs roughly north to south, from the Rocky Mountains down through the Sierra Nevada to the long spine of the Appalachians. The Ouachita Mountains, stretching across this part of southeastern Oklahoma and into western Arkansas, do the opposite. They run east to west, a trend shared by only a handful of American ranges, such as Utah's Uinta Mountains and California's Transverse Ranges, and geologists studying the region consistently flag it as one of the defining oddities of the entire Ouachita Fold and Thrust Belt. The reason traces back to how the range formed. During the Paleozoic era, a collision compressed thick layers of sedimentary rock along a line running roughly east and west, and the folds that resulted, now exposed at the surface as the ridges most visitors see along the Talimena Scenic Drive or from the overlooks above Winding Stair Mountain, simply preserve the direction of that ancient pressure. Every other geographic quirk in this region, from the folded rock of the Potato Hills to the Choctaw Fault running beneath the Talimena byway, to the way the Kiamichi and Mountain Fork rivers cut south across the grain of the mountains rather than along it, traces back to this same backward orientation. A river or a ridge that runs the wrong way compared to the rest of the continent changes how water drains, how roads get built, and even how settlers once chose where to put a town, since crossing the Ouachitas required fighting a series of east-west ridges no matter which direction a traveler actually needed to go. Most people driving the Talimena byway, admiring the view from a pull-out above Rich Mountain or Winding Stair, never learn that the ridge beneath their feet runs in a direction only a few other American mountain ranges share. After thirty-eight years of teaching geography, that is still the kind of fact that gets me the most letters from former students, the ones who drove through these mountains as kids without ever being told they were looking at one of the rarer orientations on the entire continent, hiding in plain sight the whole time.

[2. CLOSE + CTA]

There is another Southeast Oklahoma beneath the familiar one, and most people never know it's there. It's built from mountains that run the wrong way, a reservoir still argued over in federal court, a cave with more legend than evidence, and a capitol building that outlasted the government it was built to house. Even the roads tell part of the story, since the Talimena byway and the old horse-thief trails above it both exist for the same reason, a landscape that makes you fight for every mile no matter which direction you are headed.

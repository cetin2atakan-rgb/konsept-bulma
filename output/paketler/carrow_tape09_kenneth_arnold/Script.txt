[BLOK3_DOSYA_BASLIGI]
File B-008. Class B, persons, a number lower than nearly every other file I have shown you, reflecting how early this office began paying close attention to reports of this kind. Event: the afternoon of June twenty-fourth, nineteen forty-seven, the airspace near a large volcanic peak in Washington State. Disposition: blank.

A private pilot, experienced, well regarded in his local flying community, and actively engaged that afternoon in a search for a downed Marine transport plane with a reward attached to its recovery, was flying near the mountain when he observed nine objects moving in formation at a speed and in a manner he had never encountered in years of flying. He landed, reported what he had seen to local authorities within the hour, and gave, over the following days, one of the most detailed and carefully qualified witness statements in this entire drawer.

I want to establish the search mission itself a little more fully, because I think the context matters. The downed transport he was looking for that afternoon was never found as a result of his search, though it was located some time later through other means, and the reward attached to it, real money in nineteen forty-seven, went to no one for anything he saw in the sky that day, hardly a motive for a man to invent a far stranger story than any downed aircraft would have given him. He was in that airspace for an entirely mundane, verifiable reason that had nothing at all to do with anything unusual, which I think matters a great deal when weighing how much credibility to extend to what he reported seeing once the mundane search gave way to something else entirely.

[BLOK4_DUVARDAKI_KARELER]
The first item I want to describe is the pilot's own sketch, submitted voluntarily to military investigators days after the sighting, because I think it corrects something almost everyone outside this drawer gets wrong about this file. The objects are not drawn as discs. They are drawn as flat, crescent-shaped or boomerang-shaped forms, one of the nine noticeably different from the other eight — described in his own accompanying notes as "more like a half-moon, dark along one edge" — arranged in a loose, uneven line rather than a tight formation, banking and flashing as they caught the sun, which is the detail, as I will get to shortly, that actually produced the phrase the rest of the century would use.

I want to linger on the ninth object a little longer than the sketch itself does, because the pilot's own notes return to it more than once, as though he kept circling back to it while writing, unsatisfied with his first attempt to describe it. He calls it, in one line, "the odd one, dark where the others caught light, trailing slightly behind as if it couldn't quite keep the others' pace." None of the later popular illustrations of this sighting include a ninth, different object at all. Most show eight or nine identical gleaming discs in a tidy row, which is not what the man who actually saw them took the trouble to draw.

The second item is a flight-path calculation card, prepared by this office's predecessor using the pilot's own description of distance, bearing, and elapsed time between two fixed landmarks the objects crossed during his observation. The resulting estimated speed, even accounting generously for the kind of error any excited witness might introduce, comes out above twelve hundred miles an hour, well beyond any aircraft of that era, military or civilian, and the card notes, in its own flat clerical language, that "no known test program at this office's awareness was operating experimental craft in this airspace on this date," a sentence I have come to recognize, after nine tapes, as this drawer's particular way of admitting it does not have an ordinary answer.

I want to add a note about how that calculation was reached, because I think the method itself speaks to how seriously this office took the report from the very beginning. Rather than relying solely on the pilot's own estimate of speed, the analyst assigned to the card cross-referenced his stated elapsed time against the known distance between two landmarks he named specifically and independently verifiable on any standard aeronautical chart of the region, arriving at a figure derived from geography rather than from the witness's own impression of velocity. A frightened or exaggerating witness can inflate a felt sense of speed considerably. He cannot, by the same error, move two mountains closer together on a chart that existed before he ever flew past them.

The third item is a corroborating statement from a prospector working a separate location some distance away, who reported seeing similar objects, independently and before the pilot's own report had reached any newspaper, moving in a comparable formation at a comparable time that same afternoon. The two accounts were taken by different investigators, in different towns, before either man had any opportunity to compare notes, and the file's own cover memo flags the independent corroboration as "the detail that moved this report from unusual to worth sustained attention."

I want to describe the prospector's own account a little further, because it is easy, in a file this famous, to let the second witness become a footnote to the first. He described, independently, a comparable number of objects, moving at a comparable altitude, though he placed their color differently, describing a duller, less reflective surface than the pilot's own bright, flashing description. I do not think this discrepancy weakens either account. I think two men watching the same formation from different distances, different angles, and different lighting conditions would reasonably describe its surface somewhat differently, and I would trust the corroboration less, not more, if both descriptions had matched each other in every single particular.

The fourth item is a weather and instrument log from a ranger station near the mountain, noting a brief, unexplained compass deviation recorded that same afternoon, within the approximate window of the sighting, a deviation the station's own log attributes, at the time, to "equipment malfunction, cause undetermined," and never revisited. I have shown you this particular phrase, in one form or another, enough times across this drawer now that I no longer read it as neutral language. I read it as this office's oldest and most reliable tell.

The fifth item is the pilot's own later correspondence with military investigators, included in this folder in full, in which he repeatedly and with evident frustration corrects the growing public description of what he saw, insisting, in more than one letter, that he never once described the objects themselves as disc-shaped, only their motion — a flat, skipping, erratic movement he compared, in his original statement, to "a saucer if you skipped it across water." A wire service reporter condensed that single comparison into two words, and within days, newspapers across the country were running the phrase as though it described the shape of the craft rather than the manner of their flight, a distortion the pilot spent a considerable portion of the rest of his life trying, mostly without success, to correct.

There is a sixth item in this folder, smaller than the others, that I want to give its own moment because of how specific it is. A flight log page from the pilot's own aircraft, submitted voluntarily alongside his statement, shows a routine series of entries in the days before the sighting and a noticeably longer, more careful entry for the day itself, including a sketch in the margin matching the formal sketch I described a moment ago, made, by his own note, "within the hour, while it was still fresh, before anyone could tell me what I was supposed to have seen." I find that margin sketch more convincing than almost anything else in this file, precisely because it exists in a private working document he had no reason to expect anyone outside his own logbook would ever read.

I also want to mention a seventh item, a brief weather bureau report for that date and region, confirming clear visibility and calm conditions across the relevant airspace that afternoon, ruling out, as cleanly as any document in this drawer rules out anything, the possibility that cloud distortion, mirage, or unusual atmospheric refraction played any role in what the pilot or the prospector separately observed. I give this item its own line because I think it is rare, in this drawer, to find a piece of evidence that simply closes off an entire category of mundane explanation rather than leaving it open for later argument.

[BLOK5_ONLAR_HAKKINDA]
I want to say something about the pilot himself, because I think his own conduct in the days after the sighting is a model of exactly the kind of careful witness testimony I have come to value most across this drawer. He did not seek out the press. He reported to local authorities first, cooperated fully and repeatedly with military investigators, offered his own aircraft and flying logs for inspection without being asked, and continued, for years afterward, to publicly and specifically correct the shape of the objects even when doing so did nothing but complicate a story the rest of the world had already decided it preferred simpler. A man inventing a story for attention does not spend years afterward arguing with the attention over a detail that would only make the story stranger and less familiar if people actually listened to him.

I want to add one further note about his search-and-rescue background specifically, because I think it bears directly on how carefully he would have been able to judge speed, distance, and formation under pressure. He had, by the time of this sighting, logged a considerable number of hours in mountain search operations, work that depends entirely on a pilot's ability to judge scale and distance against terrain quickly and accurately, often in difficult visibility, with lives depending on getting the estimate right. A man with that specific, practiced skill is, in my estimation, a meaningfully better judge of an unfamiliar object's speed and formation than an ordinary witness would be, the same way I would trust a career sheriff's measurement of a depression in dry soil, or a captain's measurement of a ship's own rigging, over an untrained observer's impression of the same thing, and I think this office's own early interest in his report reflects exactly that judgment, made quietly, in the same flat language this drawer uses for everything it takes seriously without saying so directly.

[BLOK6_KISA_SAPMA_MAURY]
A short digression, because the weeks immediately following this sighting produced one of the strangest and most genuinely sad threads in this entire drawer, and I think it deserves its own telling rather than a passing mention.

Within days of the pilot's report reaching national newspapers, a separate account emerged from a harbor in Washington State, describing a crashed object that had allegedly expelled debris and a strange slag-like material, injuring a witness and his son. Two military officers flew out to collect and investigate the reported material personally, taking it aboard a B-25 for transport back to their base. That aircraft crashed en route, less than an hour into the flight, killing both officers.

I want to name what little this file allows me to about them, because I think a man carrying bad evidence toward an ordinary debriefing deserves at least that much. Both were experienced officers assigned specifically to investigate reports of this kind, dispatched quickly precisely because the harbor account, false as it later proved, had seemed credible enough at the time to warrant urgent, personal attention rather than a routine written report. They did their job exactly as it was meant to be done, flying out immediately on what should have been an unremarkable return flight, carrying boxes of material that turned out, as far as any official account establishes, to have been nothing more than ordinary industrial slag, and the aircraft went down anyway, for reasons an official inquiry attributed to a mechanical failure in one of its engines, a failure type that era's aircraft of that model were, by the broader historical record, not unusually prone to in any way connected to this specific case. The harbor account itself was later determined, officially and by most reasonable accounting I have found in this drawer, to have been a fabrication, invented, by most accounts, by two harbor patrolmen hoping to sell a sensational story to a pulp magazine in the excitement the pilot's legitimate report had started. The hoax was confirmed. The two dead officers were not part of any hoax at all. They were real men, flying a real and ordinary transport mission made necessary entirely by someone else's invented story, and the file notes, without further comment, that the material they were carrying when the aircraft went down was never recovered or definitively identified afterward.

I have read the investigating board's own findings on the crash more than once, looking specifically for anything that might tie it to the material itself rather than to ordinary mechanical misfortune, and I have found nothing that persuades me to doubt the board's own plain conclusion. I mention my own search mainly to be honest with you about the fact that I looked, and that looking, in this particular case, gave me nothing stranger than what the official record already offers. I think it is important, after nine tapes of finding reasons to doubt the official account, to also tell you plainly the times I looked hard and found the official account holding up.

I want to be careful with this, because I think it would be easy, reading this drawer the way I have read it across nine tapes now, to reach for a stranger explanation than the plain and tragic one actually sitting in front of me. I believe the hoax was a hoax. I believe the crash was very likely an ordinary mechanical failure of exactly the kind that killed a great many military aircrew in that era, entirely unconnected to anything extraordinary at all. I include it on this tape anyway, because I think the pilot who started this whole thread, a careful and honest man by every account in this file, spent the rest of his life also carrying the quiet, unfair weight of two deaths that followed from a lie told by men hoping to profit from an excitement he never asked for and never wanted.

I have gone back and forth about whether to include this thread on this tape at all, since it does not, strictly, belong to the physical evidence of File B-008 itself. I have decided to include it because I think a file that only describes nine shapes crossing a mountain sky, without describing what the shapes set in motion afterward among the ordinary, fallible people who reported, investigated, and occasionally lied about them, is an incomplete file, and I have promised you, across nine tapes now, that I would rather give you an incomplete picture honestly than a tidy one dishonestly.

[BLOK7_NE_OLDUKLARI]
One opinion per file, and I want to keep this one close to what the pilot himself believed, because I think his own account, corrected and re-corrected over years, deserves more trust than almost any theory I could layer on top of it.

I think he saw exactly what he described: nine flat, crescent-shaped objects, one noticeably different from the rest, moving at a speed and in a manner no aircraft of that era could match, witnessed independently by at least one other credible observer that same afternoon. I hold this opinion with more confidence than I hold almost any other across this drawer, precisely because this is the one file where the witness himself spent decades actively correcting the record rather than letting a more exciting version stand uncontested, which is not the behavior of a man protecting a lie. I do not think the shape was ever a saucer, and I think the phrase itself, however useful it became to the rest of the century, has done this file's actual evidence a disservice every single time it has been repeated in place of the pilot's own more careful words.

I want to add one further thought, because I think this file, sitting as close as it does to the very beginning of this century's modern interest in the subject, tells me something about how quickly an honest observation can be reshaped into something more convenient for everyone except the man who made it. A newspaper needed a punchy phrase more than it needed an accurate one. The public needed a simple shape to imagine more than it needed the stranger, less tidy crescent the pilot actually drew. I do not think anyone involved in that particular distortion acted with any real malice. I think it is simply easier, for almost everyone except the witness himself, to let the convenient wrong version stand than to keep correcting it, and I think that ease is its own kind of quiet erasure, no less real for being unintentional.

I want to connect this, briefly, to something I have noticed across every file I have shown you so far, in this drawer and in the one I am apparently now meant to help catalogue upstairs as well. Every single distortion I have traced back to its source in this office's own records began the same way: not as a deliberate lie manufactured by this office itself, but as an ordinary human shortcut, a reporter's tidy phrase, a translator's best available word, a frightened rancher's silence mistaken for nothing worth asking about, that this office then simply declined to correct once the shortcut proved more convenient than the truth underneath it. I do not think the arrangement invents these distortions. I think it has learned, patiently, over a very long time, simply to let other people's ordinary carelessness do its work for it.

[BLOK9_INSANLAR]
I want to tell you what happened to the pilot in the years after, because I think his story, more than almost any other in this drawer, is a story about the cost of simply being first.

He continued flying and working in his community for decades afterward, by every account a respected and ordinary man, and he gave interviews, when asked, with the same patient, slightly weary correction every time: not a saucer in shape, only in motion. He is quoted, late in life, saying something I want to read to you exactly as it was recorded: "I've spent thirty years telling people I didn't see what they think I saw. I saw something. I'll go to my grave knowing I saw something. I just wish, one time, somebody would ask me what I actually saw instead of telling me what I supposedly meant." I do not think I have anything useful to add to that. I think it is, in four sentences, the clearest statement of what this entire drawer keeps doing to the people inside it, and I think, of everyone I have quoted across these nine tapes, he is the one whose patience I understand best right now, sitting where I am sitting tonight.

I want to add that he never, as far as any record in this folder shows, tried to make much of a living from what he saw, beyond a single book he co-wrote to set his own account down in his own words, and that he continued, decades later, to answer letters from strangers and researchers patiently, correcting the same detail every single time with what one later correspondent described as "a tired kindness, like a man repeating a joke nobody else finds as unfunny as he does." I think that image, of a careful, honest man turning the same correction over and over for the rest of his working life, belongs beside every photograph and sketch I have ever shown you in this drawer, because it is, in its own quiet way, just as much physical evidence of what happened that afternoon as any flight log or compass deviation.

[1. HOOK + PERSONA INTRO]

Northeast Alabama is hiding far more than cotton fields and mountain laurel. Beneath the flat green spine of Lookout Mountain runs a river that has no business being up there, a canyon cut roughly six hundred feet into solid rock, and a cave where Cherokee families once carved sacred messages into stone by firelight, just before the Trail of Tears. Somewhere in a quiet valley town, sock mills once outfitted half of America's feet, and on a ridge above a small community called Fyffe, families still talk about the lights that filled the sky over one strange weekend in 1989. There's also a small mountaintop dam, built by one self-taught engineer, that quietly lit up five small towns before most of rural Alabama ever saw electricity. Today we will count down twenty-five remarkable geographic facts about Northeast Alabama, the stretch of country around Fort Payne, Mentone, Valley Head, and the Little River Canyon. I'm Walter Hale. I taught geography for thirty-eight years in a small high school in Kentucky, and I still can't drive through a county without wondering why it looks the way it does. Let's start at number twenty-five.

[FACT 25: Fyffe, Alabama's UFO Capital | 34.4546,-85.9416]

Fact twenty-five. On the night of February eleventh and into the morning of February twelfth, 1989, something happened over the small DeKalb County town of Fyffe that nobody there has fully explained since. Around fifty residents reported strange lights moving over the fields and ridgelines, and the witnesses were not the sort easily dismissed. The town's police chief and assistant chief both said they saw something, along with officers from two neighboring towns and several Alabama state troopers. Word spread fast, and newspapers across the country picked up the story within days. Fyffe sits on the western edge of our region, a farming community of around a thousand people tucked against the DeKalb County hills, the kind of place where everybody's family has been there for generations. The town never tried to shake the attention. Instead it leaned into it, and for more than three decades Fyffe has held an annual UFO Days festival on the last Saturday of August, with a parade, music, and vendors filling the same streets the lights once passed over. If you drive through town around festival time, you will see banners strung over Main Street and alien figures in shop windows. Nobody in Fyffe claims to know exactly what those fifty people saw. What they will tell you is that whatever it was, it was real enough to convince a police chief to file a report, and real enough that the town built its identity around a single unexplained night. The festival each year draws visitors from well outside DeKalb County, some of them serious investigators, most of them simply curious, and local shop owners say UFO Days has become as reliable a boost to the town's economy as any harvest season ever was.

[FACT 24: Collinsville Trade Day | 34.2659,-85.8666]

Fact twenty-four. Long before anyone called it a flea market, Collinsville Trade Day started in 1902 as a horse and mule swap along the banks of Little Wills Creek, the kind of gathering where farmers from three counties came to trade livestock and argue prices under the trees. The wives of those traders eventually set up tables of baked goods and handmade crafts alongside the animals, and by the 1950s the event had outgrown its downtown spot. In 1955 it moved to its current location along United States Highway 11, where it has run every Saturday since, rain or shine, except on the rare year Christmas Eve falls on a Saturday. Organizers estimate the market now sprawls across roughly sixty-five acres with more than eight hundred vendor spaces, and say that on a good weekend as many as thirty thousand people pass through looking for tools, produce, antiques, and the occasional live chicken. The gates open before dawn, often by four-thirty in the morning, and by early afternoon the crowds have thinned and the trucks are packing up. If you drive south out of Fort Payne on a Saturday morning, you will know you are close to Collinsville by the line of pickup trucks and the rows of tailgates stretching along the highway shoulder a mile before the entrance. It is easy to forget, walking past tables of power tools and secondhand furniture, that the whole tradition started with men haggling over the price of a mule. Regular vendors say the market barely changes from decade to decade, the same families setting up in roughly the same spots their parents and grandparents once held, which is part of why longtime visitors keep coming back instead of drifting off to newer flea markets closer to the interstate.

[FACT 23: The World's Longest Yard Sale | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact twenty-three. Once a year, for four days beginning the first Thursday in August, a six hundred ninety mile string of yard sales stretches from Addison, Michigan, all the way to Gadsden, Alabama, and the final leg of that route runs straight through our region. The sale began in 1987, when a Tennessee county official named Mike Walker wanted to pull travelers off the interstate and onto the back roads, and it has grown every year since. For most of its length the sale follows United States Route 127, but just south of Chattanooga it leaves that highway and follows the Lookout Mountain Parkway instead, winding down through Valley Head, Mentone, and Fort Payne before finishing in Gadsden. For four days, front yards up and down that ridge road fill with folding tables, furniture, and hand-painted signs, and the usual thirty-minute drive along the parkway can stretch into hours as buyers stop at every third driveway. Families along the route plan their whole year around it, some earning more in those four days than they do the rest of the season combined. If you drive the Lookout Mountain Parkway on any other week, you'll find a quiet two-lane road through pasture and hardwood forest. For four days in August, it becomes one of the longest open-air markets on earth. Local tourism officials have learned to plan around it, since hotel rooms across DeKalb County fill up months in advance, and some families along the parkway treat the four-day event as their single biggest payday of the entire year.

[FACT 22: The Old Union Crossing Covered Bridge | 34.5324,-85.5979]

Fact twenty-two. Not far from Mentone, a weathered covered bridge spans the West Fork of the Little River, and its history is tangled enough that even local historians don't agree on all of it. One account says the structure traces back to around 1863, when Union Army engineers built it during the Civil War to move troops and supplies across the water. Another version says the bridge started somewhere else entirely and was relocated to its current site in 1972, after the creek it once crossed dried up. What everyone does agree on is that the bridge's wooden cover, the part that makes it look like a classic covered bridge from a postcard, was built over an existing steel span only as recently as 1980. Alabama has very few covered bridges left standing, and this one survives mostly because the DeKalb County community around it has kept maintaining it rather than letting it rot into the river. If you walk across it today, the boards still creak the way old covered bridges are supposed to creak, and through the gaps you can watch the West Fork run clear and shallow over smooth stone. Whether the bridge's bones go back to the Civil War or not, it stands now as one of the few reminders that this quiet mountain country was never entirely out of reach of that war. Photographers chase it in every season, since the dark wooden frame against the pale winter trees or the green summer canopy makes it one of the most photographed small structures anywhere on Lookout Mountain.

[FACT 21: The Band Alabama Started in Fort Payne | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact twenty-one. In 1969, two cousins from Fort Payne named Randy Owen and Teddy Gentry started playing music together, soon joined by a third cousin, Jeff Cook, and the group they formed in that small DeKalb County hosiery town would eventually become one of the best-selling country acts in American history. They called themselves Young Country at first, then changed the name to Wildcountry in 1972 and moved to Myrtle Beach, South Carolina, where they played for tips in beach clubs for years before anyone outside the Carolinas had heard of them. After signing a one-year contract with a small label in 1977, they renamed themselves The Alabama Band, later shortened simply to Alabama, and within a decade they were filling arenas. The Academy of Country Music named them Artist of the Decade for the 1980s, they received a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame in 1998, and the Country Music Hall of Fame inducted them in 2005. Through all of that success, the band kept its home base in Fort Payne, and the town still claims them as its own. If you drive through Fort Payne today, you will pass streets and venues that trade on the connection, a reminder that one of country music's biggest acts came out of a town better known at the time for making socks than making records. Randy Owen and Teddy Gentry never fully left, keeping ties to their hometown long after the arena tours ended, and the connection between the band's name and the state itself still occasionally confuses out-of-town visitors who assume, reasonably enough, that a band called Alabama must have formed somewhere closer to the state's biggest cities.

[FACT 20: Streight's Raid Through the Mountain Country | 34.3340,-85.6102]

Fact twenty. In the spring of 1863, a Union colonel named Abel Streight led a cavalry raid through northern Alabama with orders to cut the railroad that supplied Confederate forces in Chattanooga, and the rugged country around what is now Little River Canyon played its own quiet part in how that raid ended. The National Park Service notes that the main body of Lookout Mountain, the terrain that now makes up Little River Canyon National Preserve, was too rugged a barrier for armies to move through easily, which meant it saw little more than occasional scouting during the war. Streight's raid itself was fought and lost farther south and east, in a string of running battles that started at Day's Gap in Cullman County on April thirtieth, 1863, where Confederate cavalry under Nathan Bedford Forrest chased the Union column for days. Near Gadsden, at the southern foot of the very ridge our region sits on, a young woman named Emma Sansom famously guided Forrest's men across a creek, and Streight's exhausted column was finally forced to surrender near Cedar Bluff, on ground that now lies along the shore of Weiss Lake. The raid lost twelve men killed, sixty-nine wounded, and more than fourteen hundred captured. If you drive the back roads near Canyon Mouth Park today, there is little to mark that history beyond a scattering of historical plaques, but the same steep, broken ground that frustrated farmers and engineers for a century also helped turn back an army. Civil War historians still debate exactly how much credit the terrain deserves compared to Forrest's aggressive pursuit, but nobody disputes that the mountain country here was never an easy place to move a column of cavalry through quickly.

[FACT 19: DeKalb County's Poultry-Powered Farms | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact nineteen. Drive the ridges above Fort Payne and you will pass chicken house after chicken house, long metal buildings tucked into pastures, and that landscape is not an accident. DeKalb County ranks among Alabama's very largest agricultural counties, generating roughly eight hundred sixty-three million dollars a year in farm sales, according to the most recent federal count. According to the 2022 Census of Agriculture, livestock, poultry, and poultry products make up about ninety-four percent of everything the county sells, a pattern that has held for decades. The same census counted more than seventeen hundred farms spread across the county, most of them tied in some way to the broiler houses that supply poultry processing plants throughout northeast Alabama. I used to put a map of Alabama's counties on the classroom wall and ask my students to guess which county produced the most food. They always guessed the flat farmland down in the Black Belt, never the mountain county with the canyon and the waterfalls. The real answer surprised them every single year, and it still surprises most visitors who come up here chasing scenery and never notice the real economic engine humming along in the valleys below the ridgeline. Local extension agents say the number of broiler houses has only grown as demand for poultry products has climbed nationwide.

[FACT 18: Cloudmont, Alabama's Only Ski Resort | 34.5582,-85.5755]

Fact eighteen. Perched on top of Lookout Mountain above the town of Mentone sits Cloudmont, the only snow-ski resort in the entire state of Alabama. The resort opened in either 1970 or 1971, depending on which source you trust, and it has operated almost every winter since, with two beginner-to-intermediate slopes each about a thousand feet long, served by small tow lifts known locally as pony lifts. Natural snow is rare this far south, so the resort depends on machine-made snow, and the owners have said the temperature needs to drop into the twenties for that equipment to work. There was reportedly one eight-year stretch when conditions simply never cooperated and the slopes stayed closed, a reminder of how marginal snow skiing is at this latitude even at Lookout Mountain's elevation. When it does open, families drive up from Birmingham and Atlanta for a taste of skiing without the long trip to the Appalachians farther north, and local visitors can rent skis, boots, and poles on site rather than hauling gear from home. If you drive through Mentone on a cold January morning after a hard freeze, you might see cars with ski racks heading up the mountain, a strange sight in a state where most winters never see snow at all. Regulars say the thrill has less to do with the modest slopes themselves and more to do with the novelty of skiing anywhere at all this far south, a bragging right most Alabama natives never expect to earn.

[FACT 17: Mentone's Mineral-Spring Resort Days | 34.5582,-85.5755]

Fact seventeen. The town of Mentone takes its name not from anything Cherokee or Southern at all, but from a resort town on the French Riviera, chosen in 1884 because it was said to mean something like a singing or musical mountain spring. That same year, a physician named Frank Caldwell built the Mentone Springs Hotel to capitalize on local mineral springs that nineteenth-century visitors believed held healing properties, and for decades afterward, families from the hot lowland cities of Alabama and Georgia rode up to the cool mountain air of Mentone every summer to take the waters and escape the heat. The hotel became the anchor of the whole community, drawing thousands of seasonal visitors during the late eighteen hundreds and early nineteen hundreds, long before anyone built a highway up the mountain. The resort era faded long ago, but the hotel itself survived until March 2014, when a fire burned the historic wooden building to the ground in a matter of hours. Mentone itself sits right on the crest of Lookout Mountain at an elevation of about seventeen hundred thirty-six feet, five hundred twenty-nine meters, which is exactly why it stayed cool enough to attract those crowds in the first place. If you walk through town today, the shops and galleries still carry some of that old resort-town charm, even though the springs that started it all are mostly forgotten. Longtime residents still point out where the old hotel stood, and a handful of newer bed-and-breakfasts have tried, in a smaller way, to recapture the same cool-mountain-retreat appeal that once drew train loads of visitors up from the lowlands every summer.

[FACT 16: The Fort Payne Opera House | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact sixteen. In 1889, during a short-lived mining boom that briefly convinced investors Fort Payne would become a major iron and coal town, builders raised an ornate theater on Gault Avenue that opened to the public in 1890 as the Fort Payne Opera House. The boom collapsed by 1892, but the Opera House kept going anyway, hosting traveling stage shows, silent films, and later serving as a school auditorium before its doors finally shut in 1935. For more than three decades after that it sat largely forgotten, used at times for storage, until a local nonprofit called Landmarks of DeKalb County bought the building in 1969 and spent a year restoring it, reopening it to the public in 1970. It now holds a place on the National Register of Historic Places and is counted among the nineteenth-century theaters still in active use anywhere in the United States, a rare survivor from an era when nearly every small Southern town built one and most eventually lost theirs to fire, demolition, or simple neglect. The adjoining Hosiery Museum, run by the same nonprofit, ties the building back to the industry that came to define this town after the mining boom faded. If you walk past the Opera House on a Saturday afternoon, there is a decent chance a live performance is happening inside the same walls that have stood on Gault Avenue since 1889. Local theater groups and touring musicians alike treat a booking there as something of an honor, aware that they are performing in one of the only buildings in the state where that has been happening, on and off, for well over a century.

[FACT 15: High Falls Park's Flood-Fed Waterfall | 34.4692,-85.9578]

Fact fifteen. Out toward Henagar and Grove Oak, Town Creek drops about thirty-five feet, eleven meters, over a wide sandstone shelf at a spot known as High Falls, and what makes this waterfall unusual is not its height but its width. On an ordinary day the falls might only cover a modest stretch of that shelf, but after heavy rain the creek rises and the whole thing can spread out to nearly three hundred feet, ninety-one meters, across, turning a narrow cascade into a broad curtain of whitewater stretching nearly the length of a football field. The park sits in a quieter corner of DeKalb County, away from the more heavily visited preserve and state park land closer to Fort Payne, and it draws a smaller but devoted crowd of local swimmers and photographers who time their visits to the weather rather than the calendar. A natural rock arch nearby, documented by regional arch surveys, adds another reason explorers make the drive out. If you visit after a dry spell, you might wonder what the fuss is about. If you visit two days after a heavy storm, with the creek running high and brown with runoff, you will understand immediately why people around here consider High Falls one of the most dramatic sights in the county, even if it rarely makes the postcards the way DeSoto Falls does. Locals keep an informal watch on rainfall totals the way other communities watch river gauges for flooding, since a good storm upstream can turn a modest Tuesday-afternoon waterfall into a Saturday-morning destination by the following weekend.

[FACT 14: Weiss Lake, the Self-Declared Crappie Capital | 34.2379,-85.6033]

Fact fourteen. At the southern edge of our region, where the Coosa, the Chattooga, and the Little Rivers all come together, sits Weiss Lake, a reservoir of roughly thirty thousand acres created when Alabama Power broke ground on Weiss Dam in April 1958. The lake took its final shape in the early 1960s, and almost as soon as the water settled, it developed a reputation as one of the best crappie fisheries in the American South. The lakeside town of Cedar Bluff, in Cherokee County, has carried the nickname Crappie Capital of the World for decades. Spring, from March through May, draws the heaviest crowds of anglers, with a second smaller rush in the fall, and the lake has hosted national crappie tournaments, including a Crappie USA event at Leesburg in March 2013. County tourism officials have claimed more out-of-state fishing licenses are sold here than at any other lake in Alabama, though that figure is a local promotional claim rather than an independently audited count. Beyond crappie, the lake supports healthy populations of bass and catfish, and its thirty-seven marinas and handful of free public access points keep boat traffic steady throughout the warmer months. If you drive into Cedar Bluff on almost any spring weekend, the boat ramps will already be full before sunrise, which tells you everything you need to know about how seriously this corner of Alabama takes its crappie fishing. Tournament organizers say the lake's shallow, stump-filled coves make it especially forgiving for less experienced anglers, which helps explain why so many out-of-state fishing parties keep coming back year after year instead of trying their luck somewhere closer to home.

[FACT 13: Buck's Pocket, the Region's Second Canyon | 34.3700,-85.9900]

Fact thirteen. Most visitors who come to Northeast Alabama for canyon scenery head straight for Little River, but a second, much quieter canyon sits only a short drive away near the community of Grove Oak. Buck's Pocket State Park protects about two thousand acres where South Sauty Creek has carved a narrow gorge into the rock of Sand Mountain, with bluffs rising high enough along the rim to give hikers a sweeping overview of the gorge floor below. Local legend offers more than one explanation for the name, including a story about Cherokee hunters chasing a deer that leapt from the canyon rim rather than be caught, and a separate, half-joking bit of Alabama political folklore about defeated candidates retreating there to brood after losing elections. The park opened to the public in 1971 and today holds thirty-six campsites set right down on the canyon floor, along with fifteen miles of hiking trails and an eight-mile off-road vehicle trail that draws a different crowd than the hikers up top. The park straddles the line between DeKalb, Jackson, and Marshall counties, with its headquarters at Grove Oak. If you drive out expecting a smaller copy of Little River Canyon, the comparison holds up better than you might expect, right down to the bluff-top views and the sense that almost nobody else knows this place exists. Park staff say the off-road vehicle trail draws almost as many visitors as the hiking trails do, giving the park two very different reputations depending on which crowd you happen to ask.

[FACT 12: DeSoto State Park, Built by the CCC | 34.4959,-85.6075]

Fact twelve. Long before most of today's hiking trails and cabins existed, the Civilian Conservation Corps set up camp in Fort Payne in November 1933 and began building what would become DeSoto State Park on top of Lookout Mountain, with work on the mountain itself starting in early 1934. Over the following several years, young men in that Depression-era federal program built roads, trails, drainage culverts, a nine-hole golf course, and a collection of stone and timber structures, including picnic pavilions, cabins, and a large group lodge, much of it built from stone quarried right there on park property. You can still see drill marks on some of those stones today along one of the hiking trails, left by crews working with hand tools about ninety years ago. The park, named for the Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto, opened in 1935 as State Park Number Five and was formally dedicated as DeSoto State Park on May 24, 1939, when it was the largest state park in Alabama. A museum dedicated to that CCC history opened inside the park's original entrance station in 2013. If you walk the park today, most of what you are standing on, from the stone bridges to the lodge walls, was built by young men who had never seen a mountain before the government sent them to this one. Many of those same men came from flat farmland in other parts of Alabama and later said the work on Lookout Mountain was the first time in their lives they had ever seen a view stretch out for miles in every direction.

[FACT 11: Sequoyah Caverns and Its Mysterious Signatures | 34.5667,-85.6175]

Fact eleven. Near the town of Valley Head sits Sequoyah Caverns, a cave system named for the Cherokee silversmith who invented the Cherokee writing system, and its walls carry some of the oldest graffiti in the region. Visitors have been leaving their names and dates on the cave's formations since at least the 1820s. One especially striking inscription, carved into a column, reads simply "Sam Houston, 1830," and while nobody can prove with certainty that the future governor of Texas carved it himself, cave historians consider it likely authentic. The cave's most famous features are its reflection pools, nicknamed looking-glass lakes by generations of visitors, where still water perfectly mirrors the stone ceiling above. Commercial development of the cave as a tourist attraction began in 1965, and it later earned recognition as a top Alabama attraction in 2006. The caverns closed their doors to visitors on September 3, 2013, and have stayed closed ever since, leaving a gap in the region's roster of show caves that locals still mention with some disappointment. If you drive past the old entrance near Valley Head today, the gates are locked, but inside, Sam Houston's name, if that is really whose name it is, is still sitting quietly in the dark. Former guides who worked the cave before it closed say visitors used to linger longest at that one column, less interested in the geology around it than in the question of whether a future governor of Texas really had once stood in that exact spot with a knife in his hand.

[FACT 10: The Dam That Powered Fort Payne | 34.5099,-85.6075]

Fact ten. Just below the brink of DeSoto Falls sits a small concrete dam that most visitors barely notice on their way to photograph the waterfall, but that dam was one of the earliest small hydroelectric plants in north Alabama. A self-taught electrical engineer named Arthur Abernathy Miller built it in 1925, after years of hauling equipment up a muddy, roadless mountain trail that regularly bogged down his car, forcing him and a Baltimore investor to build the region's first real road to the site just to finish the project. Local workers helped raise the original ten-foot dam, and later community contributions paid to extend it another ten feet to enlarge the lake behind it. Once running, the small plant generated enough electricity to power Fort Payne, Mentone, Valley Head, Collinsville, and even Menlo, Georgia, just across the state line, lighting homes in five different towns from one modest mountaintop dam. All of the original generating machinery is long gone now, but the dam itself survives, standing about twenty feet tall and holding back a small lake that sits beside the parking area most visitors use to reach the falls. If you stop at that pullout before walking down to DeSoto Falls, take a second look at the plain concrete wall beside the water. It once lit up five towns before most of rural Alabama had electricity at all. Miller's achievement looks even more remarkable considering how much of rural Alabama still had no electricity at all well into the 1930s and 1940s, which means this mountaintop dam gave a handful of small towns a modern convenience years before many of their neighbors down in the valleys ever saw it.

[FACT 9: DeSoto Falls, One of Alabama's Tallest Waterfalls | 34.5099,-85.6075]

Fact nine. The West Fork of the Little River drops one hundred four feet, thirty-two meters, over a sheer sandstone cliff at DeSoto Falls, making it one of the tallest waterfalls in Alabama and the tallest one that flows all year, though the state parks system itself lists the figure at one hundred seven feet. Only Grace's High Falls in Little River Canyon drops farther, and it runs mostly after heavy rain. The falls sit about six miles north of the main developed section of DeSoto State Park, close to the town of Mentone, and a short, easy trail leads from the parking area to an overlook with a clear view down into the gorge. Unlike many of Alabama's taller waterfalls, which require long hikes or technical descents to even glimpse properly, DeSoto Falls rewards visitors who can barely manage a quarter-mile walk, which is part of why it draws heavier crowds than almost anywhere else in the park. The water collects just upstream behind the old 1925 hydroelectric dam before spilling over the final drop, meaning the falls you see today have been shaped as much by a century-old power project as by raw geology. If you drive the short spur road off the main highway near Mentone, you can be standing above one of Alabama's tallest waterfalls within fifteen minutes of leaving your car, which is a rare bargain in a landscape this dramatic. Park staff say the short walk is exactly why so many families with young children or older relatives choose DeSoto Falls over the preserve's more demanding trails, making it one of the most accessible tall waterfalls anywhere in the Southeast.

[FACT 8: Canyon Mouth Park's Dangerous Currents | 34.3340,-85.6102]

Fact eight. At the lower end of Little River Canyon sits Canyon Mouth Park, a popular riverside picnic and swimming spot that was not always part of the federal preserve at all. The hundred sixty-seven acre tract originally belonged to DeSoto State Park, and after years of struggling to maintain it, the Cherokee County Commission donated the land in 1995, folding it permanently into what is now Little River Canyon National Preserve. Today it is widely considered the most popular swimming location anywhere along the river, complete with a large picnic area, a playground, modern restrooms, and even a soccer field laid out in the flat ground near the water. The National Park Service warns visitors that swimming anywhere in the Little River is usually not a good idea because of swift and unpredictable currents, even though a few calmer pools near the park draw crowds every summer weekend. That danger became impossible to ignore on April 12, 2020, when heavy rainfall sent the Little River surging to thirty-four thousand three hundred cubic feet per second, a flood that tore through Canyon Mouth Park and caused enough damage to keep it closed for more than twelve weeks. If you visit on a calm July afternoon with families wading in the shallows, it is worth remembering that the same river can transform, almost without warning, into something powerful enough to flatten a park. Rangers say the lesson from that 2020 flood has stuck with the local community, and warning signs posted since then spell out the danger in plainer language than the park ever used before.

[FACT 7: Little River Canyon's Forgotten Old Name | 34.3561,-85.6050]

Fact seven. Before the National Park Service ever got involved, local residents didn't call this gorge Little River Canyon at all. Older maps and oral histories refer to it as May's Gulf, using a word, gulf, that was once common across the Cumberland Plateau for exactly this kind of steep, narrow gorge cut into a plateau top. The canyon only gained federal protection fairly recently in the region's long history, when Congress established Little River Canyon National Preserve in 1992, setting aside roughly fifteen thousand two hundred eighty-eight acres along the gorge and the mountaintop around it. For more than a century before that act of Congress, the land had simply been known by local names like May's Gulf, farmed and logged at the edges while the gorge itself stayed too steep and rugged to put to much practical use. That same ruggedness, which once made the land nearly worthless to nineteenth-century farmers, is precisely what preserved the canyon in close to its natural state long enough for it to later qualify for federal protection. If you mention May's Gulf to an older DeKalb County resident today, some will know exactly what you mean, even though every modern map, trail sign, and brochure calls it Little River Canyon now. Place names like gulf, cove, and hollow still dot old deeds and family stories across this stretch of the Cumberland Plateau, a quiet reminder that the federal preserve's official name is, geologically speaking, the newest thing about this canyon.

[FACT 6: The Cherokee Removal Fort Behind Fort Payne's Name | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact six. The city of Fort Payne did not take its name from any heroic founder or early settler family, but from a United States Army officer, Captain John Payne, who commanded a wooden stockade on this site in 1838 during one of the darkest chapters in American history. That stockade existed to hold Cherokee families being forced from their homes under the terms of the Treaty of New Echota, and it stood on the site of Willstown, a once-thriving Cherokee community. The fort served as both an internment camp and a staging point for removal, and it was finally abandoned after the last group, numbering one thousand one hundred three Cherokee men, women, and children, departed on October 3, 1838, on the journey history remembers as the Trail of Tears. The settlement that eventually grew up around the abandoned fort didn't take its name officially until 1869, more than three decades after the stockade itself had closed. Today, only a single chimney survives from the original structure, standing quietly in downtown Fort Payne, and in 2012 the site earned certification as an official stop on the National Park Service's Trail of Tears National Historic Trail. I used to tell my students that a town's name is sometimes the last visible trace of a history the town itself would rather not advertise, and nowhere does that lesson land harder than here, in a pleasant small city that still carries the name of the man who ran the camp. Local historians and the Cherokee descendants who still visit the Trail of Tears marker downtown both agree the single surviving chimney deserves more attention than the handful of visitors who stop to read its small plaque each year.

[FACT 5: Manitou Cave and the Secret Cherokee Writings | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact five. At the base of Lookout Mountain, near present-day Fort Payne, lies Manitou Cave, and what nineteenth-century Cherokee people wrote on its walls in near-total darkness has only recently come to light. This stretch of ground was once Willstown, the Cherokee community where a man named Sequoyah lived from around the turn of the nineteenth century until 1826, developing the written syllabary that the Cherokee Nation formally adopted as its official written language in 1825. Inside Manitou Cave, Cherokee visitors recorded ceremonies in that same syllabary directly onto the cave walls, inscriptions that sat largely unexamined until a 2019 study published in the journal Antiquity, led by researchers Beau Duke Carroll and Jan Simek, offered the first serious interpretation of what they found. One inscription describes a stickball game, a rough ancestor of modern lacrosse, played in 1828, noting that players entered the cave before the match and again during halftime, apparently as part of ritual preparation. Another passage was written backward, as though the writer meant to address someone or something already inside the rock rather than a reader standing outside it, and researchers have never fully translated its meaning. Every one of these writings dates to the handful of years just before Cherokee families across this entire region, including the very community that produced them, were forced west along the Trail of Tears. My students always assumed written history stopped the moment a culture lost its land. Manitou Cave proves the opposite, preserving a voice that was still writing, still recording its own ceremonies, right up until the end. Researchers involved in the 2019 study have said publicly that they expect more inscriptions to be identified as conservation work continues, meaning the full record inside that cave may still be only partly understood even now.

[FACT 4: One Ridge, Three States | 34.5582,-85.5755]

Fact four. Lookout Mountain, the long ridge that defines nearly every landmark in this region, from Little River Canyon to DeSoto Falls to the town of Mentone itself, is not confined to Alabama at all. The ridge runs roughly eighty-four miles, one hundred thirty-five kilometers, from Chattanooga, Tennessee, in the north down to Gadsden, Alabama, in the south, and along that run it crosses through three separate states. Only about three miles sit inside Tennessee, another thirty-one miles cut across the northwest corner of Georgia, and the remaining fifty miles, more than half the ridge's entire length, lie here in Alabama. Geologists generally classify the mountain as the southernmost extension of the Appalachian Plateau, sometimes grouped with the larger Cumberland Plateau, built from rock laid down in shallow ancient seas somewhere between five hundred forty million and three hundred million years ago, long before anything resembling a mountain range existed on this part of the continent. Its highest point rises to about two thousand three hundred ninety-three feet, seven hundred twenty-nine meters, near a spot called High Point just across the line in Georgia, while the Alabama section, including the high ground above Mentone, sits only slightly lower. A separate ridge called Sand Mountain runs beside it, divided from Lookout Mountain by a long gap known on the Alabama side as Wills Valley. If you drive north out of Fort Payne far enough, you will eventually cross two state lines without the ridge beneath your tires ever noticing the difference. Residents on the Alabama side sometimes joke that they brush up against three different states' worth of culture within the space of a single afternoon drive, since the Georgia and Tennessee portions of the ridge sit in the Eastern time zone while the Alabama stretch stays on Central time.

[FACT 3: From Mining Bust to Sock Capital of the World | 34.4443,-85.7197]

Fact three. In the mid-1880s, speculators poured money into Fort Payne chasing coal and iron deposits they believed would turn the small mountain town into a major industrial center, and for a few feverish years, new buildings went up all over downtown in anticipation of a boom that never fully arrived. When the mining investments failed to pay off and the wealthy backers pulled out, the local economy collapsed almost overnight, leaving behind empty storefronts and half-finished construction. Out of that collapse rose an entirely different industry. The Florence Knitting Company opened a hosiery mill in October 1907, and the W.B. Davis Hosiery Mill, tied to a Chattanooga manufacturer, grew into the town's largest employer over the following decades. Through most of the twentieth century, Fort Payne's sock-making industry expanded steadily, and by the 1990s the town and its surrounding county held roughly one hundred fifty separate hosiery mills employing close to eight thousand workers, producing, by some estimates, more than half of all the socks made anywhere in the United States, which is how Fort Payne earned its enduring nickname, the Sock Capital of the World. That dominance did not survive the trade agreements of the following decade. Lower tariffs on imported textiles under NAFTA and later CAFTA opened the door to cheap overseas competition, and between 2000 and 2010, mill after mill shut its doors, until most of the industry that once defined the town had quietly disappeared. Socks are still made in Fort Payne today, just in a small fraction of the old volume, and the enormous Big Mill building that once hummed with knitting machines now holds an antique store and restaurant, with a Hosiery Museum nearby keeping the memory of that era on display. If you ask someone in Fort Payne what the town used to make, almost everyone over fifty will still answer without hesitating: socks, more socks than anywhere else on earth. Former mill workers, many of them women who spent decades at the knitting machines, still gather for reunions tied to the Hosiery Museum's events, keeping alive a shared memory of shift work and factory whistles that shaped the daily rhythm of the town for the better part of a century.

[FACT 2: The Deepest Canyon East of the Mississippi | 34.3561,-85.6050]

Fact two. Cut into the top of Lookout Mountain by a river most people have never heard of, Little River Canyon reaches depths of roughly six hundred feet, one hundred eighty-three meters, making it one of the deepest canyon systems anywhere east of the Mississippi River and, locally, considered the deepest canyon in the state of Alabama. The canyon earned federal protection in 1992, when Congress created Little River Canyon National Preserve to safeguard roughly fifteen thousand two hundred eighty-eight acres along its rim and gorge, and today the Canyon Rim Parkway follows the western edge of the chasm for miles, offering a string of overlooks where visitors can stare straight down through tree canopy to the river far below. What makes the depth genuinely strange, rather than simply impressive, is where it sits: this is not a canyon cut through a lowland valley the way the Grand Canyon or most river gorges are, but one carved directly through the flat crown of a mountain that most of the surrounding landscape sits well below. I used to show my students photographs of this canyon without telling them where it was, and almost every single one guessed somewhere out west, never Alabama, and never, not once, somewhere sitting on top of a mountain ridge instead of down in a valley floor. If you stand at one of the rim overlooks along the parkway and look straight down into that gap in the rock, you are looking at geology that, by every ordinary rule of how rivers behave, should not exist in a place like this. Geologists explain the oddity by pointing to the unusually resistant sandstone capping this part of the plateau, rock hard enough that the river could only cut straight down through it rather than spreading out sideways the way water normally does when it meets softer ground.

[FACT 1: The River That Lives on Top of a Mountain | 34.4000,-85.6100]

Fact one. Nearly everything else on this list, the canyon, the waterfalls, the old hydroelectric dam, the swimming holes, all of it exists because of one genuinely strange piece of geography: the Little River itself. For almost its entire length, the river runs not through a valley, the way virtually every river on earth does, but along the flat top of Lookout Mountain, roughly thirty miles, forty-eight kilometers, of water flowing across a mountain's crown rather than around its base. The National Park Service describes it carefully, calling it the river that forms and flows for almost all of its entire length on top of a mountain, language chosen deliberately to avoid overclaiming. Some local guides go further and call it the only river in the entire country that both begins and ends on top of a mountain, a bolder statement that I could not find fully verified by any independent source, and one I would encourage you to take as local pride rather than settled fact. What is beyond dispute is the river's path: it rises near nineteen hundred feet, five hundred seventy-nine meters, above sea level on the mountain's crest, winds across that high ground gathering force, and then drops more than twelve hundred feet, three hundred sixty-six meters, in a relatively short distance as it finally spills off the mountain's edge and empties into Weiss Lake far below. Along the way, that modest, easily overlooked river does something geologically backward: instead of being shaped by a valley, it carves its own, cutting Little River Canyon straight down through solid rock while most of the surrounding mountaintop sits almost untouched around it. Every other fact on this countdown, the sock mills, the ski slope, the covered bridge, the Cherokee inscriptions in a cave at the mountain's foot, sits somewhere in the shadow of this one unlikely river that never bothered to come down off its mountain the way a river is supposed to. I spent thirty-eight years teaching students that rivers follow valleys because water always finds the lowest path available to it, and somewhere on top of Lookout Mountain, the Little River simply never got the memo, carving its own exception into solid rock instead of waiting for the landscape to hand it one.

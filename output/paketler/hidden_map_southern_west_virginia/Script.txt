[1. HOOK + PERSONA INTRO]

Southern West Virginia is home to a city named War, a river so old that geologists still argue whether it predates the very mountains it cuts through, and a mountainside that witnessed one of the only aerial bombings of American civilians in our nation's history. Beneath these ridges also lie beehive coke ovens that once lit the night sky orange, a courthouse where a famous lawman was shot down on the front steps, and a valley where two families turned a pig dispute into one of the bloodiest feuds in American memory. There is even a bridge here that held the record for the longest steel arch span on earth for a quarter century. Today we will count down twenty-five remarkable geographic facts about Southern West Virginia, from the New River Gorge down through the coalfields of McDowell, Mingo, Wyoming, and Raleigh counties.

I'm Walter Hale. I taught geography for thirty-eight years in a small high school in Kentucky, and I still can't drive through a county without wondering why it looks the way it does. Let's start at number twenty-five.

[FACT 25: New River Gorge National Park and Preserve designation | 38.07,-81.08]

Fact twenty-five. On December 27, 2020, the New River Gorge stopped being merely a national river and became the sixty-third national park in the United States, paired with a surrounding national preserve. The change was tucked into a massive year-end spending bill in Washington, pushed through by West Virginia's congressional delegation almost as an afterthought to bigger headlines. The park itself covers about 7,700 acres along the gorge rim and bottom, while the preserve around it adds roughly 64,000 more acres where hunting and fishing are still allowed, a split that lets the region keep both its wild scenery and its working rural traditions. For a place long defined by what came out of the ground, it was a strange kind of honor to be recognized instead for what grows and flows across the surface. Visitation to the gorge jumped sharply in the years after the redesignation, as travelers who had never associated West Virginia with national parks started showing up with cameras instead of coal orders. If you drive along Route 19 north of Fayetteville today, you will cross the gorge on the very bridge that made this landscape famous long before Washington gave it a new title.
[FACT 24: Beckley Exhibition Coal Mine | 37.7784,-81.1884]

Fact twenty-four. In Beckley, Raleigh County, visitors can still ride a mantrip car down into an actual drift mine that produced real coal for nearly fifty years. The story starts with a farm. John Phillips bought the land in 1850, and his family worked it for decades before noticing coal exposed along a hillside, eventually digging a small mine for their own heating and cooking by the 1880s. Commercial shipping began on January 4, 1906, under the New River Company, and the mine stayed in production until 1953, when the City of Beckley bought the property instead of letting it go to scrap and brush. City leaders waited almost a decade before reopening the old passageway for public tours, and the Exhibition Coal Mine finally welcomed its first visitors on July 23, 1962, guided underground by men who had once worked mines just like it for a living. The tour now covers about 1,500 feet of restored tunnel, alongside a reconstructed coal camp with company houses, a church, and a superintendent's home moved onto the grounds so visitors can see how miners' families actually lived. In 2022, USA Today readers ranked it among the top ten history museums in the entire country, an unusual honor for a place that began as a single family's backyard fuel supply. If you take the exit for New River Park in Beckley, you can still feel the mine's cool, damp air rising from the hillside before you ever reach the entrance.
[FACT 23: Hinton and the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway | 37.6654,-80.8992]

Fact twenty-three. Before the railroad arrived, the spot where the Greenbrier River meets the New River held only a handful of families, maybe six households scattered along the banks. Everything changed when the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway bought land there in 1871 and built a division terminal, and within a decade the town of Hinton had grown into a bustling railroad center with a roundhouse holding seventeen engine stalls and a car shop employing more than five hundred men. By the early twentieth century the population had climbed past 6,000, supporting hotels, a hospital, and enough commerce to make Hinton the seat of Summers County, largely because the railroad itself donated the land for the courthouse. The decline came gradually through the 1950s, as diesel engines replaced steam power and coal mining methods shifted, draining away the jobs that had built the town's wealth in the first place. What remains is remarkable: an 1891 passenger depot, the only one of its kind left standing in the county, and a historic district added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 that still shows off the architecture those railroad dollars once paid for. Each October, Hinton hosts Railroad Days, filling its streets with vendors, a car show, and live music to celebrate the industry that built the town and then left much of it behind. If you walk Temple Street today, a small railroad museum there holds photographs of Hinton at its steam-powered peak. I used to put a map of Appalachian rail lines on my classroom wall and ask my students to guess which little dot would grow into a city, and almost nobody ever picked the junction towns, which were usually the right answer.

[FACT 22: John Henry legend at Big Bend Tunnel | 37.6998,-80.6918]

Fact twenty-two. Near the town of Talcott in Summers County sits the mouth of the Big Bend Tunnel, bored through solid rock between 1870 and 1872 for the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway, and this is the place most commonly tied to the legend of John Henry, the steel driving man who supposedly beat a steam powered drill in a head to head contest before collapsing and dying from the effort. Historians admit the documentation is thin. Company records from that era were destroyed in an 1880 fire, and the sociologist who studied the legend most closely concluded only that a case could be made either for or against Big Bend as the true site, noting that other tunnels along the same railway share the name and that several Black laborers on the crew were known to go by John Henry. Whether or not the contest happened exactly here, the symbolism took hold powerfully in Talcott, where a statue and memorial plaque now stand along the highway crossing above the old tunnel. The John Henry Historical Park sits right at the tunnel's mouth, and a small museum nearby holds photographs, books, and local artifacts donated over the years by residents who grew up with the story as part of their family history. Every July, the town holds John Henry Days, drawing a parade, live music, and a car show to a community of only a few hundred people. If you stand at the tunnel mouth today, you can still hear trains rumble through the mountain, the same sound that inspired the ballad sung in railroad camps and coal towns across the entire region.
[FACT 21: Buffalo Creek flood disaster | 37.85,-81.93]

Fact twenty-one. On the morning of February 26, 1972, a coal waste dam on Buffalo Creek in Logan County gave way after days of heavy rain, sending more than 130 million gallons of black water and sludge crashing down the narrow hollow below. Over the next three hours the flood tore through seventeen small communities strung along the creek, demolishing homes, cars, and entire streets with almost no warning for the families living there. The official death toll stands at 125, though some state records list 123, and more than 1,000 people were injured while roughly 4,000 were left homeless in a single morning. The dam belonged to a coal company, and its first public response blamed an act of God and heavy rainfall, but later state and federal investigations found the dam had been poorly built from loose mining waste and never should have been allowed to hold back that much water. Survivors eventually settled their claims for a fraction of what they had lost once legal fees were taken out, a settlement that still feels painfully small set against what the valley endured, while the disaster itself helped push through new state and federal laws governing how coal waste dams must be built and inspected. The communities along the creek were rebuilt, smaller and more cautious, and a research center and memorial markers along Route 16 now help visitors understand what the valley endured. If you drive the hollow today, the creek itself looks almost too narrow and peaceful to have ever carried that much water.
[FACT 20: Upper Big Branch Mine disaster | 37.9256,-81.5248]

Fact twenty. On April 5, 2010, an explosion tore through the Upper Big Branch Mine near Montcoal in Raleigh County, killing twenty-nine miners in the worst coal mining disaster the United States had seen in forty years. Investigators later determined that sparks from cutting equipment ignited a pocket of methane gas, triggering a chain reaction of blasts that overwhelmed safety systems which should have prevented exactly this kind of catastrophe. The mine's owner at the time, Massey Energy, came under intense scrutiny, and its chief executive, Don Blankenship, was eventually convicted in 2015 of a misdemeanor charge related to willfully violating federal mine safety standards, drawing a one year prison sentence that many families of the victims considered far too light. Massey was bought by Alpha Natural Resources in 2011, and Alpha agreed to a settlement of more than two hundred million dollars, while the mine itself was permanently sealed in 2012, two years after the disaster. A memorial in nearby Whitesville now lists the names of all twenty-nine men, and the community gathers there to remember them each April. The disaster reshaped how Americans talked about mine safety enforcement, prompting new federal rules and tighter inspection schedules across coalfields far beyond West Virginia's borders. If you drive Route 3 through the hollow near Montcoal, little marks the spot beyond a quiet memorial, a stark contrast to how loudly the explosion was heard across the entire country.
[FACT 19: Hatfield-McCoy Trail system | 37.78,-81.87]

Fact nineteen. When coal employment collapsed across the southern coalfields in the late twentieth century, state lawmakers looked for something else the mountains could offer, and in 1996 they introduced legislation creating what would become the Hatfield-McCoy Trail system, an off-road network that today stretches across roughly 1,000 miles through nine southern West Virginia counties. The trails wind through reclaimed mining land and hardwood forest alike, built mainly for all terrain vehicles, side by sides, and dirt bikes, though a few systems like Bearwallow and Cabwaylingo also welcome full sized off-road trucks. Rockhouse, one of the three original systems, remains the largest single network in the trail family, while Devil Anse, named for the Hatfield feud patriarch, runs near Matewan, and Buffalo Mountain has become a favorite destination for dirt bike riders chasing tight single track. The system deliberately connects to small towns like Man and Gilbert, steering riders toward local diners, gas stations, and motels that depend on trail tourism the way their grandparents once depended on a coal tipple. It is open 365 days a year, rain, snow, or summer heat, and its economic footprint has become significant enough that regional newspapers still publish annual progress reports on tourism numbers two decades after the first trail opened. The name itself, borrowed from the region's most famous feud, turns a story of old violence into a shared regional brand that both Kentucky and West Virginia sides of the Tug Valley now market together. If you ride Route 44 through Logan County on a summer weekend, you will likely pass more loaded trailers hauling side by sides than coal trucks, a reversal that would have seemed impossible fifty years ago. I used to tell my students that geography rewrites its own economy generation by generation, and these trails are proof that a hollow which once shipped coal out can learn to bring visitors in instead.

[FACT 18: Devil Anse Hatfield's grave statue | 37.7865,-81.9987]

Fact eighteen. In the small Hatfield Family Cemetery near Sarah Ann in Logan County stands a larger-than-life marble statue of William Anderson Hatfield, known to history as Devil Anse, the patriarch whose name became inseparable from the entire Hatfield-McCoy feud, raised atop a pedestal that itself stands some thirteen feet tall. The statue was carved in Italy from Carrara marble, shipped across the Atlantic, and erected in 1926, five years after his death in 1921, at a cost that one source places near 3,500 dollars and another closer to 5,000, paid for by his thirteen children to honor their father. Sculptors reportedly worked from photographs and family descriptions rather than a live sitting, since Devil Anse had already passed away by the time the commission began, and the finished figure stands with his name and the names of all thirteen children engraved into the base. The statue has suffered only minor damage over the past century, including a small chipped section of the coat tail, remarkably little considering how many visitors have climbed the cemetery's steep hillside path to see it up close. Devil Anse spent his later years as a respected, even wealthy timber man along the Tug Fork, a striking transformation for someone whose name had once been synonymous with gunfire and blood feud. The cemetery sits along West Virginia Route 44, and a historical marker near the first switchback on the hillside trail helps visitors find the grave without wandering the wrong ridge. If you make the climb yourself, the white marble figure appears almost suddenly against the green hillside, taller and stranger looking than most visitors expect from old photographs alone.
[FACT 17: Winding Gulf Coalfield | 37.6967,-81.3578]

Fact seventeen. Stretching across western Raleigh County and eastern Wyoming County, the Winding Gulf Coalfield earned the nickname the Billion Dollar Coalfield in the early twentieth century, a boast that turned out to be remarkably close to accurate given how much coal eventually came out of its hollows. Geologist David Ansted had identified the region's thick bituminous seams as early as 1853, but it took decades of railroad building before the coal could reach any market, and the first real shipment did not leave until 1907, when the Slab Fork Coal Company sent a load out under the direction of the Caperton family. The Virginian Railway completed a branch line along Winding Gulf creek by 1909, running from Mullens to Pemberton, and that single stretch of track opened dozens of previously untouched mines almost overnight. Coal baron Justus Collins built company camps at Winding Gulf starting in 1910, and by the 1920s the settlement had grown into the third largest town in all of Raleigh County, its fortunes tied entirely to four productive seams known as the Pocahontas Number 3, Pocahontas Number 4, Fire Creek, and Beckley coals. The field's corporate ownership shifted repeatedly over the decades, eventually merging into the Westmoreland Coal Company by the early 1970s, while mining itself continued in the gulf into the late 1980s before finally petering out. Mining here, like across the rest of the southern coalfields, came at a real cost in miners' lives, a toll local historians have never been able to fully tally but that belongs in any honest accounting of the place. If you drive the back roads between Sophia and Mullens today, old grade cuts and scattered foundations are often the only visible trace of towns that once held thousands of residents.
[FACT 16: Twin Falls Resort State Park | 37.6273,-81.4345]

Fact sixteen. Tucked between Mullens and Pineville in Wyoming County, Twin Falls Resort State Park sits on nearly 3,800 acres donated in 1964 by two land companies with deep roots in the coal business, a gift that turned former company land into public recreation almost overnight. The state legislature formally established the park that same year, though it took until the late 1960s and early 1970s for a golf course, cabins, a swimming pool, and a full lodge with restaurant and gift shop to actually open to visitors. The park's real centerpiece, though, is its living history farm, built around the restored 1835 Bower cabin, which staff rebuilt from an original homestead that had stood on the property before development began. Completed in 1974 and staffed by live-in caretakers since 1977, the farm lets visitors watch demonstrations of pioneer era chores and crafts that have mostly vanished from daily life across the rest of the region. A powerful tornado outbreak tore through the park in the early hours of April 3, 1974, downing countless trees just as the farm restoration project was getting underway, forcing crews to rebuild twice over in the same decade. More recently, the lodge underwent a seven million dollar renovation completed in 2011, adding twenty-seven new rooms along with a swimming pool and fitness center to keep the property competitive with newer resorts elsewhere in the state. If you hike the short trail near the lodge, you can usually hear one of the twin waterfalls the park takes its name from before you ever catch sight of the water itself.
[FACT 15: Pinnacle Rock State Park | 37.3703,-81.3273]

Fact fifteen. Along US Highway 52 between the towns of Bluewell and Bramwell in Mercer County stands Pinnacle Rock, a dramatic sandstone formation whose summit sits about 3,100 feet above sea level, giving its name to a small day use park built around it. The state's Conservation Commission purchased a twenty-six acre tract surrounding the rock back in 1938, and within a few years the Works Progress Administration had built parking areas, a walkway, and a picnic shelter, giving Depression era workers steady jobs while creating a lasting public attraction. The park has since grown past 400 acres, with more than seven miles of interconnected trails leading hikers up toward an overlook near the top of the formation, where the view stretches across the ridges separating Mercer County from the rest of the coalfields to the north. A fishing lake named for longtime conservation advocate Jimmy Lewis was added in the late 1960s, giving the park a second draw beyond the rock itself and turning it into a popular weekend destination for families from nearby Bluefield. Unlike some of the larger resort parks built on former coal land elsewhere in the region, Pinnacle Rock has never offered overnight lodging, staying true to its original purpose as a simple day trip destination rather than a full vacation resort. The formation's sheer face has made it a local landmark visible from miles away along the highway, a natural signpost long before road signs told travelers they were entering Mercer County's coal country. If you pull off Route 52 at the right bend, the rock appears almost without warning above the tree line, a single sandstone spike standing apart from the rounded ridges around it.
[FACT 14: War, West Virginia | 37.3029,-81.6751]

Fact fourteen. In the farthest southern reaches of McDowell County sits the city of War, the only place in the entire United States that carries this particular name, taking its title from War Creek, which flows into the Dry Fork right within the town's limits. Before its incorporation in 1920, the settlement went by the far less memorable name Miners City, a practical label common across dozens of coal camps that sprang up along these hollows in the early twentieth century. Like so many of its neighbors, War grew up almost entirely around coal extraction in the Tug Fork valley, and its population once supported company stores, churches, and schools built to serve miners' families working the surrounding seams. Census records show a population hovering in the low 600s by 2020, a steep decline from the town's mid-century peak, and economic hardship has become a defining feature of life here as coal employment across the county has fallen for decades. Despite that decline, War still earns a geographic distinction as the southernmost incorporated municipality in all of West Virginia, sitting just a few miles from the Virginia border. Hunters and outdoor recreation travelers still pass through regularly, drawn by the nearby Berwind Lake Wildlife Management Area and its forested hollows, giving the town's handful of remaining businesses at least some steady visitor traffic. If you drive Route 16 south toward the state line, the road narrows and climbs until War appears almost suddenly in a tight hollow surrounded by ridges on every side. I used to joke with my students that West Virginia is the only state where you can casually mention driving through War on your way to lunch, and most of them never quite believed me until I showed them the map myself.

[FACT 13: Keystone's Black history and banking collapse | 37.4001,-81.4357]

Fact thirteen. The small McDowell County town of Keystone, originally known as Cassville before the Keystone Coal and Coke Company opened the area's first mine in 1892, became an unlikely center of Black political and economic life in early twentieth century West Virginia. The town elected one of the state's first Black mayors, supported the McDowell Times, a widely read minority newspaper run by publisher Matthew Thomas Whittico, and was home to Minnie Buckingham Harper, who became the first Black woman to serve in any state legislature in the entire country when she was appointed to the House of Delegates in 1928. Decades later, Keystone gained a very different kind of national attention when the First National Bank of Keystone, chartered in 1904 and later expanded into a sprawling national lending operation under Pittsburgh banker Knox McConnell, collapsed in 1999 in one of the most expensive bank failures in the history of federal deposit insurance. Federal regulators seized the bank's records amid allegations of fraud, a stunning end for an institution headquartered in a town of only a few hundred residents by that point, far removed from the banking centers most Americans would expect to produce such a scandal. The contrast between Keystone's early reputation as a hub of Black achievement and its later infamy as a financial cautionary tale captures something true about how quickly fortunes can turn in these coal dependent hollows. Local history groups and county archives have worked to preserve records connected to both chapters of the town's story, insisting that neither the achievement nor the scandal should be allowed to overshadow the other in how Keystone is remembered. If you drive through town along the Tug Fork today, little remains to suggest either its political prominence or its banking notoriety, just quiet streets and a scattering of older brick buildings.
[FACT 12: Welch and the McDowell County Courthouse | 37.4312,-81.5862]

Fact twelve. Welch, the seat of McDowell County, grew up around a courthouse built between 1893 and 1894 in the Romanesque Revival style at a cost of just over forty-three thousand dollars, including the jail that still stands behind it. The county seat itself only landed in Welch after a bitterly contested 1892 election, so contested that, according to local accounts, officials moved the county records from the previous seat at Perryville under cover of darkness, hauling them by wagon to avoid the violence that a public transfer might have triggered. Captain Isaiah Welch, the town's namesake, donated the land for both the courthouse and jail, cementing the town's new role at the center of one of the richest coalfields in the eastern United States. The courthouse's most notorious moment came on August 1, 1921, when Baldwin-Felts detectives shot and killed Matewan police chief Sid Hatfield and his friend Ed Chambers right on the courthouse steps, an assassination that helped ignite the massive labor uprising known as the Battle of Blair Mountain just weeks later. Additions to the courthouse continued for decades, including a 1909 expansion for county and city clerks and further construction between 1963 and 1979 that added a new jail wing behind the original building. The courthouse earned a place on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, preserved today as both an architectural landmark and a physical reminder of how violently the Mine Wars era played out across these coalfields. If you walk the courthouse steps in downtown Welch, a small marker now notes the assassination, though many visitors pass by without realizing exactly what happened on that spot.
[FACT 11: Bramwell, the town of millionaires | 37.3217,-81.3245]

Fact eleven. Along the Bluestone River in Mercer County sits Bramwell, a town so small today that visitors are often shocked to learn it once held more millionaires per capita than almost any other town in the United States. The settlement traces its founding to 1888, when the Flat Top Coal Company, tied closely to the Norfolk and Western Railway, built a new community along the riverbank and named it after civil engineer Joseph Bramwell of New York. As Pocahontas coalfield wealth poured in during the 1880s and 1890s, Bramwell's banks became the financial backbone of the entire southern coalfield region, with accounts estimating that somewhere between fourteen and nineteen millionaires, along with their extended families, called the town home at its peak. A devastating fire tore through much of downtown Bramwell in 1910, but the town rebuilt quickly, a testament to just how much capital was still flowing through its banks at the time. That wealth could not survive the Great Depression, however, and the once powerful Bank of Bramwell closed its doors in the early 1930s, marking the symbolic end of the town's gilded age. What remains today is a remarkable collection of Victorian mansions, many built by coal operators who wanted their homes to match their bank balances, and the entire district earned a spot on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983. Tourists now walk the same streets where coal barons once compared fortunes, and several of the old mansions operate seasonal tours that let visitors step inside homes built on Pocahontas coal money. If you drive Route 52 into Bramwell today, the mansions still rise above a town of fewer than four hundred residents, an odd and striking mismatch that tells the whole history in a single glance.
[FACT 10: Kaymoor's coke ovens | 38.0598,-81.0661]

Fact ten. Deep in the New River Gorge, the Low Moor Iron Company of Virginia bought land at Kaymoor in 1873 but waited more than two decades before opening an actual mine there in 1899, finally shipping its first coal down the gorge in 1900. What made Kaymoor distinctive was not just the coal itself but the rows of beehive coke ovens built near the railroad tracks at the bottom of the gorge, where raw coal was sealed inside dome shaped brick ovens and burned for several days until it converted into coke, the purified fuel needed for blast furnaces making iron and steel. Moving workers and supplies between the gorge rim settlement and the river bottom town required an unusual single track incline, powered by a steam driven cable hoisting drum, a system that operated with only minor modifications from 1900 all the way through 1962. Low Moor sold the entire complex to the Berwind-White company in 1925, just as the national market for merchant pig iron was declining and beehive coke ovens everywhere were falling out of favor for more efficient industrial processes. Much of the coal and coke produced at Kaymoor in its later decades went toward fueling United States Navy ships, tying this one narrow gorge hollow directly to national military supply lines most visitors would never guess from looking at the quiet forest there today. The mine finally closed for good in 1962, and the gorge slowly reclaimed most of the site, leaving only scattered foundations, one gutted house, and one collapsed structure to mark where an entire working community once stood. If you hike the Kaymoor Trail today, rows of crumbling coke ovens still line the old railroad grade, dark brick arches standing silent against the green hillside above the New River.
[FACT 9: Nuttallburg and Henry Ford's coal venture | 38.0661,-81.0509]

Fact nine. In 1870, an English-born entrepreneur named John Nuttall began buying land along Keeneys Creek in the New River Gorge, building the infrastructure that would let his namesake town become the second mining settlement in the gorge to ship the prized smokeless coal once the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway reached the area in 1873. Nuttallburg's most surprising chapter arrived decades later in the 1920s, when automobile industrialist Henry Ford leased the town's mines outright, part of his broader push toward vertical integration that aimed to control every stage of production for his company, right down to the coal that fueled his steel mills. Ford's plan at Nuttallburg ultimately failed to deliver the efficiencies he had hoped for, and ownership passed through three more companies before the mine finally ceased production altogether in 1958, closing out nearly ninety years of continuous coal extraction along that single creek. What the National Park Service preserves today includes a massive coal tipple used to sort and load coal onto waiting rail cars, along with a 1,385 foot long conveyor belt system that once hauled coal down the steep hillside from the mine entrance to the processing area below. Reaching the site still requires driving the narrow, winding, and steep Keeneys Creek Road, a route the park service itself warns visitors to approach carefully, a small taste of just how difficult daily travel through this gorge once was for the miners who lived and worked here. The site's connection to one of America's most famous industrialists gives it a kind of name recognition that many other abandoned coal towns in the gorge never quite achieve, even though the mine itself operated on a modest scale compared to larger regional operations. If you hike down to the tipple structure today, its rusted steel frame still towers dramatically above the forest floor, an industrial skeleton in an otherwise quiet, reclaimed hollow. I used to tell my students that even the biggest names in American industry occasionally gambled on these remote hollows and lost, and Nuttallburg is proof that fame elsewhere never guaranteed success down here.

[FACT 8: Thurmond, the railroad ghost town | 37.9586,-81.0775]

Fact eight. On the banks of the New River inside what is now New River Gorge National Park and Preserve sits Thurmond, a town that thrived as a bustling railroad hub in the early twentieth century, when coal shipments, passengers, and commerce all passed through its depot at a pace that seems almost impossible to imagine given how quiet the town has become. At its peak, Thurmond's rail yards and depot handled enormous volumes of coal traffic moving along the Chesapeake and Ohio line, supporting banks, hotels, and storefronts that served railroad workers and travelers passing through the gorge. The decline mirrored so many other coal towns across the region, as diesel locomotives replaced steam power and the coal industry itself contracted, draining away both the jobs and the population that had sustained the town for decades. Today Thurmond's full time population sits at roughly five residents, making it one of the most dramatic examples of a boomtown turned near ghost town anywhere in Appalachia, even though its buildings remain remarkably intact compared to many other abandoned coal settlements. The Thurmond Depot still stands as the town's centerpiece, its wooden platform still used by passing trains today, while old bank buildings and houses line the narrow street running alongside the tracks. Entry to the town is free, and visitors are welcome to wander among the historic structures at their own pace, with leashed dogs permitted along the quiet main street. If you walk through Thurmond on a weekday afternoon, you may see only one or two other visitors, a strange stillness for a place that once handled some of the busiest rail traffic in the entire state.
[FACT 7: Grandview's panoramic overlook | 37.8372,-81.0867]

Fact seven. High above the New River near Beaver in Raleigh County sits Grandview, home to one of the most dramatic overlooks in the entire gorge, where the main viewing platform stands roughly 1,400 feet above the river below. The state of West Virginia purchased fifty-two acres here in 1939 to build a day use park, and Civilian Conservation Corps crews spent the following years constructing the roads, shelters, and picnic areas that still anchor the site today, before the land eventually transferred to the National Park Service in 1990. On a clear day, the main overlook offers a panorama stretching more than fifteen miles, taking in seven miles of the New River and its surrounding watershed in a single sweeping view that regularly ranks among the most photographed vistas in the entire state. A second viewpoint called Turkey Spur Overlook sits along a narrow ridge surrounded on three sides by the river below, giving visitors an even more dramatic sense of standing suspended above the gorge. The seasonal blooms here draw their own crowds, with purple Catawba rhododendrons flowering in mid-May and white great rhododendrons following in July, turning the hillside trails into a changing display across the warmer months. Camping is not permitted directly at Grandview, though nearby Little Beaver State Park offers a nearby campground for visitors wanting to extend their stay in the immediate area. If you arrive early enough on a winter morning, the valley below often sits wrapped in fog while the overlook itself stays clear, giving the impression of standing above the clouds rather than above a river gorge.
[FACT 6: Sandstone Falls, the widest falls on the New River | 37.6866,-80.8554]

Fact six. About eight and a half miles below the town of Hinton, the New River widens to roughly 1,500 feet across and tumbles over Sandstone Falls, widely described as the largest waterfall anywhere along the entire river's course through the gorge. Several small islands split the falls into separate channels, each dropping somewhere between ten and twenty-five feet, creating a complex, braided waterfall rather than a single clean drop most visitors might expect from the name alone. The falls formed as softer layers of shale and conglomerate eroded away beneath a much harder cap of sandstone, leaving overhanging ledges that periodically break off in sections and tumble into the river below, a slow motion collapse that has been reshaping the falls for thousands of years. On the eastern side of the gorge, an overlook along State Route 20 provides a sweeping view of the falls from roughly 600 feet above the water, while a boardwalk on the western side crosses two small bridges leading visitors out onto islands and observation decks positioned right at river level. The National Park Service calls Sandstone Falls the largest waterfall on the entire New River, and at least one hiking guide goes further, describing it as the widest and most structurally complex waterfall anywhere in the state of West Virginia. Unlike the dramatic single drop waterfalls found elsewhere in Appalachia, Sandstone Falls rewards patience, since its full complexity only becomes clear once visitors walk the boardwalk and see how many separate channels the river has carved across the rock. If you visit after a heavy rain, the falls roar loud enough to hear from the parking area, while a dry summer week can reduce the same falls to a gentle, almost musical trickle across exposed stone.
[FACT 5: The Hatfield-McCoy feud | 37.6368,-82.1646]

Fact five. Along the Tug Fork, the river that still forms the border between West Virginia and Kentucky, the Hatfield family of Logan and later Mingo County and the McCoy family of Pike County, Kentucky, fought a feud so bitter and so prolonged that their names became permanent shorthand for Appalachian family conflict across the entire country. Most accounts trace the roots back to a disputed 1878 hog trial, when Floyd Hatfield was accused by Randolph McCoy of stealing a pig, a case decided against the McCoys largely on the testimony of a local man who was later killed by two McCoy brothers in apparent retaliation. Tensions boiled over completely during an election day confrontation in 1882, when a brawl left Ellison Hatfield mortally wounded, triggering a cycle of retaliatory killings that would stretch on for years and claim more than a dozen lives from both families combined. One of the feud's most haunting episodes played out across the state line in Kentucky, where the Hatfields tied several McCoy sons to pawpaw bushes and shot them, a site still marked today by a highway marker along Kentucky Route 1056. The feud's patriarch, William Anderson Hatfield, known as Devil Anse, eventually outlived most of the violence and died peacefully in 1921, buried beneath the towering marble statue his children later built near Sarah Ann in Logan County. Modern tourism has reshaped how both states present this history, with a dedicated driving tour linking sites on both sides of the Tug Fork, including the Coal House building in Williamson that now serves as a local visitor center stocked with feud related artifacts and maps. Local leaders have worked for decades to balance public fascination with the feud against the discomfort many area families still feel about having their ancestors' worst years turned into a tourist attraction. If you drive along the Tug Fork today, quiet hollows on either side give almost no hint of the violence that once defined this stretch of river, aside from the scattered historical markers placed carefully along the roadside. I used to tell my students that the Hatfields and McCoys became famous not because their feud was uniquely brutal by frontier standards, but because journalists in the 1880s discovered that readers across the entire country loved a story about mountain families settling scores with rifles, and that fascination never fully faded.

[FACT 4: The West Virginia Mine Wars | 37.8497,-81.8303]

Fact four. Beginning with a January 1920 organizing campaign by the United Mine Workers of America, tensions across the non-union coalfields of Mingo, Logan, and McDowell counties escalated into open violence that historians now collectively call the Mine Wars, among the most significant and most often overlooked labor conflicts in American history. The first major clash came on May 19, 1920, in Matewan, when Baldwin-Felts detectives arrived to evict striking miners' families from company housing, triggering a gunfight that left seven detectives, two miners, and the town's mayor dead on the streets within minutes. Police chief Sid Hatfield, who fired on the detectives alongside seventeen other strikers, stood trial for murder the following year and was acquitted, only to be assassinated himself outside the McDowell County Courthouse in Welch on August 1, 1921, an act that finally pushed thousands of angry miners toward open rebellion. Within weeks, roughly 10,000 armed coal miners marched toward Logan County intending to force unionization at gunpoint, clashing with around 3,000 lawmen and private detectives along Blair Mountain in what became the largest armed labor uprising in the history of the United States. The fighting raged for more than a week before federal troops finally intervened, and in a detail that still startles most visitors, bombs rained down on miner positions from private airplanes hired by the coal operators, and by some accounts from military aircraft as well, making Blair Mountain one of the only instances of aerial bombing used against American civilians on domestic soil. Estimates of the dead from the battle itself range widely, from as few as sixteen miners according to some accounts to as many as one hundred according to others, a gap that reflects just how chaotic record keeping became once thousands of armed men scattered across a single mountainside. The Mine Wars ultimately failed to deliver immediate unionization, since most of the southern coalfields would not see widespread union recognition for another decade, but the conflict permanently reshaped how both companies and the federal government approached labor relations across the entire Appalachian coal industry. If you drive Route 17 through Blair today, historical markers along the roadside are often the only visible sign that an actual military engagement once stretched across these quiet, wooded ridges.
[FACT 3: The Pocahontas coal seam | 37.4556,-81.4987]

Fact three. Sometime around the Civil War, a blacksmith named Jordan Nelson, working near the Virginia town of Pocahontas, dug into a coal seam roughly thirteen feet thick, a discovery that would eventually reshape the economy of several counties just across the border in West Virginia. Surveyors Isaiah Welch and Jedediah Hotchkiss mapped the seam in 1873 and promoted it aggressively to northern investors, who quickly purchased some 40,000 acres of what would become known as the Flat Top-Pocahontas coalfield, a formation that eventually stretched deep into McDowell, Mercer, and Wyoming counties. The Norfolk and Western Railway reached the seam in March of 1883, setting off a wave of rapid industrialization across the region as company after company built mines, coal camps, and rail spurs into the steep ravines wherever the seam could be reached. The coal itself proved exceptionally valuable, prized as a low-sulfur, nearly smokeless fuel ideal for coking into steelmaking fuel and favored by the United States Navy as its preferred steam coal during the entire age of coal-fired warships. Dozens of company towns sprang up along the seam almost overnight, places like Elkhorn, Maybeury, Upland, and Keystone, built into tight ravines near the mine entrances and largely abandoned once those same mines eventually closed decades later. Labor here drew an unusually diverse workforce for the era, with a 1923 survey finding that nearly a third of the region's miners were African American and roughly one in five were foreign born, a mix of backgrounds unusual for rural Appalachia at the time. Of the original three billion tons of coal estimated in the field when mining began, roughly 900 million tons remain unmined today, according to the West Virginia Encyclopedia, meaning this single seam has already produced more than two-thirds of everything it ever held. If you drive US Highway 52 through McDowell County, the Coal Heritage Trail signs marking the route are a direct nod to just how thoroughly this one geological formation defined the entire local economy for more than a century.
[FACT 2: The New River's disputed ancient age | 37.75,-80.90]

Fact two. The New River is widely repeated to be the second oldest river on earth, trailing only the Nile, a claim so often stated as fact that many visitors assume it has been scientifically settled, when in reality geologists remain genuinely divided on the question. Age estimates for the river span an enormous range, from roughly three million years on the low end to as much as 320 million years on the high end, a gap so large that it reveals just how difficult it actually is to date a river rather than the rocks surrounding it. The West Virginia Geological and Economic Survey has promoted the higher estimate, arguing that the New River must be older than the very Appalachian Mountains it cuts through, since its course appears to predate the uplift of the ridges around it. Critics, including geologist Philip Prince in a public television interview, have pushed back hard on that logic, pointing out that nobody has ever directly dated a river channel and that the underlying assumption, that the New fully cuts through the mountains rather than simply following an older valley, remains unproven. The National Park Service takes a more cautious position, describing the New as possibly one of the oldest river systems on the planet and noting that this stretch likely follows the ancient course of the long vanished Teays River system that once drained a much larger portion of the continent. Whatever its true age, the New River's physical behavior supports the ancient river argument in at least one sense: it flows north, cutting directly through the grain of the Appalachian Mountains rather than around them, the kind of course typically associated with rivers old enough to have existed before the mountains rose. The debate has become part of the region's identity regardless of its scientific resolution, repeated in park brochures, tourism materials, and classroom lessons across the state for generations now. If you stand at any overlook along the gorge and watch the river running due north instead of toward the ocean, you are looking at the exact behavior that convinced generations of geologists this river deserved its reputation as one of the oldest on the planet.
[FACT 1: The New River Gorge Bridge | 38.0676,-81.0742]

Fact one. Completed on October 22, 1977, the New River Gorge Bridge near Fayetteville carries US Route 19 across a canyon 876 feet deep on a steel arch spanning 1,700 feet, a single engineering achievement that for twenty-six years held the title of the longest steel arch span on the entire planet. The bridge's overall length runs roughly 3,030 feet, built using more than 21,000 tons of structural steel and weighing in at well over 88 million pounds once fully assembled, figures that still astonish engineers visiting the site today. Designed by Michael Baker Junior Incorporated and constructed by the American Bridge Division of United States Steel at a total cost of 37 million dollars, the bridge replaced a winding, 40 minute drive down into the gorge and back up the other side with a 45 second crossing high above the river. It is also one of the highest vehicular bridges in the United States, and even today sources differ on whether it ranks second or third tallest in the country. Every October, the bridge becomes the centerpiece of Bridge Day, when the span closes to traffic and thousands of spectators watch trained athletes legally base jump and rappel from the deck, turning the same structure that once simply connected two sides of a canyon into one of the most recognizable adventure sports festivals in the country. The bridge's image now appears on West Virginia's state quarter, a permanent reminder that this single span has become as central to the state's identity as any mountain range or river ever could. Engineers still study the structure for its clever use of a deck truss system suspended above the arch itself, a design choice that let builders avoid constructing the bridge from the canyon floor upward, a method that would have taken years longer and risked far more lives in a gorge this steep and this deep. If you pull into the overlook at the Canyon Rim Visitor Center on a clear afternoon, the bridge appears to float impossibly above the gorge, a single curved line of steel holding together two halves of mountain that nature itself never intended to connect.

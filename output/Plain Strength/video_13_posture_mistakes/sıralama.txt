9. First, why does posture change how strong you look, even when your muscles stay exactly the same.
10. When your head drifts forward and your shoulders round, your upper body takes up less space.
11. Your chest looks smaller, your shoulders look narrower, and you look shorter than you really are.
12. Stand tall, and the same frame suddenly looks broader, taller, more solid, and far more confident.
13. We covered this briefly in our narrow shoulders video, and now we go deeper.
14. Posture is also one of the few things about your body you can change in a single second.
15. A two thousand sixteen study by Vacharkulksemsuk and colleagues looked at posture and first impressions.
16. In speed dating and a dating app test, open, expansive postures were more likely to get a yes.
17. The researchers linked expansive posture with looking more dominant and more open at first sight.
18. Posture may also affect how you feel inside, not just how other people see you from outside.
19. A two thousand fifteen study by Nair and colleagues had people sit slumped or upright under stress.
20. Their posture was held in place with tape, so the groups really did sit differently.
21. Upright participants reported higher self esteem, better mood, and less fear than slumped participants.
22. The slumped group also used more negative emotion words when they spoke during the task.
23. It was one small study, so treat it as a useful signal rather than a final answer.
24. Still, it fits what most of us feel, sitting slumped rarely makes anyone feel powerful.
25. So let us go through the six posture mistakes one by one, starting with the most common.
26. Mistake one, letting your head drift forward, so it sits in front of your shoulders, not over them.
27. Hours of phones, laptops, and driving pull the head forward, and many men stay that way all day.
28. From the side, a forward head makes your neck look thin and your upper back look rounded.
29. The fix starts with a simple move called the chin tuck, gently drawing your head straight back.
30. Imagine making a slight double chin, keeping your eyes level, without tipping your head down.
31. Hold for a few seconds, relax, and repeat a few times during the day, especially at your desk.
32. Chin tucks can feel strange at first, but they get easier and more natural within a few weeks.
33. Strengthening the neck, as in our hard to mess with video, also helps hold the head up.
34. Bring your screen closer to eye level, so you are not looking down for hours at a time.
35. If you use a phone a lot, lift it closer to your face instead of dropping your head.
36. Mistake two, rounded shoulders, where your shoulders roll forward and your chest seems to collapse.
37. This often comes from lots of pushing, sitting, and phone time, with little work for the upper back.
38. A two thousand ten study by Lynch and colleagues gave elite college swimmers an eight week exercise program.
39. The program combined upper back strengthening exercises with simple chest and shoulder stretching work.
40. After eight weeks, the exercise group showed less forward head and less rounded shoulder posture.
41. Their shoulder girdle strength also improved, though the study was small and not blinded.
42. A separate small trial found that stretching and strengthening both improved head and shoulder posture in healthy adults.
43. So the fix combines both, strengthen the upper back and open up the front of the chest.
44. Rows, face pulls, band pull aparts, and reverse flyes train the muscles that pull your shoulders back.
45. A doorway chest stretch, held gently for a short time, opens up the front of the shoulders.
46. And remember from our push up video, balance every pushing set with a pulling set.
47. A simple rule is to do at least as many pulling sets as pushing sets each week.
48. And here is a rule that applies to every exercise in this video and on this channel.
49. If anything causes sharp pain, numbness, or tingling, stop, and talk to a doctor or physical therapist.
50. Posture work should feel like gentle effort and stretch, never like sharp pain in a joint.
51. If you have a known back or neck condition, ask your doctor which exercises are right for you.
52. Mistake three, slumping when you sit, which is where most men spend much of their day.
53. Remember the Nair study, slumped sitting during stress was linked to lower mood and self esteem.
54. Many men slump at a desk, in the car, and on the couch for hours every single day.
55. Over time, slumping becomes your default shape, and it follows you when you stand up too.
56. The fix is not sitting perfectly still, it is sitting tall more often and moving regularly.
57. Sit back in your chair, let your lower back rest against support, and let your chest lift.
58. Keep your feet flat, your shoulders relaxed, and your screen roughly at eye level.
59. Every half hour or so, stand up, stretch, and walk for a minute to reset your posture.
60. Small resets during the day add up far more than one perfect posture held for a moment.
61. A standing desk can help some people, but even then, changing position often matters most.
62. Mistake four, standing small, with arms crossed, hunched shoulders, and a body that takes up little space.
63. People read closed body language quickly, and it can make even a big man look smaller.
64. Remember the Vacharkulksemsuk study, open and expansive postures were linked to a better first impression.
65. That does not mean spreading out aggressively, it means standing in your natural space.
66. Stand with your feet about hip width apart, weight even, and knees soft, not locked.
67. Let your arms hang relaxed by your sides, or use them naturally when you talk.
68. Lift your chest gently, let your shoulder blades settle back and down, and keep your chin level.
69. When you walk into a room, take a breath, stand tall, and pause a moment before you speak.
70. This small habit costs nothing, yet it changes the first impression you make every single time.
71. Mistake five, believing a two minute power pose will change your hormones and fix everything.
72. A famous study claimed that holding a power pose for a short time changed hormone levels.
73. A two thousand fifteen replication by Ranehill and colleagues tested this with two hundred people.
74. They found that power poses made people feel more powerful, but found no effect on hormones.
75. They also found no effect on the behavior tests they measured in that study.
76. So posture may help how you feel, but it is not a hormone switch or a magic trick.
77. Feeling more powerful is still useful, but it should not be confused with a physical change.
78. The real benefit comes from a strong body that holds good posture easily, every single day.
79. And trying to hold a stiff, military pose all day usually fails, because it is tiring and unnatural.
80. A review by Swain and colleagues found no clear consensus that specific spine postures cause back pain.
81. So there is no single perfect posture you must hold, and you do not need to fear movement.
82. The goal is a strong, relaxed, upright default, with plenty of movement throughout the day.
83. Your best posture is often your next posture, so change position and keep moving often.
84. Mistake six, trying to fix posture with reminders alone, without building the strength to hold it.
85. You can tell yourself to stand tall all day, but weak muscles will pull you back down.
86. Posture is easiest to hold when the muscles behind it are strong and used regularly.
87. Your upper back, rear shoulders, and neck hold your head and shoulders in a strong position.
88. Your core and glutes help keep your hips and lower back stable when you stand and walk.
89. Rows, face pulls, and reverse flyes train the upper back, and planks and bridges train the core.
90. Two or three short sessions each week can make good posture feel natural instead of forced.
91. Combine that strength with small daily resets, and good posture slowly becomes your default.
92. Think of strength as the foundation, and daily habits as the walls that keep everything standing.

17. The trapezius, or traps, is a large diamond shaped muscle running from your neck down your upper back.
18. The upper part of the traps runs from the base of your skull to your shoulders.
19. Developed upper traps create that sloped, solid line from neck to shoulder that people notice from the front.
20. The middle and lower traps sit between your shoulder blades and add thickness seen from behind.

25. Let us go through the five exercises one by one, starting with the classic trap builder.
26. Number one, the shrug, the simplest and most direct exercise for your upper traps.
27. A shrug is just what it sounds like, you lift your shoulders straight up toward your ears.
28. A two thousand three study by Ekstrom and colleagues measured trap activity in thirty healthy people.
29. They tested ten different exercises for the trapezius and the muscles around the shoulder blade.
30. The one arm shrug produced the highest upper trap activity of all ten exercises they tested.
31. That makes the shrug one of the most direct tools for building the upper traps specifically.
32. You can shrug with dumbbells, a barbell, or even heavy bags, so it fits any home or gym.
33. Stand tall, hold the weights at your sides, and lift your shoulders straight up, not rolling them.
34. Pause for a moment at the top, then lower the weight slowly under control.
35. Rolling your shoulders in circles adds nothing useful, so keep the movement straight up and down.
36. Keep your neck long and your chin level, without jutting your head forward as you lift.
37. Many men use far too much weight and barely move, so use a load you can lift fully.
38. Shrugging one arm at a time, like in the study, also lets you focus on each side.
39. Number two, direct neck training, the exercise almost nobody does and almost everybody notices.
40. Many men assume their neck will grow from regular training, like squats, deadlifts, and rows.
41. A study published in nineteen ninety seven by Conley and colleagues tested exactly that idea.
42. Active college students trained for twelve weeks, some with general lifting, some adding neck exercises.
43. Both training groups did big lifts like squats, deadlifts, push presses, and rows, three days a week.
44. Only one group also did a head extension exercise, lifting a light weight with a head harness.
45. Muscle size was measured with scans, and only the group with direct neck work grew their neck muscles.
46. The general lifting group, despite all those heavy lifts, showed no change in neck muscle size.
47. So if you want a thicker neck, the research suggests you need to train it directly.
48. The neck is delicate, so this is the one exercise where patience matters more than anything else.
49. Start with no equipment at all, using your own hand to gently resist your head as you move.
50. Push your forehead into your palm and let your neck slowly bend forward against light resistance.
51. Then do the same at the back of your head, and on each side, always slow and controlled.
52. Over weeks, you can move to a light plate on a towel or a head harness, slowly.
53. Never jerk, bounce, or rush neck movements, and never use heavy weights on your neck.
54. And here is a rule that applies to every exercise in this video and on this channel.
55. If anything causes sharp pain, dizziness, or tingling, stop, and talk to a doctor or physical therapist.
56. That is especially true for the neck, so if you have any neck history, get checked before starting.
57. Number three, the farmer's carry, an old strongman event that builds traps, grip, and a rock solid core.
58. You simply pick up two heavy weights, one in each hand, and walk with them under control.
59. A two thousand nine study by McGill and colleagues measured muscle activity during several strongman events.
60. During the farmer's walk, the abdominals and many back muscles worked hard to keep the body upright.
61. The latissimus dorsi and the spinal erectors peaked as the legs swung forward with each step.
62. Your traps work the whole time too, holding your shoulders up against the pull of the weight.
63. That is why carries build the kind of thick, solid upper body that people associate with real strength.
64. You can carry dumbbells, kettlebells, heavy bags, or water jugs, so it works anywhere.
65. Stand tall at the start, shoulders back and down, chest up, weights at your sides.
66. Walk with short, controlled steps, and do not let the weights swing or pull you sideways.
67. Keep your gaze forward and your breathing steady, and set the weights down with a straight back.
68. When picking up and setting down the weights, bend your knees and keep your spine neutral.
69. Carries also train grip strength, which we will cover in a full video of its own soon.
70. Number four, the overhead press, the lift that builds shoulders and traps at the same time.
71. Pressing a weight overhead takes your shoulders, your triceps, and your upper traps working together.
72. As your arms rise overhead, your shoulder blades rotate upward, and the upper traps help drive that.
73. A two thousand twenty two study by Coratella and colleagues measured muscle activity in overhead press variations.
74. They tested barbell and machine presses in front of and behind the head with competitive bodybuilders.
75. The authors described the overhead press as suitable to stimulate the upper traps, deltoids, and triceps.
76. Barbell versions produced more overall activity than machines, since the lifter must also steady the bar.
77. Pressing in front of your head is the simpler place to start for most men.
78. Start standing tall with the bar at your upper chest, elbows slightly in front of the bar.
79. Squeeze your glutes and brace your stomach, so your lower back does not arch as you press.
80. Press the bar straight up, moving your head slightly back, then forward once the bar passes your face.
81. Finish with the bar over the middle of your head, arms locked, shoulders shrugged up slightly.
82. Dumbbells work well too, and they let each arm move in its own natural path.
83. Number five, the deadlift, the lift that builds thickness across your entire back from top to bottom.
84. The deadlift means lifting a loaded bar from the floor to standing, using your hips and legs.
85. While your legs and hips drive the lift, your upper back and traps hold the weight in place.
86. A two thousand two study by Escamilla and colleagues recorded sixteen muscles in thirteen college football players.
87. The upper traps were active during the lift, especially in the lower part near the floor.
88. Holding a heavy bar forces your traps and upper back to work hard just to keep position.
89. That is one reason men who deadlift regularly can look thick and solid from behind.
90. The deadlift is a complex lift, so learning good form matters more than lifting heavy fast.
91. Start with the bar over the middle of your feet, shins close, hands just outside your legs.
92. Hinge at the hips, bend your knees, flatten your back, and take the slack out of the bar.
93. Push the floor away and stand up tall, keeping the bar close to your legs the whole time.
94. Finish standing straight, without leaning back, then lower the bar with the same flat back.
95. If the barbell feels awkward, a trap bar or heavy dumbbells can teach the same movement more safely.
96. Never round your lower back to grind out a rep, because that is how deadlifts go wrong.

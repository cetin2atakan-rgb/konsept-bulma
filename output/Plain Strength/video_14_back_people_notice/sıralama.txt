9. First, let us look at the map, because your back is not one muscle, it is a team.
10. The lats are the large, wide muscles that run from under your arms down toward your lower back.
11. Developed lats create width, the classic tapered shape that people see from the front and from behind.
12. The traps and rhomboids sit across your upper back, between and around your shoulder blades.
13. These muscles create thickness, the dense, solid look that makes a back seem built rather than flat.
14. Along your spine run the erectors, long muscles that keep you upright and add lower back depth.
15. Width and thickness together are what make a back stand out, even under a plain shirt.
16. Most men have one or the other, and the men who stand out usually have both.
17. And because most men train what they see in the mirror, the back is often the weakest link.
18. Remember from our shoulders video, judged strength explained most of how attractive men's bodies were rated.
19. A broad, thick back is one of the clearest visual signals of upper body strength.
20. The good news is that the back responds very well to a few simple pulling movements.
21. You do not need fancy machines, a bar, a band, and a sturdy table go a long way.
22. Let us go through the five exercises one by one, starting with the classic width builder.
23. Number one, the pull up and the chin up, the bodyweight lifts that build width like nothing else.
24. A pull up uses an overhand grip, and a chin up uses an underhand grip, palms toward you.
25. A two thousand ten study by Youdas and colleagues measured muscle activity during pull ups and chin ups.
26. Both exercises produced very high activity in the lats, well above a maximal static contraction.
27. The chin up produced more biceps and chest activity, while the pull up worked the lower traps more.
28. So both are excellent for the lats, and the grip mainly shifts which helper muscles join in.
29. Choose the grip that feels best on your shoulders and elbows, and use both over time.
30. Start from a full hang with straight arms, then pull your chest toward the bar.
31. Think about driving your elbows down toward your back pockets, rather than just pulling with your hands.
32. Lower yourself slowly all the way down, because the lowering phase is part of the work.
33. If you cannot do a pull up yet, use a band for help, or start with slow lowering.
34. Even a few slow lowering reps each session build the strength for your first full pull up.
35. Number two, the lat pulldown, and the grip debate that has filled gyms for decades.
36. Many men believe that a very wide grip is the secret to a wider back.
37. A two thousand ten study by Lusk and colleagues compared grip widths and hand positions on the pulldown.
38. In their study, an overhand grip produced more lat activity than an underhand grip, whatever the width.
39. A two thousand fourteen study compared narrow, medium, and wide overhand grips in fifteen men.
40. Lat activity was similar across all three grip widths when they looked at the whole movement.
41. The men were also slightly stronger with the narrow and medium grips than with the wide grip.
42. A two thousand twenty five study testing seven pulldown variations found no significant difference in muscle activation.
43. So grip width is not a magic switch, and the endless debate matters less than you think.
44. Pick a grip slightly wider than your shoulders that feels strong, and focus on effort and range instead.
45. Pull the bar to your upper chest, lean back only slightly, and control it on the way up.
46. Let your arms straighten fully at the top, so your lats get a full stretch on every rep.
47. Some men like a neutral grip handle, palms facing each other, which many find easier on the shoulders.
48. And here is a rule that applies to every exercise in this video and on this channel.
49. If anything causes sharp pain in your back, shoulders, or elbows, stop and see a doctor.
50. Back training should feel like hard muscle work, never like sharp pain in your spine or joints.
51. If you have a back injury or condition, ask your doctor which pulling exercises are right for you.
52. Number three, the inverted row, the underrated bodyweight row that builds the upper back.
53. You lie under a bar or a sturdy table, grip it, and pull your chest up toward it.
54. A two thousand nine study by Fenwick, Brown, and McGill compared three different rowing exercises.
55. The inverted row produced the highest lat, upper back, and hip extensor activity of the three.
56. It also produced the lowest load on the lower back of the three rows they tested.
57. The authors suggested it as a good choice for people who want to spare their lower back.
58. That makes the inverted row a strong choice at home, or when your lower back needs a break.
59. Set a bar in a rack at about waist height, or use the edge of a sturdy table.
60. Keep your body straight from heels to head, like a plank turned upside down.
61. Pull until your chest nearly touches the bar, squeezing your shoulder blades together at the top.
62. To make it harder, raise your feet on a box, or lower the bar closer to the floor.
63. Inverted rows are also a great way to build up to your first pull up.
64. Number four, the bent over row, the heavy row that builds thickness from top to bottom.
65. In the Fenwick study, the bent over row activated the back evenly from top to bottom.
66. But it also produced the largest load on the lower spine of the three rows they tested.
67. That is not a reason to avoid it, it is a reason to respect it and lift well.
68. Start standing with a barbell, then hinge at your hips until your torso leans well forward.
69. Keep your back flat and your stomach braced, with your knees slightly bent throughout the whole set.
70. Pull the bar toward your lower ribs, then lower it under control without letting your back round.
71. If your lower back tires before your upper back, switch to a chest supported row on a bench.
72. A chest supported row lets your upper back work hard while the bench takes stress off your spine.
73. One arm dumbbell rows, with a hand on a bench, are another back friendly choice.
74. Whichever row you choose, aim for a hard squeeze between the shoulder blades on every rep.
75. Number five, the straight arm pulldown, the move that trains the lats without letting the arms take over.
76. In most pulling exercises, your biceps help, and in some men they tire before the back does.
77. The straight arm pulldown keeps your elbows nearly straight, so the lats do most of the work.
78. Stand facing a cable or band anchored high, arms straight in front, elbows slightly bent.
79. Sweep the handle down in an arc toward your thighs, keeping your arms long.
80. Squeeze your lats at the bottom, then let the handle rise slowly back to the start.
81. Keep your torso still, because leaning and swinging moves the work away from your back.
82. This is a great finisher, or a warm up to help you feel your lats before heavy pulls.
83. Many lifters find it easier to feel their lats working after a set or two of these.
84. Now, let us talk about the most common back training mistakes, so you avoid them from the start.
85. The first is using momentum, swinging your body to move more weight than your back can handle.
86. If your torso rocks back and forth on every rep, the weight is too heavy for your back.
87. The second is cutting the range short, never fully straightening the arms or fully squeezing at the end.
88. Half reps feel stronger, but they leave a big part of the muscle untrained.
89. The third is training back with far fewer sets than chest, because the back is out of sight.
90. Remember from our earlier videos, more weekly sets were linked to more muscle growth.
91. A simple rule is to do at least as many pulling sets as pushing sets every week.
92. The fourth is only ever training width or only thickness, instead of building both together.
93. A complete back plan includes a vertical pull, like pull ups, and a horizontal pull, like rows.
94. Together, they build both the width and the thickness that make a back stand out.
95. Now, a quick word on effort and progress, because back muscles are easy to under train.
96. Many men feel their arms more than their back, so they stop before the back is tired.
97. Slow down, control the lowering, and keep pushing sets until only a rep or two remains.
98. Add a rep or a little weight over time, and write it down so you can see progress.
99. Back changes take a few months to show, so give it time before you judge the results.
100. Consistency matters more than any single perfect workout, so keep showing up week after week.
101. Your shirts will tell you first, as they start to feel tighter across your back.
102. Now let us put all five exercises together into one simple weekly plan you can follow.
103. Here is the plan, and it fits into whatever training split you already use.
104. Train your back on at least two days each week, rather than one long back day.
105. On each back day, include one vertical pull, like pull ups, chin ups, or pulldowns.
106. Add one horizontal pull, like inverted rows, bent over rows, or chest supported rows.
107. Finish with straight arm pulldowns when you have time, to give your lats extra direct work.
108. Choose grips that feel strong and comfortable, instead of chasing the perfect grip width.
109. Do at least as many pulling sets as pushing sets across the whole of your training week.
110. Use full range, control every lowering phase, and avoid swinging your body to move the weight.
111. Write down your reps and weights every session, and add a little more over the weeks.
112. Take a photo of your back today, from behind, and compare it in three months.

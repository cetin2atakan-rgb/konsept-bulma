23. Now, a quick look at what actually makes a grip strong, so the exercises make sense.
24. Most of your grip comes from muscles in your forearm, connected to your fingers by long tendons.
25. When you squeeze, the muscles on the inside of your forearm pull your fingers closed around the object.
26. Your thumb has its own muscles, and it is the key to pinching and locking your grip shut.
27. The muscles on the back of your forearm open your hand and keep your wrist stable.
28. Developed forearms also look powerful, especially when your sleeves are rolled up in daily life.
29. Strong hands also make everyday life easier, from carrying groceries to opening tight jars.
30. Let us go through the five exercises one by one, starting with the simplest one of all.
31. Number one, the dead hang, simply hanging from a bar with straight arms as long as you can.
32. It looks easy, but holding your whole bodyweight forces your fingers and forearms to work hard.
33. A two thousand nineteen study by López Rivera and González Badillo trained twenty six advanced climbers with hangs.
34. One group hung in short, repeated bouts with rests in between, for eight weeks.

38. Grab a pull up bar with a full, overhand grip, hands about shoulder width apart.

46. Number two, the heavy farmer's carry, the classic crushing grip builder we met in our last video.

48. Unlike the dead hang, you can add weight easily, so you can keep making it harder over time.

55. Number three, the plate pinch, the exercise that builds the thumb strength most men completely ignore.
56. You hold two smooth weight plates together between your fingers and thumb, with the smooth sides out.
57. Your thumb has to squeeze against your fingers, because there is no handle to wrap around.
58. That thumb strength helps you lock your grip shut, which is exactly what a firm handshake needs.
59. Stand tall, hold the plates at your side, and squeeze them for as long as you can hold.
60. Start with light plates, because pinch strength is usually much weaker than normal grip strength.
61. Do not hold heavy plates over your feet, and set them down before your grip fails.
62. If you train at home, a thick book or a sturdy wooden block can work the same way.
63. Train both hands, because most men find one hand noticeably weaker than the other.
64. Number four, wrist curls and reverse curls, the two moves that build the visible size of your forearms.
65. Grip strength is not only about the hand, the forearm muscles behind it need direct work too.
66. For a wrist curl, sit with your forearms on your thighs, palms up, holding a light dumbbell.
67. Let your wrist bend down slowly, then curl the weight up using only your wrist and fingers.
68. For a reverse wrist curl, turn your palms down and lift the back of your hand toward you.
69. Reverse curls with a bar, palms facing down, also work the top of your forearm and upper arm.
70. Use light weights and slow, smooth reps, because wrists do not like heavy, jerky loading.
71. These moves are small, but over months they help build forearms that look strong and capable.
72. Number five, pulling heavy without straps, so your grip is trained by the lifts you already do.
73. Many men put on lifting straps for every row and deadlift set, so their hands never work.
74. A two thousand twenty one study by Jukic and colleagues compared deadlifts with and without straps.
75. With straps, grip strength held up better, grip recovered faster, and lifts felt easier.
76. That tells you something important, without straps, your grip is working hard and getting tired.

80. Try your warm up sets and lighter working sets without straps, and save straps for the heaviest ones.
81. Rows, pull ups, and deadlifts will then train your back and your grip at the same time.

83. Your forearms will also get a real workout from every heavy set you hold onto yourself.

9. First, let us be honest about what actually changes in a man's body after forty.
10. A two thousand study by Janssen and colleagues measured muscle with scans in four hundred sixty eight adults.
11. Muscle as a share of body weight started to decline from around the third decade of life.
12. Absolute muscle mass did not drop noticeably until around the end of the fifth decade, around age fifty.
13. And the authors linked that decline mainly to a loss of muscle in the lower body.
14. So the legs and hips, the muscles you use to climb, stand, and carry, tend to go first.
15. That is why the muscles you skip most often can become the ones that let you down later.
16. Muscle power, the ability to produce force quickly, also changes as the years go by.
17. A two thousand twelve review by Reid and Fielding described power declining earlier and more steeply than strength.
18. They described power as an important predictor of how well older adults function in daily life.
19. That matters for looks too, because how you move is a big part of how old you seem.
20. A man who rises quickly, walks briskly, and carries things easily looks younger than his years.
21. The good news is that muscle, strength, and power all still respond to training after forty.
22. The right training can push back against much of this decline, even when you start late.
23. So let us go through the six mistakes one by one, starting with the most damaging one.
24. Mistake one, believing it is too late to build real strength, and quietly giving up on serious training.
25. Many men over forty drop weights altogether, or stick to gentle exercise because they think it is safer.
26. A two thousand eleven review by Peterson and colleagues pooled forty nine studies of adults over fifty.
27. Across over thirteen hundred people, training added about one point one kilograms of lean mass on average.
28. Most of those programs lasted only around twenty weeks, so the gains came in a matter of months.
29. A two thousand ten review by Peterson and colleagues looked at strength across forty seven studies.
30. Strength gains on the leg press and chest press were roughly twenty four to thirty three percent.
31. Those numbers come from people over fifty, many far older than you, so forty is not too late.
32. The Peterson lean mass review also found that the oldest participants gained somewhat less on average.
33. That is not a reason to give up, it is a reason to start now, not later.
34. Every year you train, you build a bigger buffer of strength for the decades ahead.
35. And starting at forty puts you far ahead of starting at sixty, or never starting at all.
36. Mistake two, training too light, because you are worried that heavy weights are dangerous at your age.
37. Light exercise feels safer, but it often fails to give your muscles a strong enough reason to adapt.
38. In the Peterson strength review, higher intensity training was linked to greater strength gains in older adults.
39. The authors concluded that resistance exercise works for older adults, particularly with higher intensity training.
40. Heavy does not mean reckless, it means challenging weights lifted with careful, controlled form.
41. Build up to heavier weights gradually over weeks, so your joints and tendons have time to adapt.
42. Use machines or dumbbells if barbells feel awkward, the muscles do not care which tool you use.
43. Controlled reps with clean form matter far more than the exact number written on the plates.
44. Ask a qualified coach to check your form on the big lifts if you are unsure.
45. And here is a rule that matters even more after forty, for every exercise in this video.
46. If anything causes sharp pain, chest discomfort, or dizziness, stop, and talk to a doctor right away.
47. If you have heart, joint, or blood pressure issues, get cleared by your doctor before heavy training.
48. Mistake three, skipping leg training, even though your legs are the muscles most at risk with age.
49. Remember the Janssen study, the decline in muscle was linked mainly to the lower body.
50. Yet many men over forty train chest and arms, and barely train their legs at all.
51. Weak legs make a man struggle on stairs, shuffle when he walks, and push off chairs.
52. Strong legs do the opposite, they keep your stride long, your balance steady, and your movement easy.
53. Squats, leg presses, lunges, step ups, and hip hinges should be in your training every single week.
54. If deep squats hurt, squat to a box or bench at a comfortable height, and build from there.
55. Step ups onto a sturdy box train balance and leg strength together, which is useful at any age.
56. Hip hinges, like Romanian deadlifts with dumbbells, build the back of your legs and your lower back.
57. Your legs carry you through life, so they deserve at least as much attention as your chest.
58. Even a few weeks of steady leg work can make stairs and long walks feel noticeably easier.
59. Mistake four, never training speed and power, and only ever moving your weights slowly and carefully.
60. Remember the Reid and Fielding review, power tends to decline earlier and more steeply than strength.
61. Power is what lets you catch yourself when you trip, or stand up quickly from the floor.
62. If you only ever lift slowly, you train strength but leave power, your fastest declining quality, untouched.
63. The fix is simple, on some sets, lift the weight with intent, as fast as you can control.
64. Then lower it slowly and smoothly, training speed on the way up and control on the way down.
65. Fast step ups, quick sit to stand from a chair, and medicine ball throws are simple power moves.
66. Keep the weights moderate for power work, because speed matters more than load here.
67. Stop each set while the reps are still fast, because slow, grinding reps no longer train power.
68. Start gently, and only add jumps if your joints are healthy and your strength base is solid.
69. Power training needs little time, a few fast sets at the start of a session are enough.
70. Mistake five, recovering like you are still twenty five, or overcorrecting and resting all the time.
71. Many older athletes believe their recovery slows down with age, and some research supports that concern.
72. A two thousand eight review by Fell and Williams discussed how aging might affect muscle recovery.
73. They suggested older muscle may be more prone to damage, and may repair more slowly after hard training.
74. But they also warned that many studies mixed up aging with simply being less active.
75. A later review by Borges and colleagues argued inactivity may explain more of the decline than age itself.
76. So the honest answer is that recovery may slow a little, but staying active matters most.
77. The fix is to space your hardest sessions with a rest day between them, not to stop training.
78. On rest days, walk, stretch, or do light movement instead of sitting still all day.
79. Sleep well, and pay attention to how your joints and muscles feel before each hard session.
80. If you feel beaten up for days, reduce your sets for a week, then build back up slowly.
81. Smart recovery is a skill, and it gets better the longer you train and pay attention.
82. Mistake six, doing too little total work, like one quick set here and there without a real plan.
83. In the Peterson lean mass review, higher volume programs were linked to greater lean mass gains.
84. Volume simply means the total amount of hard work you do across a week of training.
85. One or two random sets cannot match a steady plan with enough weekly work for each muscle.
86. That does not mean living in the gym, it means a few focused sessions you actually repeat.
87. Two or three full body sessions a week, done consistently, can cover every major muscle well.
88. Count your weekly hard sets for legs, back, chest, and shoulders, and slowly build them up.
89. Remember from our earlier videos, effort and progression matter just as much as the number of sets.
90. Full body sessions also save time, which matters when you have work, family, and everything else.
